#### PAGE 26 — Le Faussaire qui priait
*Threads: COMPLOT, LANGUE, FOI, PARCHEMIN, JEU*
*Voice: PASCAL*

I discovered the conspiracy in the margins of a parchment I should never have read. The monks at Saint-Riquier had forged not merely a relic but a language — fabricating vernacular glosses in a tongue that never existed, a phantom French laid over the Latin of Nithard's codex like a palimpsest of fraud. The forgery was theological: they believed God spoke in the language of the people, and so they invented that language where it was missing, filling the vellum's silences with a fabricated vernacular. Faith drove the fabrication. I understand this. At Port-Royal, we Jansenists translated scripture into French against Rome's prohibition — was that not also a kind of forgery, a conspiracy of belief against authority? The monks' fraud was an act of prayer disguised as scholarship.

The manuscript I held bore ink in three hands: Nithard's original scribe, the forger of the twelfth century, and an annotator who noticed the fraud but said nothing — a silent accomplice. The parchment held all three testimonies. Le Complot des Moines, the Porteur de Maliettes would later call it, but I see it differently. Every forgery is a game with rules as strict as any OuLiPo constraint. The forging monk obeyed the lipogram of plausibility: he could not use words that postdated the ninth century. His clinamen was a single anachronism — the word *grammaire*, two centuries too early — and it betrayed him. The tongue he invented almost passed for real. Grace, perhaps, intervened. Or the game simply demanded its own exposure, as every constraint demands its own violation. I pray for the forger. His faith was genuine. His codex was not.

#### PAGE 27 — Les Vertices du serment
*Threads: LANGUE, NOMBRE, EPEE, MALIETTE, JEU*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, counted the words of the Strasbourg Oaths and found in them a graph. Each word of the vernacular is a vertex; each syntactic bond, an edge. The tongue I recorded — that rough proto-French the soldiers spoke — has forty-two vertices in its oath-graph, connected by fifty-seven edges of grammar and breath. I did not know the word *combinatorics*, but I knew the Oaths were a structure as precise as a sword-formation. Every syllable carried the weight of cavalry behind it. The language was not merely spoken; it was wielded.

Le Porteur de Maliettes, in his Lille workshop, would later reconstruct this graph. Into his largest maliette he packed thirty wooden vertices, painted red for French words and blue for Germanic, connected by wire edges that hummed when struck. A chimere of linguistics and mathematics. ZaZiPo called it Le Nithardosaure — a beast made entirely of language-numbers, its skeleton a combinatorial structure, its flesh the vernacular itself. The game was to rearrange the vertices without breaking the edges, a constraint as demanding as any sextine.

I wielded my sword at Fontenoy with one hand and my stylus with the other. The battle taught me that a tongue is a blade: the oath cuts loyalty into the air, binding armies as surely as iron. The number of soldiers who understood both languages — the French and the Germanic — I estimated at fewer than one hundred. These bilingual vertices were the critical edges of the alliance. Without them, the graph of the oath would have been disconnected, two isolated components shouting into mutual incomprehension. The game of language is the game of war: count your connections or perish.

#### PAGE 28 — La Chronique des aretes
*Threads: CHRONIQUE, LANGUE, NOMBRE, EPEE, JEU*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes reads the chronicle as a mathematician reads a proof. Nithard's *Historiae* — four books, each a testimony, each a record of Carolingian fratricidal war — contains exactly two thousand three hundred and seventeen sentences. He has counted them. Each sentence is a vertex. The edges are references: when Book III mentions a battle from Book I, an edge connects them across the graph. The chronicle's topology is not linear but recursive, a sword biting its own hilt.

The language fascinates him most. Nithard wrote in Latin, but the Oaths he transcribed — those forty-two vernacular words — are the chronicle's living heart. He counts the ratio: Latin to French, ninety-seven to three. The tongue of the people appears only at the moment of the oath, the moment the sword is pledged. Everywhere else, the ecclesiastical language reigns. This asymmetry is the chronicle's combinatorial secret. Remove those forty-two vertices and the graph loses its only connection to the living — every other node is addressed to God or to posterity, never to the soldiers who bled.

He makes it a game. In the Lille workshop, he lays out index cards — one per sentence of the *Historiae* — and asks his students to find the edges. They connect cards with red thread for war, blue for oaths, green for theology. The resulting graph fills the room, a web of chronicle and number hanging from the ceiling like a battle-standard. One student asks: *What are the rules?* The Porteur smiles. The rules are Nithard's — the OuLiPo constraint of witness. Record only what you saw. The clinamen: that no witness sees everything, and the chronicle's silences are its sharpest edges, its invisible swords.

#### PAGE 29 — Les Os et les nombres
*Threads: COMPLOT, NOMBRE, FOI, EPEE, PARCHEMIN*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, know nothing of the conspiracy that would grow around my bones. But I know the numbers of my faith. At Fontenoy I counted the cavalry on the right flank — three hundred and twelve horsemen, each a vertex in the graph of slaughter, each connected by the edge of a shared charge. When the swords met, forty-seven of those vertices were extinguished in the first quarter-hour. I recorded this on parchment still warm from the sun, the vellum balanced on my shield. The numbers were my prayer. To count the dead is an act of faith — it insists that each life was singular, irreducible, not merely part of a mass.

Centuries after my death, monks would dig up my bones and call them relics. The conspiracy — Le Complot des Moines — required fabrication: forged parchment attesting to my sanctity, a codex of invented miracles. I worked no miracles. I swung a sword and wrote a chronicle. But the forgers needed my skeleton as a graph needs its vertices: without the bones, the fraud had no structure. They arranged my remains in a reliquary as I had arranged my casualty numbers on vellum — each bone a node, each connection a manufactured edge of holiness.

The ink on my manuscript was made from oak-gall and iron, the same iron that tipped the cavalry lances at Fontenoy. Parchment and sword share a metallurgy. The scribe's craft and the soldier's are joined by a combinatorial edge I had not recognized until the battle forced it upon me. I counted the dead. I wrote their names. The number of edges between living and dead, at any moment during the charge, shifted with every stroke of the blade. My faith held the graph together. Without belief that the count mattered, the vertices would scatter like bones in an empty crypt.

The Porteur treats the fraud with the reverence of a scholar and the delight of a gamesman. Le Complot des Moines is, he tells his students, the greatest OuLiPo text of the Middle Ages.

The rules of the forgery were strict: imitate the hand of a ninth-century scribe, use vellum scraped to the correct thinness, choose ink whose iron content would pass a casual inspection. The constraint was authenticity. The clinamen — the deliberate swerve that every game requires — was the monks' faith. They believed in Nithard's holiness. Grace, in their theology, preceded evidence.

The Porteur lifts a chimere from another maliette — a hybrid beast carved from boxwood, half-monk half-dragon, its tail curling around a miniature manuscript. Le Nithardosaure, he calls it. ZaZiPo posed the question differently: can a sextine written under constraint be less authentic than a poem written freely? The Porteur does not answer. #### PAGE 31 — Le Pari du copiste
*Threads: PARI, CHRONIQUE, LANGUE, PARCHEMIN, JEU*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes stands before the facsimile of Nithard's chronicle and wagers. The bet is simple: that the parchment tells the truth. Not the historical truth — he knows the manuscript has been copied, re-copied, and copied again, each scribe a player in a game of transmission whose rules no one wrote down. The wager is that the tongue survives. The vernacular words of the Strasbourg Oaths, copied across twelve centuries of vellum, still sound like the language soldiers spoke. The odds are against it. Every copyist introduced risk. Every codex is a gamble against corruption.

He traces the chronicle's genealogy: from Nithard's original manuscript, lost, to the single surviving copy at the Bibliotheque nationale, a testimony in tenth-century hand. Between the two, at least three intermediate copies, each a bet placed by an anonymous scribe that his Latin was good enough, his parchment clean enough, his ink permanent enough to carry the record forward. The probability of faithful transmission through three intermediaries: the Porteur calculates roughly one in four, assuming a five-percent error rate per copy. The language should be riddled with corruption. Yet the Oaths are recognizably proto-French. The tongue endured.

The game of copying is the oldest OuLiPo constraint. The rule: reproduce exactly. The clinamen: every scribe's hand trembles differently. The Porteur makes his own copy — longhand, on handmade paper, in the Lille workshop — and introduces deliberate errors, a lipogram in the letter *d*. He calls it his pari du copiste: a wager that the chronicle can survive even willful mutilation. The vernacular, he discovers, is resilient. Remove one letter and the language bends but does not break. The parchment forgives what the scribe transgresses.

#### PAGE 32 — Le Decompte des morts
*Threads: PARI, CHRONIQUE, NOMBRE, EPEE, MALIETTE*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, wagered my chronicle on the accuracy of my counting. At Fontenoy, between the second and third charges, I numbered the dead on our flank: two hundred and nine cavalry, eighty-three foot soldiers. The edges of my graph — kinship lines connecting the fallen — revealed that seventeen of the dead were cousins, brothers, or fathers-and-sons. The battle cut families in half. I recorded these numbers in my *Historiae* as a gambler records his losses, each digit a testament to the stakes.

The chronicle is a ledger of blood. Every testimony in my four books carries a number: how many swords, how many horses, how many days of march. The combinatorics of a Carolingian campaign are staggering — I count forty-seven distinct units, each a vertex in the military graph, connected by two hundred and twelve edges of command, supply, and rivalry. The odds that such a structure would hold through a single battle: perhaps one in three. At Fontenoy, it did not hold. The graph fragmented at the centre, and my sword filled the gap where numbers failed.

Centuries hence, le Porteur de Maliettes will find these figures in his maliette — transcribed onto index cards, each card bearing one number from my chronicle. He will build a model: wooden vertices for commanders, wire edges for chains of command, a chimere of war and mathematics. The maliette will barely close around it. The bet I placed at Fontenoy — that counting the dead would honour them — is a wager the Porteur inherits. He counts differently: not bodies but data points, not blood but combinatorial structures. The chronicle transforms. The numbers remain.

#### PAGE 33 — La Langue falsifiee
*Threads: CHRONIQUE, COMPLOT, LANGUE, EPEE, JEU*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes discovers the conspiracy in the language itself. Reading Nithard's chronicle for the eleventh time, he notices a word in Book III that does not belong: *chevalerie*, a term from the twelfth century inserted into a ninth-century record. The forgery is linguistic. Some monk, copying the *Historiae*, replaced the Carolingian military vocabulary with his own century's tongue, a sword-word traded for a courtly one. The fabrication is tiny — one vertex altered in the graph of the chronicle's vocabulary — but it shifts the meaning of the entire battle passage.

He brings this to his students as a game. The rule: find the anachronisms. The OuLiPo constraint is temporal — every word must be dateable, and those that postdate the chronicle's composition are clinamens, deliberate or accidental swerves in the transmission. The students find seven suspicious terms. The Porteur maps them: a graph of conspiracy, seven vertices connected by edges of monastic transmission. Le Complot des Moines operated not through grand fabrication but through the quiet erosion of the vernacular, sword-words smoothed into peacetime synonyms.

The testimony of the chronicle depends on the precision of its tongue. If the language has been altered, the record of Fontenoy — the cavalry charges, the blood, the fratricidal swords — becomes unreliable. The Porteur stages a public reading in Lille. He reads the chronicle aloud in reconstructed ninth-century Latin, then again in the monk's corrupted twelfth-century version. The audience hears the difference: the original has edges, the forgery has curves. War-language cuts. The game reveals what scholarship alone cannot: that a conspiracy against a tongue is a conspiracy against the dead who spoke it.

#### PAGE 34 — Le Graphe de la priere
*Threads: LANGUE, NOMBRE, FOI, MALIETTE, JEU*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes builds a graph of prayer. Each vertex: a word used in both the Strasbourg Oaths and the liturgy of the ninth century. He counts nineteen such vertices — *Deus*, *fides*, *salvament*, *christian* — words that belong simultaneously to the tongue of soldiers and the tongue of the faithful. The edges connect them by phonetic proximity: *fides* to *fidelity*, *salvament* to *salvation*. The graph has nineteen vertices and twenty-three edges, and its structure is neither military nor devotional but both — a chimere of language and belief.

He packs the graph into a maliette, each vertex a carved wooden bead, each edge a thread of red silk. In the Lille workshop, he suspends the structure from a frame and spins it slowly. The combinatorics of overlap between faith and language are denser than he expected. The number of paths from *Deus* to *christian* is seven, each passing through different vertices of the vernacular. The game he proposes to ZaZiPo: write a sextine using only the nineteen shared words. The constraint is simultaneously linguistic and theological. The OuLiPo rule mirrors the historical one: the soldiers who swore the Oaths were praying without knowing it.

The Porteur records the result. The sextine, when completed, sounds like a prayer in proto-French — each line ending on a word of faith, each stanza turning through the number-cycle of the form. The clinamen: the seventh path through the graph, which passes through *salvament* twice and creates a repetition the classical sextine forbids. He allows it. The tongue demands its own exceptions. The game of belief has rules that override the game of constraint, and the number of possible prayers in a nineteen-vertex graph exceeds anything his maliette can hold.

#### PAGE 35 — Le Parchemin voyageur
*Threads: CHRONIQUE, EPEE, PARCHEMIN, MALIETTE, JEU*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes tracks the parchment's journey. Nithard's chronicle — the original manuscript, long lost — was copied at least three times before the surviving codex reached the Bibliotheque nationale. Each copy was a station, a scriptorium where a scribe took up the game of transmission. The Porteur maps the route in his maliette: wooden markers for each monastery, red thread for the manuscript's path. The chronicle traveled from the Carolingian court to Saint-Riquier, then to Corbie, then to the royal library. At each station, swords guarded the gates and the parchment passed through.

The testimony of the codex is also the testimony of its material. The vellum was scraped from calfskin, the ink mixed from oak-gall and iron — the same iron that forged cavalry blades. The Porteur holds a sample of reproduction parchment to the light: you can see the animal's veins, the ghost of the living creature that became the page. Nithard wrote on skin that had once felt the sword of the slaughterer. Every manuscript carries the violence of its making.

He turns the journey into an OuLiPo game. The rule: at each station on the map, the copyist must introduce one constraint — a lipogram, a univocalic, a prisoner's constraint where no letter extends below the baseline. The students play. By the third station, the chronicle is barely recognizable, a chimere of Nithard's war-record and formal invention. The Porteur laughs. This, he says, is what actually happened. The game of copying IS the game of constraint, and the clinamen is time itself, erasing and rewriting with each pass of the quill over fresh parchment.

#### PAGE 36 — Le Pari de l'epee
*Threads: PARI, FOI, EPEE, MALIETTE, JEU*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I wagered my life on the sword and won, but the bet was not mine — it was God's. At Fontenoy, I charged with the cavalry knowing that faith alone separated me from the forty-seven who fell in the first quarter-hour. The odds were calculable: three hundred and twelve riders, forty-seven dead, a survival probability of roughly eighty-five in a hundred. But probability is not faith. Faith is the wager that transcends calculation, the bet placed not on numbers but on grace. My sword arm swung, and I believed, and the blade found its mark or found empty air, and I lived.

The game of battle is the oldest game. Its rules are the rules of iron: strike before you are struck, guard the flank, trust the rider beside you. The clinamen is panic — the moment a soldier breaks formation and the line collapses. At Fontenoy, our clinamen came at terce, when Lothair's reserves charged our weakened centre. I prayed. Not aloud — there was no breath for prayer — but in the rhythm of the sword-stroke, which is itself a kind of sextine: cut, parry, thrust, cut, parry, thrust, the six-beat cycle of survival.

Centuries later, le Porteur de Maliettes will pack a miniature sword into his maliette — a chimere of blade and quill, sharp at one end, inked at the other. He will call it the weapon of the chronicler. ZaZiPo will admire the workmanship. The wager I placed at Fontenoy — my life against the faith that God had further use for my testimony — is the same bet the Porteur places each time he opens a maliette and begins the game of invention. The stakes are different. The structure is identical. Every act of creation is a cavalry charge against the void.

#### PAGE 37 — Les Probabilites du sang
*Threads: PARI, NOMBRE, FOI, EPEE, MALIETTE*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes calculates the probability of Nithard's survival. From the chronicle's own numbers — three major battles, each with a cavalry mortality rate between twelve and twenty percent — the odds that a single horseman survived all three are roughly sixty-four percent, assuming independence. But battles are not independent. The graph of Carolingian warfare has edges of accumulating exhaustion: each vertex-battle weakens the rider for the next. The Porteur adjusts: fifty-one percent. A coin-toss. Nithard's faith was a wager on even odds.

He opens a maliette and removes a set of carved dice — not standard cubes but dodecahedra, twelve-faced, each face bearing a number from the chronicle. He rolls them across the workshop table. The sword-numbers come up: forty-seven dead at Fontenoy's first charge, three hundred and twelve cavalry on the right flank, seventeen kinship-edges among the fallen. The combinatorics of the dice reproduce the combinatorics of the battle. Each roll is a bet, each outcome a possible world in which Nithard lived or died. The Porteur rolls one hundred times and records the results.

In fifty-three of the hundred simulations, Nithard survives. The number is remarkably close to the Porteur's adjusted estimate. Faith, in this model, is the residual — the gap between the fifty-three percent that probability predicts and the one hundred percent that Nithard's chronicle requires. For the chronicle to exist, the swordsman had to live. The bet was not optional. God — or chance, or the combinatorial structure of the battlefield's graph — ensured that the edges connecting Nithard to death were never activated. The maliette holds the dice, the numbers, the faith that a fifty-three percent chance was enough.

#### PAGE 38 — Le Pari du temoin
*Threads: PARI, CHRONIQUE, FOI, PARCHEMIN, JEU*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, wager my testimony against all future doubt. The chronicle I write on this parchment is an act of faith — not in God alone, but in the survival of vellum. The codex I entrust to the monks is a bet: that the manuscript will outlast the empire it records. The odds are unfavourable. I have seen parchment rot, seen ink fade to ghost-traces on vellum so degraded the scribe's words float like spirits between the lines. Yet I write. The probability of a chronicle surviving a century is perhaps one in ten. Across twelve centuries, the odds collapse to vanishing.

But my wager, like the one the geometrician Pascal will someday formulate, rests on infinite stakes. If my *Historiae* survives, the record of Fontenoy endures, the testimony of brother against brother is preserved, and all future witnesses will know what happened in June of 841. If it perishes, the event itself is half-erased. The expected value of the bet — probability times consequence — is infinite in one direction. So I write, and I pray, and I commit my chronicle to parchment as a gambler commits his fortune to the table.

The game of testimony is the oldest OuLiPo game. Its rule: tell only what you witnessed. Its clinamen: that no witness is reliable, that memory warps the record, that faith fills the gaps where observation fails. I obey the constraint. I record what I saw — the swords, the blood, the oaths — and I leave blank what I did not see. The parchment holds my silence as faithfully as my speech. Someday a reader will find this codex and wager in turn: to believe or disbelieve the man who was there. My faith bets on his.

I never owned a maliette, but I understand the principle. The Porteur de Maliettes carries language in a suitcase the way I carry theorems in my mind — compressed, portable, ready to unfold at need. I would have enjoyed this game. At Port-Royal, we translated endlessly — the Bible, the Church Fathers, our own polemics. Every translation is a chronicle in miniature: a record of what one tongue can and cannot say in another's terms. The testimony of the Oaths depends on this: that what the soldiers swore in the vernacular could not have been sworn in Latin. The oath's power was linguistic. The sword that backed it merely enforced what the tongue had already cut. The maliette carries the evidence. The game proves the chronicle's point: language was the first weapon.

#### PAGE 43 — Le Complot des chiffres
*Threads: CHRONIQUE, COMPLOT, LANGUE, NOMBRE, EPEE*
*Voice: PASCAL*

I count the conspiracy's arithmetic. The monks at Saint-Riquier fabricated Nithard's relics in the year 1065 — two hundred and twenty-four years after his death at Fontenoy. In that interval, the chronicle was copied at least three times, and each copy offered an opportunity for fraud. The number of words in the *Historiae* is approximately eleven thousand. The number of words the monks needed to alter for their conspiracy: I calculate fewer than thirty. Thirty vertices in a graph of eleven thousand. The forgery was combinatorially elegant, the way a single sword-stroke in a battle of thousands can turn the flank.

The language was their accomplice. By 1065, the vernacular had shifted enough that Nithard's ninth-century Latin was half-foreign to the copyists. The tongue had moved. They could insert anachronisms — sword-words, ecclesiastical terms, theological vocabulary — and few readers would notice. The conspiracy exploited the natural entropy of language: every century, the number of obsolete words increases, and the edges connecting old meaning to new meaning stretch and fray. The monks rode this linguistic drift like cavalry riding the confusion of a broken formation.

Their chronicle — for they produced their own version of the *Historiae*, annotated to support the relic-cult — survives only in fragments. I have counted the fragments: seventeen sentences, totaling three hundred and one words. The ratio of forged text to genuine: roughly one in thirty-seven. The conspiracy was not a rewriting but a marginal annotation, a few edges added to the graph, a few swords resharpened for a battle long over. The fabrication was modest. Its effects were vast. The number of pilgrims who venerated the false relics: uncountable. The tongue of the fraud spoke louder than the tongue of the chronicle.

#### PAGE 44 — Les Lames et la foi

*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, number the languages of faith. At Fontenoy, the priests blessed us in Latin — the tongue of God, they said, though God, I think, has no tongue. The soldiers prayed in their own speech: the French-speakers in proto-French, the Germanic in their rough eastern dialect. I counted three languages of faith on the battlefield and recorded them on parchment: Latin for the liturgy, the vernacular for the soldiers' whispered prayers, and the silence of the dead, which is a language beyond number.

The number of edges in a battle-graph is always larger than the number of vertices, because each death connects to many causes — the sword that struck, the commander who ordered, the faith that justified. I counted the edges at Fontenoy: more than the parchment could hold. My vellum filled, I wrote in the margins. The manuscript became a palimpsest of numbers overlapping numbers.

The graph of Fontenoy has no boundary. Every edge connects to another battle, another oath, another prayer in a tongue I do not know. My codex holds a finite number of words on a finite number of pages. My sword made a finite number of strokes. #### PAGE 45 — Le Pari du faussaire
*Threads: PARI, COMPLOT, LANGUE, EPEE, JEU*
*Voice: PORTEUR*

The Porteur de Maliettes bets against the forger. Le Complot des Moines — the conspiracy to fabricate Nithard's relics — required a wager of its own: the monks bet that no one would check. The odds favoured them. In 1065, who could verify a ninth-century manuscript? The tongue had changed, the witnesses were dead, the swords that fought at Fontenoy had rusted to nothing. The fabrication was a safe bet, a low-risk gamble on collective amnesia.

But every conspiracy carries its own exposure, like every OuLiPo constraint carries its clinamen. The game of forgery obeys rules as strict as any lipogram: do not contradict the surviving evidence, do not use language from the wrong century, do not claim miracles too implausible for the era. The monks broke the third rule. They attributed to Nithard a miracle of the sword — that his blade glowed before battle, a Carolingian Excalibur. The fabrication was too dramatic. The tongue of hagiography betrayed the forger's hand: this was twelfth-century marvel, not ninth-century testimony.

The Porteur stages the exposure as a game in the Lille workshop. He distributes excerpts from genuine and forged sources and asks his students to wager: which is authentic? The odds should be fifty-fifty, but the students learn to read the language's age, to hear the vernacular shift, to detect the sword-vocabulary of one century bleeding into another. By the third round, they identify the forgeries with eighty percent accuracy. The conspiracy fails against trained readers. The Porteur smiles. The bet he placed — that careful attention to the tongue defeats any fabrication — is the oldest wager in scholarship. Le Nithardosaure's bones are genuine. The relics are not.

#### PAGE 46 — Le Jeu du chroniqueur
*Threads: PARI, CHRONIQUE, LANGUE, EPEE, JEU*
*Voice: PASCAL*

I wager that Nithard was the first to play the chronicler's game — the constraint that would later become literature. His *Historiae* obeys a rule so strict it might be an OuLiPo invention: record only what you witnessed with your own senses. No hearsay, no rumour, no secondhand testimony. The tongue that speaks must have seen the sword fall. This constraint transforms the chronicle from history into something rarer: a first-person account of fratricidal war by a man who wielded a blade in the very battles he described.

The language of witness is different from the language of report. I recognize this. When I write of grace, I write from experience — the game of faith played in my own heart, not in a book. When Nithard writes of Fontenoy, his Latin carries the rhythm of cavalry, the tempo of charge and retreat, the vernacular of a man who was there. The odds that such a chronicle would survive: negligible. The bet was on the improbable. Nithard staked his testimony on parchment and trusted time.

The game has rules Pascal would appreciate. Record only what you witnessed — the clinamen being that the witness may lie, the constraint being that the lie must be sustained across four books of chronicle without contradiction. The odds of a consistent fabrication across eleven thousand words: vanishingly small, the Porteur de Maliettes calculates. The tongue trips. The sword-metaphor tangles with the oath-metaphor. Nithard's consistency is itself the evidence. The wager resolves: the chronicle is genuine because the game of sustained witness is harder to play than the game of truth. The language of the real is cheaper than the language of invention.

#### PAGE 47 — Le Complot et le calcul

*Voice: PASCAL*

I calculate the conspiracy's probability. Le Complot des Moines required three conditions: first, that Nithard's bones be discoverable — a probability I estimate at thirty percent, given battlefield burial practices. Second, that a forger with sufficient skill exist at Saint-Riquier — perhaps fifty percent, given the monastery's scriptorium. Third, that no contemporary challenge the fabrication — ninety percent, given the distances and the number of potential witnesses: zero. The product: thirteen and a half percent. The conspiracy was a long-odds bet that paid off for centuries.

The parchment they forged was convincing. I have seen forgeries — at Port-Royal, we dealt in contested manuscripts, each faction producing documents to support its theology. The sword of scholarship is textual criticism. The monks' fraud survived because medieval readers lacked the combinatorial tools to detect it: they could not count the anachronisms, could not graph the vocabulary's chronological distribution, could not calculate the probability that a genuine ninth-century codex would contain twelfth-century words. The numbers expose what the naked eye forgives.

I wager, across the centuries, that the conspiracy will eventually be unmasked by counting. Not by faith, not by prayer, not by the sword of polemics, but by the patient arithmetic of probability. The number of forged words, the number of anachronistic edges in the manuscript's graph, the probability that natural scribal error accounts for the discrepancies — these numbers, properly calculated, will expose the monks as surely as a cavalry charge exposes a weak flank. The parchment remembers. The bet is that mathematics remembers longer.

#### PAGE 48 — Le Pari de la parole

I, Nithard, wager on my own words. The tongue in which I write — this ecclesiastical Latin, stiffened by convention, weighted by the Church's authority — is not the tongue in which I think. The conspiracy against my chronicle will begin here, in the gap between the language I used and the language I meant. The fabrication was linguistic before it was material. The forged parchment was merely the vehicle. The real fraud was the falsified tongue.

I play a game against the future. The rule: write in Latin so precisely that any alteration will be detectable, as a lipogram's missing letter is detectable by its absence. The clinamen I cannot control: that Latin itself will change, that the codex will be copied by scribes who think in a different century's tongue, that the parchment will travel through conspiracies I cannot foresee. My wager is that the structure of my sentences — their rhythm, their vocabulary, their syntax — will survive the game of transmission. The OuLiPo of the chronicler: constrain your language so tightly that forgery becomes impossible. The bet is on discipline. The stakes are truth.

#### PAGE 49 — Le Parchemin et l'epee

I, Nithard, dipped my quill in ink mixed with the same iron that forged our cavalry swords.
