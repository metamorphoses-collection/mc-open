#### PAGE 51 — The Equation of Relics
*Threads: WAGER, CONSPIRACY, NUMBER, PARCHMENT, MALIETTE*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, have counted what remains of me. The monks of Saint-Riquier claimed three bones, a jawbone, and a fragment of parchment bearing my name. The number is precise: five relics for a man who left one manuscript. The conspiracy required arithmetic. To forge a saint you need enough bones to fill a reliquary but not so many that the anatomy becomes implausible. The monks wagered that no one would count. They were nearly right. For eight centuries the equation balanced: five relics, one parchment, zero suspicion.

But the parchment they attributed to me was written in a hand three centuries too late. I know my own hand. I pressed hard into calfskin because I wrote in camp, on uneven ground, with ink mixed from oak gall and iron that the quartermaster cursed me for wasting. The forgers wrote on sheep vellum, in a scriptorium, with steady hands and anachronistic minuscule. The conspiracy was competent but innumerate — they miscounted the centuries as one miscounts rations before a siege.

Le Porteur de Maliettes understood this arithmetic. He opened a maliette in Lille and spread the photographs across a seminar table: here is the parchment they claimed was mine, here is the parchment that is mine, count the differences. His students counted. The number of discrepancies was forty-seven — palaeographic, chemical, linguistic. Forty-seven proofs that the wager of the monks had failed, though it took a professor with battered suitcases to call the bet. The conspiracy had survived not because it was clever but because no one had performed the subtraction: five relics minus one authentic manuscript equals four forgeries. I, Nithard, who recorded the first wager in the vernacular, find it fitting that my bones became the stakes in another. The parchment endures. The relics do not. The number settles the account.

#### PAGE 52 — The Catalogue of Impossible Texts
*Threads: CHRONICLE, TONGUE, FAITH, SWORD, GAME*
*Voice: PASCAL*

There exists a catalogue of texts that should not have survived, and the game of history is played with its entries. Nithard's chronicle belongs to this catalogue. A single manuscript, copied once, nearly destroyed — the probability of survival approaches zero, yet here it is, carrying the Strasbourg Oaths in a tongue that no other document of the ninth century preserved. Faith in Providence is one explanation. I prefer to say that the game has rules we have not yet discovered, and that the chronicle survived because the sword that killed Nithard at Angoumois did not reach his manuscript.

The tongue of the Oaths is itself impossible. No grammarian had codified it, no school had taught it, no church had blessed it. It was the language of men who carried swords, not missals. Yet this unblessed tongue became the first written proof that French existed — or rather, that something which was not Latin and not yet French had enough faith in itself to appear on vellum. The chronicle records the moment a language decided it was real. Every language makes this wager; only the Oaths provide the date.

I have played games with numbers all my life — the arithmetic triangle, the cycloid, the calculus of chances. But the greatest game is the one the chronicle plays with time. Nithard wrote for his contemporaries; his tongue was transparent to them and opaque to us. We read it now as one reads a cypher, reconstructing the grammar by faith and comparison, wielding scholarship like a sword against ambiguity. The chronicle survives in a single copy because the game required only one. One manuscript, one tongue, one soldier's faith that the record mattered. The sword that killed the chronicler could not kill the chronicle. The game is still in play, and every scholar who opens the manuscript adds a move.

#### PAGE 53 — The Professor's Inventory
*Threads: WAGER, CHRONICLE, CONSPIRACY, FAITH, MALIETTE*
*Voice: PROFESSOR*

Le Porteur de Maliettes keeps an inventory. In the first suitcase: photocopies of Nithard's Historiae, every folio of manuscript Latin 9768, annotated in three colours of ink. In the second: the dossier of the conspiracy — carbon-14 reports, the bishop's letter of 1090 authenticating bones that were not Nithard's, the abbot's charter granting indulgences to pilgrims who venerated a skeleton assembled from spare parts. In the third maliette, which is the heaviest: faith.

Not his own faith — le Porteur is an ironist, not a believer — but the faith of others, catalogued and cross-referenced. The faith of the monks who forged the relics. The faith of the pilgrims who kissed them. The faith of the Maurist scholars who, in the seventeenth century, began to doubt. The chronicle of doubt is longer than the chronicle of belief, and le Porteur teaches both. He opens the maliette and says: here is the wager the monks made — that institutional faith would outweigh material evidence. For eight centuries, they won.

The conspiracy was not malicious. Le Porteur insists on this. The monks needed relics because relics brought pilgrims and pilgrims brought revenue and revenue kept the scriptorium alive and the scriptorium preserved manuscripts — including, by a conspiracy of irony, the very chronicle by which Nithard's authentic words survived. The forgers of the bones were also the custodians of the text. The wager cuts both ways. Without the conspiracy, the Historiae might have perished. Le Porteur unpacks this paradox in every workshop: the faith that forged false relics also preserved the true chronicle. He closes the maliette and says: this is why I carry suitcases and not conclusions. The inventory is never complete. The wager remains open.

#### PAGE 54 — The Geometry of Civil War
*Threads: TONGUE, NUMBER, SWORD, PARCHMENT, GAME*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, drew the battle lines of Fontenoy on parchment before the killing began, because geometry precedes slaughter as grammar precedes speech. The number of our forces I estimated at thirty thousand; the number of Lothar's, perhaps the same. The sword does not care about estimates, but the commander must. I mapped the terrain — the slope, the marsh, the wood where cavalry could not manoeuvre — and the game of war became, for one hour, a game of angles and distances. Then the swords met and the geometry dissolved into mud and screaming and a number I could never calculate precisely: forty thousand dead, give or take the uncounted serfs.

The tongue in which I recorded the battle was Latin, because Latin is the language of precision and death deserves precision. But when the soldiers cursed, they cursed in the vernacular — the same tongue that would, seven months later, carry the Oath at Strasbourg. The parchment holds both languages: the Latin of my chronicle and, between the lines, the ghost of the spoken tongue that no scribe thought worth recording. I recorded it. The game of history would later call this an act of philological courage. I call it reporting.

The geometry of civil war is simple: brothers on opposite sides of a field, the sword making the argument that the tongue could not. Lothar and my lords Charles and Louis spoke the same language at table and different languages at Strasbourg, because the game of alliance required a public tongue — a tongue the soldiers could witness and hold their lords to. The number of words in the Oath is small. The number of consequences is not. I set them down on parchment because the sword is eloquent but brief, and the chronicle must outlast the blade.

#### PAGE 55 — What the Odds Demand
*Threads: WAGER, CONSPIRACY, FAITH, SWORD, PARCHMENT*
*Voice: PASCAL*

The odds demand that you choose, and the refusal to choose is itself a choice — this is the sword hidden in my wager. The libertins said: we will not bet. I said: you already have. The parchment on which I wrote the Pensees was covered in corrections, because the argument refined itself as a blade is refined on a whetstone, each pass removing what is unnecessary until only the edge remains. Faith is the edge. The conspiracy of reason against faith — that Enlightenment confidence that the universe is a clockwork requiring no watchmaker — was a wager too, though its partisans refused to name it so.

I think of Nithard's parchment, that soldier's chronicle written in camp with battle still wet on his hands. He wagered that testimony mattered. The sword he carried at Angoumois killed him before he could finish the fourth book of his Historiae. The conspiracy of silence that followed — centuries in which no one copied, no one cited, no one remembered — was not deliberate but was effective. One manuscript survived. The faith I ask of the gambler is no greater than the faith the chronicle demanded of time: that one copy, one parchment, one voice would be enough.

The sword of my argument is this: you cannot escape the wager by claiming insufficient evidence, because the parchment of your life is being written whether you hold the pen or not. The conspiracy of indifference — the pretence that the question of God is merely academic — collapses before the simplest calculation. Infinite stakes, finite life, the odds demanding a position. Nithard understood. He wrote the chronicle not because he was certain it would survive but because faith in the act of writing was the only wager a soldier-scribe could make. The parchment, like the soul, submits to one question: what have you wagered, and on what?

#### PAGE 56 — Counting What Survives
*Threads: CHRONICLE, NUMBER, PARCHMENT, MALIETTE, GAME*
*Voice: PROFESSOR*

Le Porteur de Maliettes plays a game with his students: count what survives from the ninth century. The number is small enough to be shocking. Of the thousands of manuscripts produced in Carolingian scriptoria, fewer than seven thousand remain. Of those, most are liturgical — psalters, sacramentaries, books the monks copied because the Rule required it. The chronicles, the histories, the secular texts survive by accident, by oversight, by the conspiracy of chance that le Porteur refuses to call Providence.

He opens a maliette and produces the number: one. One manuscript of Nithard's Historiae. Paris, Bibliotheque nationale de France, Latin 9768. The parchment is tenth-century — a copy, not the original. The original is lost. The game of transmission reduced Nithard's four books to a single copy made by a scribe who may not have understood what he was copying, because the vernacular passages — the Strasbourg Oaths — were unlike anything else in his experience. The chronicle survived not because it was valued but because it was bound with other texts the monastery wished to keep. The parchment of the Oaths rode into posterity as a stowaway.

Le Porteur counts further. The number of words in the Oaths: one hundred and eleven in the Romance version. The number of scholars who have edited them: more than forty. The number of interpretations: uncountable. He packs the numbers into the maliette alongside the photographs and the transcriptions and says: this is the game — a chronicle reduced to one parchment, an entire language reduced to one hundred and eleven words, and from that reduction, an entire discipline. The game of philology begins where the number approaches zero. Le Porteur closes the suitcase. The count is done. What survives is enough.

#### PAGE 57 — The Soldier's Lipogram
*Threads: WAGER, TONGUE, NUMBER, SWORD, MALIETTE*
*Voice: NITHARD*

I, Nithard, did not know the word lipogram, but I wrote under constraint as surely as any player in le Porteur's workshop. My constraint was the tongue itself. When I transcribed the Oath at Strasbourg, I wagered that the Roman vernacular could be written — but no spelling existed, no orthography, no convention for representing sounds that Latin had abandoned or never possessed. Every letter was a decision. Every word was a negotiation between the tongue I heard and the alphabet I had inherited. The number of choices was infinite; the number of precedents was zero.

The sword teaches constraint. In battle you do not choose from every possible action — you choose from the two or three that the geometry of combat permits. I wrote the Oaths the same way: not from freedom but from the narrow corridor between what the tongue demanded and what the parchment could hold. Le Porteur de Maliettes would call this a lipogram of circumstance — not the voluntary suppression of a letter but the involuntary suppression of an entire orthographic tradition. I wrote without rules because no rules existed, and the absence of rules was the strictest rule of all.

The number of letters in my transcription of the Romance Oath is two hundred and sixty-eight. Le Porteur counted them in a workshop in Lille, opened his maliette, and told his students: here is the first constrained text in French, written by a soldier who did not know he was playing. The wager was that these two hundred and sixty-eight letters, chosen without precedent, would become the foundation. The tongue they represent is no one's mother tongue now. The sword that defended it is rust. But the maliette carries the constraint forward, and in every workshop where a student submits to a rule and writes what the rule permits, the soldier's lipogram continues. I, Nithard, wagered on the tongue. The number holds. The constraint endures.
