-- The Vernacular as Constraint I wager that the tongue conceals a conspiracy. The Strasbourg Oaths, as Nithard reports them in his chronicle, are the first testimony of French vernacular -- but who guarantees their fidelity? The chronicler's account is also a game: he chooses to transcribe nascent French rather than pouring everything into the Latin mold. This linguistic choice is a wager, a considerable stake. The risk is incomprehension; the hoped-for gain, the truth of testimony. The OuLiPian constraint already exists in this Carolingian chronicle. Writing in vernacular in the ninth century is imposing a rule as strict as a lipogram: one renounces Latin as Perec renounces the letter e. The game of the tongue is a conspiracy against the norm. Potential literature is born in this refusal -- this fabrication of a French still without grammar, without dictionary, with no other legitimacy than the spoken word. Chance willed that French make its entrance in a war narrative. The stake is not merely political -- it is linguistic. The conspiracy of tongues plays out on several levels. The copyist monks, in transcribing the Oaths, may have altered the original vernacular. Fraud is always possible -- a false Oath would be a false birth certificate of French. Nithard's wager is to have staked his testimony on a chronicle in two tongues, an account that plays with the clinamen of translation. The probability that these words are exactly those pronounced at Strasbourg is infinitesimal. But the rule of the literary game accepts this uncertainty. The hoped-for gain is not exactitude -- it is verisimilitude.
