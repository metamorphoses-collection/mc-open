-- Faith in the Ink I wager on ink as I wager on God -- without proof that either will hold. The parchment's faith is the vernacular's: believing that words inscribed on vellum will survive the tongue that produced them. The French of the Oaths is no longer our French -- linguistics teaches us this --, but the manuscript still bears the mark of that first belief: one can write in the tongue of the people and be read a thousand years later. The linguistic wager is an act of faith as vertiginous as mine. The stake surpasses grammar -- it is the survival of thought in a mortal tongue. The risk is obsolescence: every vernacular eventually dies, every parchment eventually decomposes. The probability that ink holds is that of grace -- incalculable, hoped for, never guaranteed. The Porteur de Maliettes stores his texts in valises because he believes in the duration of invention. His Lille workshop is a secular Port-Royal -- a place where belief in French translates into chimeras stored in maliettes. The Professor's prayer is the text itself. The vellum of the codex and the inventor's valise share the same faith: that the parchment resists time, that the tongue survives the death of its speakers, that the maliette reaches its destination. The chance of transmission is every copyist's wager. Ink is our only verifiable prayer.
