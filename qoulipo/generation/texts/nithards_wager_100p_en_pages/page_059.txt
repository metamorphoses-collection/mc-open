-- The Maliette of the Oaths The Porteur de Maliettes opens the valise marked "Strasbourg." Inside, reproductions of the Oaths -- the oldest French, traced in a tongue that did not yet know it would be French. Nithard's chronicle preserved this linguistic moment when the vernacular rose like a sword against Latin. The faith of the warrior who transcribes his king's words is not theological -- it is the witness's belief in the power of the spoken word. Louis swears in the tongue of the other, and this crossed oath is a double-edged blade: it binds the armies but it also binds the tongues. The Professor stores his chimeras beside the Oaths. In his Lille workshop, he measures the distance between the ninth-century vernacular and the French of his maliettes. Linguistics traces a thousand-year arc -- from the Carolingian cavalry to contemporary invention. Each word of the Oaths is an act of political faith: believing that the tongue of the people has the same force as that of the clerks. Nithard's sword defended this belief on the battlefield. The Professor defends it in his valises. The chronicle is the thread linking the medieval oath to the modern maliette. The grace of the vernacular is to have survived armies, monks, centuries. The tongue is a sword that never rusts.
