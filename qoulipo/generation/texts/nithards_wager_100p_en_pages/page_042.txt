-- The Oath on the Vellum The Porteur de Maliettes unrolls the parchment of the Oaths. The vellum is a replica -- but the tongue it bears is authentic, that Carolingian vernacular Nithard transcribed in his chronicle as one transcribes an oath of arms. The chronicler's wager was linguistic: fixing on parchment a tongue no one had yet written, a French so young it had no grammar. The Professor reads aloud. The tongue of the Oaths sounds like a sword on a shield -- hard, brief, cutting. The risk of this transcription was immense: what if the copyist misheard? What if the parchment betrayed the vernacular? The probability of faithful transmission is a wager the chronicler made with a soldier's audacity. The hoped-for gain was linguistic truth -- the exact testimony of what the armies pronounced. The codex of the Historiae is the only manuscript bearing this tongue. A single parchment for the first text in French. The Professor measures the stake: the cavalry of Carolingian words charged on a single vellum, the sword of the vernacular struck a single codex. Had this parchment burned, French would have no birth certificate. Nithard's chronicle is a wager won -- the chance of transmission preserved the tongue. The Professor closes the vellum. The ink has held. The sword of words has not rusted. The parchment is the battlefield where French delivered its first charge.
