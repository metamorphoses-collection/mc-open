-- The Oath of the Blade The Strasbourg Oath is a linguistic and warrior wager. I transcribed the tongue of Louis and that of Charles -- the Romance vernacular and the Teutonic -- because political faith demanded it. The sword was not enough; words were needed. The armies facing each other that day awaited not only a victory -- they awaited a tongue, a parchment signed in the idiom of the other. Nascent French was born of a military wager. The stake was the empire; the hoped-for gain, peace; the risk, betrayal. Linguistics will later say that this moment is foundational -- the first written testimony of the vernacular tongue on an official parchment. But for me, that day, it was a weapon. The words in Romance were verbal swords, blades extended toward the adversary to prove the good faith of my liege lord. The vellum of the Oaths is a codex of war as much as of grammar. Faith in the given word is the only faith that holds on a battlefield. Divine silence does not answer oaths -- it is the human tongue that commits. The parchment I covered with ink that day bears the trace of this belief: spoken words are worth more than mute prayers. The wager of the vernacular is a total risk -- writing in a tongue without literary tradition, without fixed grammar, with no legitimacy other than the sword that supports it. The gain is French itself. The manuscript is proof that the blade and the tongue march together. The cavalry of words charged at Strasbourg. Faith in the spoken word held.
