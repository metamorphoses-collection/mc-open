-- The Vellum and the Blade The Porteur de Maliettes holds in one hand a vellum and in the other a replica Carolingian sword. The tongue of the parchment is that of the soldier who prayed before the charge. The vernacular of the Historiae bears traces of steel -- words hard as blades, sentences short as cavalry orders. Nithard's faith is expressed in a warrior linguistics, a nascent French forged by battle. In the Professor's maliettes, chimeras mingle the sacred and the martial. The Lille workshop produces inventions where parchment and sword cohabitate -- imaginary codices where the Latin of prayers lives alongside the vernacular of oaths of arms. The Professor's belief is that of the craftsman: the tongue is worked like vellum, ink is forged like steel. Grace is in the gesture -- the hand of the copyist and the hand of the blacksmith accomplish the same silent prayer. Nithard's manuscript is a linguistic battlefield. French and Latin clash there as armies at Fontenoy. Every word in vernacular is a sword drawn against the domination of Latin -- a blade of tongue that cuts through conventions. The Professor understands that the chronicler's faith was also a faith in the tongue -- the belief that nascent French deserved the parchment, that vellum was not reserved for sacred Latin. His maliettes carry this conviction: the vernacular tongue is a prayer as worthy as Latin, the manuscript in French is a codex as sacred as the Gospel books. The sword forged French. The parchment consecrated it. The Professor's faith transmits it.
