-- The Grammar of Faith Does faith have a grammar? Port-Royal believed so -- the General and Rational Grammar sought in the tongue the structures of divine thought. The number of grammatical categories is a calculation reflecting the architecture of the world: subject, verb, complement are the vertices of a graph whose edges God has traced. The linguistics of Port-Royal is a chronicle of reason -- the account of an attempt to enumerate the forms of the French tongue and submit them to faith. The grammarians' parchment bears tables of conjugations and declensions -- graphs of the vernacular, combinatorics of the verb. The grammatical codex is a manuscript of belief: believing the tongue obeys rules, that French has a hidden architecture, that the number of forms is finite. The vellum carries this rationalist faith -- ink traces edges between words, vertices between sounds. Nithard's chronicle prefigured this quest: by transcribing the Oaths in vernacular, he laid the first vertices of the graph of French. The enumeration of Romance forms in his manuscript is the first act of French linguistics. The grace of the tongue is to be a number that speaks -- a combinatorics that is heard. The parchment of Port-Royal and that of Nithard are the two codices of the same faith: believing that French is a tongue of reason.
