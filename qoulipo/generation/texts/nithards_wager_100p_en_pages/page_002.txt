-- Fontenoy in the Rain I write my chronicle in the June rain, blood still fresh on my hands. Fontenoy is not merely a battle -- it is the moment when the Carolingian tongue split like a blade on a shield. My testimony in the Historiae says what other accounts suppress: Lothar's cavalry charged at morning, and the faith we placed in God did not prevent the swords from biting flesh. War is an inverted prayer, a game whose rule is death. I am the armed chronicler. My vernacular is that of the soldier who has seen armies clash and who searches in Latin for the distance necessary to tell the tale. The tongue of my chronicle hesitates between two worlds -- the nascent French of the Strasbourg Oaths and the Latin of the clerks. This double tongue is a literary constraint I impose on myself without knowing it, a game of registers where each word is a stake. Linguistics will later say that I captured a moment of tipping. I was merely bearing witness. Faith at Fontenoy was that of men who pray before the charge. The armies of Louis and Charles knelt, then drew the sword. The account of this war is a terrible game: how to tell the truth of blood in a tongue that does not yet have words for carnage? My chronicle invents its vocabulary at the same time as French invents its grammar. Each battle is a belief put to the test. Each testimony, a new rule in the game of writing. Grace left me alive so that I might play this game to the end.
