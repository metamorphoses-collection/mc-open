The wager was always arithmetic. I, Nithard, chronicler of the Carolingian fracture, understood this before any mathematician gave it a name: every battle is a calculation in which the terms are blood and territory, and the remainder is always an empire smaller than the one your grandfather built. At Fontenoy, where the sword settled what negotiation could not, I counted the dead -- not from piety but from the chronicler's compulsion to number what the world would rather forget. Three thousand on the western slope. Twice that in the valley where the stream became undrinkable for a fortnight. Numbers that no one asked me to record but that I recorded anyway, because the chronicle that omits the count is a chronicle that lies by silence.

The game of war has rules that the participants rarely articulate. You advance or you hold. You commit the cavalry early or you wait for the infantry to tire the center. Every choice is a wager placed against the fog of terrain and the unknowable intentions of your cousin across the field. Lothar wagered on Pepin's Aquitanians arriving by noon; they arrived at three, and by then the western slope was lost. Charles wagered on my loyalty, and I paid that wager with a sword stroke that still aches in my shoulder when the winter rains come. The game does not ask whether you enjoy playing. The game only asks whether you will move.

I have written four books of history, and in each of them the numbers tell a story that the narrative alone cannot convey. The number of oaths sworn and broken. The number of miles between Aachen and Verdun. The number of copyists who will reproduce my words across centuries without knowing what the words cost. The chronicle is a ledger of events, and the ledger demands accuracy, and accuracy demands counting, and counting is the first game that any civilization learns to play.

The sword does not count. The sword divides.

Le Porteur de Maliettes once spent an entire seminar proving that the number of possible translations of the Strasbourg Oaths exceeded the number of atoms in the visible universe. This was not hyperbole -- it was combinatorics. Each word in the proto-Romance text admits between three and seventeen modern French equivalents, and the choices are not independent: selecting one translation for *pro Deo amur* constrains what you can do with *et pro christian poblo*. The number of consistent translations forms a lattice, and the lattice has a structure, and the structure tells you something about the language that produced the original.

The chronicle of the Oaths is short -- barely a paragraph in my Historiae. But that paragraph spawned a thousand-year conspiracy of interpretation. Every philologist who touches the text adds a layer of commentary that obscures the original a little further, like a palimpsest written in reverse: instead of scraping away old text to make room for new, the scholars pile new text on top of old until the original is buried under its own fame. Le Porteur called this the conspiracy of the glossers. Not a deliberate deception, but a structural one: the very act of explaining a text in a language that did not exist when the text was written creates a distortion that no amount of good faith can eliminate.

He would bring his battered suitcase to class -- the maliette that gave him his name -- and from it extract photocopies of manuscript pages, each annotated in a different hand across different centuries. "Count the hands," he would say. "That is the number of conspiracies between you and Nithard." The students would count: four, seven, eleven distinct hands on a single folio. Each hand a reader, each reader an interpreter, each interpreter a co-author who never asked permission. The tongue that Nithard wrote in has no living speakers. The tongue that explains Nithard has too many.

The parchment on which I write is sheepskin stretched and scraped until it accepts ink without bleeding. The process is violent -- everything in the Carolingian world is violent, from the shearing of the sheep to the sharpening of the quill to the wars that provide the subject matter for the chronicle. I do not sentimentalize this. The parchment is a tool, like the sword, and both are instruments of record: the sword inscribes its decisions on the body, the parchment on the page. The game of history consists in translating one inscription into the other.

I wager that my chronicle will survive the fire that will eventually find this monastery. The odds are poor. Fire is the great enemy of parchment -- worse than water, worse than insects, worse than the monks who scrape old text to reuse the skin for hymns. In the ninth century, every library exists under sentence of combustion. The abbot keeps a bucket of sand beside the scriptorium door, as if sand could hold back the conflagration that razed Lindisfarne, that consumed the library at Bobbio, that will one day reach Saint-Riquier and reduce my careful Latin to a smear of carbon.

And yet I write. The wager is not that the parchment will survive but that the act of writing has value independent of the parchment's fate. The game is played for the playing, not for the trophy. If my Historiae burn, the events they record still happened -- Fontenoy still bled, the Oaths were still spoken, the empire was still partitioned. The chronicle does not create reality; it acknowledges it. And the acknowledgment, even if it perishes, was real while it lasted.

The sword has a similar logic. The blow is real whether or not anyone records it. But I record it anyway, because I am both the swordsman and the scribe, and the game I play requires both instruments.

## Page 4

Le Porteur understood the Strasbourg Oaths as a military document disguised as a linguistic artifact. The tongue in which they were spoken -- proto-Romance on one side, Frankish on the other -- was not chosen for literary reasons. It was chosen because soldiers need to understand what they are swearing. Faith in an oath depends on comprehension, and comprehension depends on language, and language in the ninth century was already a sword drawn between two civilizations that would become France and Germany.

The wager of the vernacular is this: that ordinary speech, the speech of camps and kitchens and bedrooms, can bear the weight of a sacred promise. Latin could do it -- Latin had carried Roman law across the continent. But Latin was no longer anyone's mother tongue, and an oath sworn in a language you learned in school is not the same as an oath sworn in the language you learned at your mother's knee. The soldiers of Charles and Louis needed to hear the promise in their own words, or the promise was just noise.

Le Porteur told us this with the fervor of a man who had spent decades studying the gap between the word and its meaning. In his seminar, faith was not a theological concept -- it was a linguistic one. Do you believe what I am saying? Then you have faith in my language. Do you doubt me? Then you doubt the medium, not the message. The sword of doubt cuts the speaker and the hearer alike, and the wound it leaves is called misunderstanding.

He carried in his maliette a dog-eared copy of the Oaths in every edition published since Pithou in 1588. Each edition was a wager -- a scholar's bet that this arrangement of letters on the page was closer to what Nithard actually heard in that February field at Strasbourg. No edition agreed with any other. The faith required to choose one over the rest was, le Porteur said, indistinguishable from the faith required to believe in God: both demanded a leap past the evidence.

The conspiracy of the number is this: every statistic about a medieval manuscript is a fiction agreed upon by those who do the counting. How many folios in the surviving codex of Nithard's Historiae? The catalogues say ninety-three, but le Porteur counted ninety-five, because he included two fragments that the Bibliotheque nationale had classified as fly-leaves rather than text pages. The number changes depending on who counts and what they count, and the parchment itself offers no opinion.

Le Porteur brought a magnifying glass to every archive visit -- not the kind philologists use for paleography, but a jeweler's loupe, ten-power, the sort used to examine the facets of a cut diamond. He examined the edge of each parchment leaf for evidence of cutting, because a leaf that has been trimmed was once part of a larger sheet, and a larger sheet implies a different mise en page, and a different mise en page implies a different intention. The conspiracy is in the scissors. Someone, at some point in the manuscript's history, made the parchment fit a binding that was not its original home. The number of leaves is an artifact of that trimming, not a property of the text.

The sword analogy pleased him. A manuscript that has been rebound is like a battlefield that has been plowed: the evidence of what happened is still there, beneath the surface, but you need the right instrument to recover it. The magnifying glass was his weapon. With it he could see the ghost impressions of letters on pages that had been scraped and reused -- palimpsest traces that told a story the current text did not. The maliette always held the loupe, wrapped in chamois, alongside the pencils and the index cards and the half-eaten biscuits that were his archive sustenance.

Every number he produced was an argument. Every argument was an act of counting. And every act of counting, he insisted, was a small conspiracy against the innumeracy of the humanities.

Pascal would have recognized the problem: how do you calculate the probability that a medieval text has been altered? The conspiracy of the copyist is not a binary event -- not "altered" or "faithful" -- but a spectrum of interventions, from the unconscious normalization of spelling to the deliberate insertion of passages that support the monastery's territorial claims. Every copy is a wager that the previous copy was reliable, and the chain of wagers stretches back to the lost original like a game of telephone played across centuries.

I know this because I invented the mathematics of expectation. The Chevalier de Mere brought me a problem about dice and I solved it by counting possible futures. The same method applies to manuscripts: at each point where a scribe might have introduced an error, the text branches into possible versions -- the faithful and the corrupted, the verbatim and the paraphrased. The number of possible versions after ten copyings is immense; after twenty, it exceeds comprehension. Faith in the received text is faith in a single path through a forest of alternatives, and the forest is too dense to map.

The tongue in which the text was written matters enormously. Latin texts are easier to check because Latin has been studied continuously; the errors are visible against the grammar. But a text in a language that was not yet standardized -- in the proto-Romance of the Strasbourg Oaths, for example -- offers no stable grammar against which to measure deviation. Every variant could be an error or a dialectal form or a scribal innovation, and the conspiracy of the glossers is to pretend that they can tell the difference.

The wager of the philologist is the wager of faith: I believe this word was written thus because I believe the scribe was competent, and I believe the scribe was competent because I believe the word was written thus. The circle is not vicious. It is the only circle we have.

The monks of Saint-Riquier kept meticulous records. They numbered everything: the books in the library, the barrels of wine in the cellar, the acres under cultivation, the masses said for the souls of the dead. The chronicle of monastic life is a chronicle of counting, and the faith that sustains the counting is the faith that God attends to details. Every grape harvested, every folio copied, every prayer recited enters the great ledger of creation, and the ledger must balance.

But the ledger can be falsified. The conspiracy that le Porteur uncovered was not a conspiracy of violence but a conspiracy of bookkeeping: the monks inflated the number of relics in their possession because relics attracted pilgrims and pilgrims brought money. A femur attributed to Saint Richarius was worth ten ordinary donations. A skull fragment allegedly belonging to Nithard -- Charlemagne's grandson, author of the Historiae -- was worth a hundred. The number was everything. Not the authenticity of the bone, not the faith of the pilgrim, but the number of coins that the bone could extract from the pilgrim's purse.

The parchment trail tells the story with forensic clarity. In 1989, a document surfaced at Saint-Riquier claiming that an excavation had uncovered Nithard's sarcophagus. The inscription was too neat, the dating too convenient, the announcement too perfectly timed to coincide with a fundraising campaign for the abbey's restoration. Le Porteur obtained photographs of the inscription and subjected them to a statistical analysis: the letter spacing, the chisel marks, the proportion of majuscules to minuscules. His numbers showed what his intuition had already told him -- the inscription was modern.

The chronicle of the conspiracy is itself a chronicle. Faith in the historical record demands skepticism about the historical record. The number is both the tool of the conspiracy and the instrument of its exposure. Parchment holds the lie and the truth on the same surface.

Every game of thrones begins with a death. The death of Louis the Pious in 840 was the catalyst for the civil war that consumed my generation. I chronicle it not because I wish to memorialize suffering but because the rules of the game demand that someone keep score. Lothar claimed the title of Emperor. Charles and Louis rejected the claim. The conspiracy was familial -- brothers against brothers, uncles against nephews -- and the sword was the only arbiter who never recused himself.

The wager was existential. I bet on Charles because Charles was my lord and because my mother was Charlemagne's daughter Berthe, which made Charles my cousin and ally by blood. Blood is a currency in the Carolingian game, and I spent mine freely. At Fontenoy I led a cavalry charge that broke Lothar's left flank, and the chronicle I wrote afterward gave the credit to Charles because the game of patronage requires that the patron appear decisive even when the vassal did the work.

The conspiracy of the chronicle is the conspiracy of credit. I wrote the history; Charles made the history; but the division between writer and maker is never clean. By choosing which events to record and which to omit, I shaped the narrative that would define the war for posterity. This is not deception -- it is the game of writing itself. Every historian plays it. The rules are simple: tell the truth, but tell only as much of it as serves the story. The sword cuts indiscriminately; the quill selects.

The game of OuLiPo, centuries later, would formalize what the medieval chronicler knew intuitively: that constraint is not the enemy of expression but its architecture. I constrain my chronicle to the events I witnessed. The constraint makes the chronicle credible. Remove the constraint, and the chronicle becomes a romance -- entertaining, perhaps, but no longer a wager on the truth.

Le Porteur de Maliettes held that every language is a number system in disguise. French distinguishes singular from plural -- a binary operation. Latin distinguishes six cases -- a senary operation. Proto-Romance, as Nithard transcribed it, stands at the hinge between the two systems, and the Strasbourg Oaths are the only surviving snapshot of the transition. The faith we place in that snapshot is the faith we place in a single frame extracted from a film: it shows us something, but it cannot show us the movement.

The wager of the linguist is temporal. You bet that the state of the language at time T can be inferred from texts written at time T. But texts are not speech -- they are speech filtered through the conventions of writing, through the education of the scribe, through the ideology of the institution that houses the scriptorium. The tongue that Nithard wrote is not the tongue that the soldiers spoke. It is a representation, a model, a mathematical approximation of a living system. And every approximation introduces error.

Le Porteur spent years quantifying that error. He built databases of variant spellings in ninth-century manuscripts, computed the standard deviation of each phoneme's orthographic representation, and published a paper arguing that the noise in medieval spelling was not noise at all but signal -- evidence of dialectal variation that the standardizing impulse of later centuries had erased. The numbers told a story that the traditional philologists had missed: the Oaths were not written in early French but in a specific regional dialect, spoken within a fifty-mile radius of Strasbourg, and nowhere else.

His maliette contained index cards for every word in every variant of the Oaths. Three hundred and seventy-two cards for a text of sixty-five words. Each card a small act of faith: that the word had been read correctly, that the variant had been noted accurately, that the classification was consistent. The number of cards was itself a number worth counting.

Le Porteur would say: count the swords in Nithard's chronicle. Not as a metaphor -- literally count them. How many times does *gladius* appear? How many times *ensis*? How many times *spatha*? I am Nithard, and I confirm that I did not choose my words at random. The chronicle demanded precision, and precision in Latin meant selecting from a vocabulary that was simultaneously too rich and too poor -- too many synonyms for common objects, too few terms for the political innovations of my era. The empire that my grandfather built had no Latin word for itself. *Imperium* was Roman; *regnum* was too small; *Francia* was too narrow. I wagered on *respublica*, which the classicists would mock, but which captured something the other words did not: the sense that this thing we were fighting over was not a kingdom but a commonwealth, a shared enterprise that the sword was destroying.

The wager of the word is the smallest and most consequential wager in the chronicler's arsenal. Le Porteur understood this. He would spread his index cards on the seminar table -- each card a word, each word a choice, each choice a tiny bet that this term would carry the meaning he intended -- and say: the number of decisions a writer makes per page is a number worth knowing. He estimated it at three hundred for dense prose, five hundred for poetry, a thousand for legal text. The chronicle, he said, fell between prose and law: about four hundred decisions per page. Four hundred wagers. Four hundred moments when the quill could have gone left instead of right.

The sword makes one decision at a time. The quill makes four hundred. The master in the maliette knew the difference.

The parchment remembers what the tongue forgets. Le Porteur proved this with a simple experiment: he took three modern French translations of the Strasbourg Oaths, stripped them of authorial attribution, and asked his students to identify which was most faithful to the original. The students disagreed violently. Not because they lacked linguistic training, but because faithfulness is not a measurable quantity -- or rather, it is measurable in so many ways that the measurements contradict each other. Faithfulness to the sounds? To the grammar? To the meaning? To the intention? Each criterion yields a different ranking, and the number of possible rankings is factorial in the number of criteria.

Faith in the manuscript is faith in the parchment's memory. The skin holds the ink; the ink holds the letters; the letters hold the sounds; the sounds hold the language that was spoken once and will never be spoken again. The chain of holding is fragile at every link. The parchment can tear. The ink can fade. The letters can be misread. The sounds can be mispronounced. And the language itself, the living system that the text was meant to capture, has evolved so far from its ninth-century form that the capture is now a fossil, and the fossil requires interpretation, and the interpretation requires faith.

Le Porteur's maliette held fossils of another kind: photographs of manuscript pages taken under ultraviolet light, which revealed erasures invisible to the naked eye. The monks had scraped away certain passages and written new ones in their place. The number of such erasures in the Nithard codex was, according to le Porteur's count, seventeen. Seventeen moments of editorial intervention, seventeen acts of censorship or correction or improvement, seventeen points at which the text we read diverges from the text that Nithard wrote. The tongue of the ninth century speaks to us through seventeen filters, and each filter is someone else's faith about what the text should say.

I, Nithard, wrote the Strasbourg Oaths in two tongues -- the Romance and the Germanic -- because the game of the oath required bilingual performance. Charles swore in the tongue of Louis's men; Louis swore in the tongue of Charles's men. The symmetry was deliberate: each king wagered his credibility in the other's language, staking his honor on words he had learned for the occasion. The number of rehearsals required for this performance is not recorded in my chronicle, but I can tell you that Charles practiced for three days, stumbling over the Frankish gutturals like a man walking on ice.

The game of translation is the oldest game in diplomacy. You say one thing; I say the same thing in different words; we both pretend that the two sayings are equivalent. But equivalence in language is a mathematical fiction -- a mapping between sets that preserves no structure except the intention to communicate. The wager of the translator is that intention is enough. The number of ways to betray that intention is infinite.

My chronicle records the Oaths in both tongues, side by side, the earliest instance of parallel text in the French tradition. Scholars have spilled more ink on those sixty-five Romance words than on all four books of my Historiae combined. The game of posterity is cruel: it remembers the appendix and forgets the body. I wagered on political analysis; posterity wagered on linguistics. The arithmetic of faith is paradoxical: the more evidence you demand, the less faith you have, and the less faith you have, the more evidence you need. I know this paradox from the battlefield, where faith in your commander must be total or it is nothing. At Fontenoy, the soldiers who hesitated -- who demanded proof that Charles's strategy would work before committing to the charge -- were the first to die. The sword does not wait for the demonstration. The tongue that gives the order must be obeyed on faith, or the battle is lost before the first blow falls.

My chronicle records this truth without editorializing. The numbers speak: of the twelve hundred men in my cavalry squadron, nine hundred reached the enemy line. Of the nine hundred, six hundred survived the engagement. Of the six hundred, four hundred were still capable of fighting the next day. The arithmetic of attrition is the only arithmetic that the soldier knows, and it is an arithmetic of subtraction. You begin with a number and end with a smaller number, and the difference between them is measured in graves.

The tongue of the chronicle smooths the violence into syntax. "The battle was joined at the third hour" -- a sentence that conceals the screaming, the smell of blood, the sound of iron on bone. The language of record is a language of faith: faith that the sentence can contain the event, faith that the reader will supply what the sentence omits. The number of things omitted from any chronicle exceeds the number of things recorded by a factor that cannot be calculated, because the denominator -- the totality of experience -- is infinite, and infinity divided by any finite number is still infinity.

I write in Latin because Latin is the language of the chronicle. But the soldiers died in Frankish and in Romance. The gap between the language of dying and the language of recording is the gap that faith must bridge.

Le Porteur de Maliettes played a game he called "the Conspiracy of Tongues." In his seminar, he would distribute photocopies of the same manuscript passage transliterated into five different alphabets -- Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Arabic, Hebrew -- and ask the students to determine which transliteration was most faithful to the original. The game was rigged, of course: the original was in a sixth alphabet, Carolingian minuscule, which none of the students could read without training. The conspiracy was le Porteur's own -- a pedagogical deception designed to teach the lesson that every act of reading is an act of translation, and every act of translation is an act of faith.

The tongue betrays the speaker. Le Porteur knew this from Nithard's chronicle, where the Latin occasionally slips into Romance constructions -- evidence that Nithard was thinking in the vernacular and writing in the learned language, and that the two tongues were not separate systems but overlapping fields whose boundary was as blurred as the boundary between faith and superstition.

The game demanded that the students commit to an answer before le Porteur revealed the trick. This was the wager: bet your intellectual credibility on a judgment you cannot verify. The students who refused to play -- who said "I don't know enough to answer" -- received le Porteur's sharpest criticism. The maliette would snap shut with a decisive click, and he would say: "Not knowing is not an excuse for not choosing. Pascal knew this. The wager demands a move." And the students who chose wrong learned more than the students who chose right, because the wrong choice revealed the assumption that had led them astray, and the assumption was always about language -- about which tongue they trusted and which they did not.

Faith in a language is faith in a community. Le Porteur's conspiracy was to remind us that communities are constructed, that tongues are invented, that the game of communication is played on a board whose rules are always changing.

The game of numbers is the game of the chronicle. I, Nithard, learned this from my grandfather's court, where Alcuin of York taught the liberal arts to a generation of Frankish nobles who would have preferred to be hunting. Alcuin's arithmetic was not the arithmetic of merchants -- it was the arithmetic of the cosmos, the Pythagorean faith that the world is structured by number and that to understand number is to understand God. The conspiracy of the schoolroom was to disguise theology as mathematics, to teach the boys that counting was prayer.

I count because I was taught to count. The numbers in my chronicle -- the dates, the distances, the casualties -- are not mere data. They are acts of faith in the countability of the world. The conspiracy of the historian is to present these numbers as objective, as if three thousand dead at Fontenoy were a fact rather than an estimate, as if the distance between Strasbourg and Verdun were a measurement rather than a series of approximations corrected for the meandering of the road. Every number in the chronicle is a little game of pretend: I pretend to know, the reader pretends to believe, and between the two pretenses a shared reality is constructed.

The monks who later conspired to fabricate Nithard's relics understood this game better than most. They knew that a number with provenance -- "this bone was found at a depth of four cubits in the northeast corner of the garden" -- is more persuasive than a number without. The specificity creates the illusion of precision, and the illusion of precision creates faith. Four cubits, northeast corner: the numbers are probably invented, but their specificity makes them sound true. The game of the forger is the game of the chronicler inverted: instead of constraining fiction with fact, the forger constrains fact with fiction.

The OuLiPo would recognize the technique. Every constrained text is a forgery of spontaneity -- a text that pretends to flow naturally while obeying hidden rules. The game is the same; only the stakes differ.

Le Porteur's masterpiece was a play -- *Le Complot des Moines* -- written under a constraint that he never publicly disclosed. The game was this: every line of dialogue contained at least one word borrowed from the Strasbourg Oaths, embedded in modern French like a fossil in limestone. The tongue of the ninth century spoke through the mouths of twentieth-century characters, and the conspiracy of the play was the conspiracy of language itself: the past inhabits the present whether the present knows it or not.

The parchment of the Historiae was, for le Porteur, not a historical artifact but a living text -- a text that continued to produce meaning because the language in which it was written continued to evolve. Every time a French speaker says *salvament* (salvation), they are, without knowing it, quoting Nithard. Every time they say *amur* (love), they are completing a circuit that began at Strasbourg in 842. Le Porteur's maliette held a list of these survivals -- words from the Oaths that had made it into modern French unchanged or nearly so. The list was short: fewer than twenty words. But each word was a bridge, and each bridge was a conspiracy of continuity.

The play was performed once, at the university, by students who did not know about the constraint. They delivered their lines naturally, unaware that each sentence was a game board on which ninth-century and twentieth-century French pieces occupied the same squares. The conspiracy was revealed only in the program notes, distributed after the performance, and the audience's reaction was exactly what le Porteur had hoped for: astonishment that the constraint was invisible, that the game had been played in plain sight without anyone noticing.

The parchment of the play -- the typescript, actually, since le Porteur wrote on a manual Olivetti -- still exists in the maliette, alongside everything else. The game is still being played. The tongue is still conspiring.
