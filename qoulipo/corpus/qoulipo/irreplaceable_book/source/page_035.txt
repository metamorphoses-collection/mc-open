The professor maintained that sacred writings might be distilled to their numerical essence. In his leather case he transported the evidence: word frequencies tabulated across manuscript leaves, the enumeration of devotional expressions per page, the sum of pious declarations set down in iron gall across centuries of scribal labor. His conviction was that quantity reveals what prayer obscures.

The codices of the Jansenists constitute a record of penned devotion. I, Pascal, committed my convictions to the page—every nib's descent a small gesture of belief, every stroke a term in the mathematics of redemption. The professor's portfolio contained the census of my written testimony: how often I set down "grace," how frequently "conviction," how many times the quill met prepared skin in service of theological expression.

The conviction of this arithmetical divine is a conviction of permanence: trust in the enumeration, trust in the accounting, trust that devotional manuscripts bear their essence in the calculations he derives from their gatherings. His briefcase preserved the documentation: commonplace volumes dense with notation, each opening a lesson in the discipline of quantified theology.

The conviction I committed to vellum is a conviction of measurement: every mark subject to counting, every stroke available for tabulation, every spot of lampblack a datum in the record of devotion. The professor's hypothesis was that analyzing my writings would expose the architecture of my belief—that enumerating my words would lay bare the pattern of divine favor.

The case carried by this scholar is a repository of text and calculation. Every manuscript he examined was a treasury of patterns concealed within devotional inscription. His hypothesis—the intellectual wager of a pedagogical career—was that enumeration cannot deceive, that the census of written testimony constitutes the most reliable witness, and that conviction preserved in manuscript may be interpreted through the quantities embedded in its own composition.
