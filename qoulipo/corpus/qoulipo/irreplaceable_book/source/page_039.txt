The history of armed conflict is set down in ink upon prepared skin, and the scribe's art transmutes the violence of battle into the orderly script of the ledger. I have written the record of Fontenoy in the manner of witness-bearing—each stroke of the quill a memorial to the blade, each application of pigment a rendering of iron into text upon the membrane.

The work of the copyist is the work of interpretation: to comprehend the circumstances of engagement, then to set them upon the leaf in the hand of the lettered clerk. The prepared hide receives all manner of expression equally—the account of the man-at-arms and the dictation of the cloistered brother, the language of the palace and the rough speech of the encampment. The writing fluid shows no preference; the scribal hand preserves all testimony.

The weapon speaks in the register of forged metal. The nib responds in the medium of stain. Between the instrument of war and the written word lies the work of interpretation—the relation between the logic of armed strife and the logic of the historical account. The sheet is the ground where these two registers converge, and the historical narrative is the reckoning of their meeting.

The expression of those who fight is not the expression of those who write. The historical record must span the distance between the utterance of the combatant and the notation of the clerk, between the vocabulary of sharpened edges and the penmanship of lampblack. The office of the manuscript is the office of this spanning: the membrane receives the raw matter of occurrence and renders it into the form of the archive.

The language of sworn obligation, the movement of the honed implement, the trace of the writing instrument, the surface of the treated skin, the office of historical preservation: these meet in the record of armed conflict, where the weapon is rendered in ink and the vellum bears the testimony of those who fought in the hand of those who inscribe.
