The gamble of the blade rests on this: that your years shall outlast your opponent's. The gamble of the codex rests on another faith: that the inscription shall outlast the scribe who formed it. Between these two ventures lies the mirror wherein civilization beholds itself—that polished surface in which the vocabulary of warfare glimpses its twin in the vocabulary of preservation.

I, Pascal, never wielded steel, yet I grasped what stood at hazard. The Chevalier de Méré, who schooled me in the mathematics of chance, had borne arms in the civil strife that sundered France whilst I assembled my calculating engines. He spoke of combat and dice as kindred ventures, differing only in their instruments: in both, one risks fortune upon the mirror of likelihood. The duellist's tongue speaks through tempered metal; the gamester's through numerical odds; yet both say the selfsame thing: I trust my prospects exceed yours.

Vellum is the mirror wherein the blade beholds itself. What steel inscribes in vital fluid, the scroll preserves in letters. History's great contest is one of translation—the tongue of happening rendered into the tongue of documentation—and the venture is that this rendering remains true. Every reflection warps; every page simplifies; every act of copying surrenders something in transit.

The tongue of Old French, safeguarded on animal skin by monastic hands, mirrors an epoch of steel. The words Pro Deo amur were uttered by men who gripped swords in one fist whilst laying the other upon sacred leaves. The oath's contest is the gamble's contest: one speaks in the common soldier's vernacular because one wagers that his devotion, sworn upon his weapon, binds more firmly than the polished Latin of the scriptorium.

The mirror of vellum preserves every wager. Steel's contest fades when the final warrior perishes. Yet the codex remembers the oath's diction, and the oath reflects the blade, and the blade reflects the gamble. This interplay of reflection and vernacular and animal skin and tempered metal is the tournament without end.

The stratagem the scholar uncovered was no conspiracy of persons but rather a stratagem of leaves. The monks conspired not in cloisters nor forged materials in darkness. Their stratagem was linguistic: each occasion a copyist reproduced a folio, his vernacular infected the membrane with the vocabulary of his own generation. Transmission's contest is involuntary fabrication's contest, enacted in oak-gall upon prepared skin across generations that never encounter one another.

The lecturer preserved his evidence in a leather satchel. He would arrange the photographic reproductions across the seminar table and indicate the anachronisms: locutions that could not have been inscribed in the ninth century because the dialect that produced them had not yet come into being. This is the stratagem, the scholar would announce during his sessions. Not forgery but syntax. Not a conspiracy of malevolence but a counterfeiting of vernacular. Vellum is a surface whereon every scribe leaves his trace in pigment, and the accumulated marks of centuries yield a text its original author would fail to recognize.

The tongue of Old French, as safeguarded on the leaves bearing the Strasbourg Oaths, represents a stratagem of well-intentioned scribes. Each dipped his quill in prepared fluid and inscribed what his own vernacular perceived, not what Nithard's pen had set down. Philology's contest is to reverse this fabrication—to strip away the strata of writing until the original tongue emerges from beneath the accumulated evidence carried in satchel-loads of gatherings and calligraphy.

The scholar pursued this contest with a cryptographer's patience and an archivist's burden. He instructed his pupils that vellum's stratagem and linguistic drift constitute not scandal but phenomenon—a natural consequence of textual transmission's contest, enacted in oak-gall across centuries. Vernacular shifts; the codex preserves the shift; time's plot inscribes itself in strokes no copyist intended.

His satchel held the pedagogy of deception: every manuscript a lesson, every trace of pigment a chapter in the curriculum of fabrication and vernacular transformation.
