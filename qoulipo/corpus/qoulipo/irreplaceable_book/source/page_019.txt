The idiom of the sworn pledge and the idiom of the final accounting share this commonality: both require trust, and both are set down upon vellum by men who grasp the weapon in one fist and the stake in the other. At Strasbourg my kinsmen risked all upon a performative declaration—an utterance in the common speech that would bind their soldiery more firmly than any formula couched in the Church's Latin.

The folios of the pledge are determinate: a fixed inventory of clauses, a specific enumeration of syllables, an exact tally of locutions pronounced in the soldiers' dialect. The trust underlying these gatherings is the trust of the fighting man: conviction that the spoken word binds, conviction that the manuscript preserves, conviction that the weapon enforces what the hazard promises. Every stroke in the pledge has a corresponding entry on the field of battle—the count of those who swore, the count of those who honoured their word, the count of those who perished with the grammar of their devotion still upon their tongues.

The membrane of the pledge is a quire of potentiality. The hazard is that the vernacular, uttered by ordinary soldiers who comprehend the diction of their native speech, will endure against the exigencies of combat. The weapon tests the pledge; the spoken idiom delivers it; the prepared skin records it; the trust sustains it; the accounting verifies it. Each inscription in the register of the pledge is a modest act of devotion—a conviction that pronunciation signifies, that syntax bears force, that the enumeration of syllables is not capricious.

The idiom I employ is the idiom of attestation. The hazard I undertake is the hazard of the copyist. The weapon I bear is the weapon of the witness. The membrane I employ is the calfskin of the pledge-keeper. The strokes I enumerate are the strokes of piety. And the trust—the trust is that all of these—spoken word, hazard, weapon, codex, inscription—converge in the solitary utterance that establishes a tongue and seals an invocation.
