The librarian unfastened the morocco case and laid the chronicle upon the reading table. Against the oak he positioned leaves from the register alongside the questioned passage, and their juxtaposition disclosed what flourishes had obscured. The iron gall of the founding testament diverged from that of the transcribed folio. Some hand had wagered that subsequent readers would overlook the replacement.

The interpolation, as the cataloguer reconstructed from the surviving gatherings, was paleographical in nature: the brothers who rendered the account in a later age adapted the letterforms to mirror their own scriptorium. Their deception lacked malice—the corruption arose not from deliberate falsification but from confusion of scribal convention. Each copyist's hand bore the mannerisms of his own period, and the manuscript accrued strata until the founding text lay buried beneath generations of penwork.

The morocco case held decades of collation. He had examined every witness, traced every variant, mapped the evolution of letterforms from quire to quire. The interpolation he identified was a sequence of scribal custom: each leaf exhibited the conventions of its copyist's workshop, and codicological methods exposed the corruption beneath the codex's surface.

The premise of textual criticism, the cataloguer argued in his monograph, holds that one might strip away accretions and recover the founding text beneath. He staked his standing on this endeavour. The portfolio swelled with each new variant, each new instance of scribal intervention recorded in the abbey's accounts.

The Old French preserved in these registers is a testament frozen in letterforms. Some voice uttered those words; some hand inscribed them; some scribe rendered them anew. At each remove the chain of transmission altered the penwork slightly, and well-meaning brothers rendered the corruption invisible. The portfolio served as his instrument against this interpolation—not steel but a collection of leaves, each an echo of an echo of an echo.
