The scholar bore the manuscript of the Carolingian campaigns in his leather case, and the tongue he employed to expound it was that of reverence tempered by doubt. In his courses he would recite passages from the annals of Fontenoy, rendering the Latin into the vernacular of the lecture room, then pause to inquire: what compact does this record propose? What credence does the annalist require of us? And what obliteration menaces the witness if these entries prove false?

The case contained the documentation of ages. The scholar's confidence in the manuscript rested upon measured calculation—he relied upon the annals sufficiently to present them, yet held that every witness harboured some distortion introduced by scribal prejudice. The vocabulary of the eighth century stands remote from that of the twenty-first, and the instructor demonstrated to his pupils that meticulous scrutiny must penetrate the idiom of each epoch to recover the original phrasing.

The manuscript preserving the oaths constitutes a register of utterances made beneath the blade's menace. My kinsmen trusted that shared parlance would unite their forces, and the scholar's acceptance of that trust formed the basis of his syllabus. He maintained that the diction of the oath-maker and that of the recorder are distinct registers—one employs the rhetoric of allegiance, the other the formulae of documentation—and the case holds both.

Each session in the course represented a small compact: that the record of armed conflict might be translated into the parlance of contemporary learning without forfeiting the conviction that inspired the source. The scholar's case held the apparatus of that undertaking—transcribed leaves, commentaries, the chalk traces of decades of academic dedication. The pen of instruction serves as the tool of exactitude, and the teacher's vocabulary becomes the register of scrupulous witness.
