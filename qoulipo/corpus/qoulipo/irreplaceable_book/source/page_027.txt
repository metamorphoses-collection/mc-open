The scholar ventured that the manuscript's significance might be distilled to its calculations. In his lectures he maintained that every codex bears a hidden reckoning—an inventory of lexical frequencies, a summation of dialectal markers, a sequence of syntactical repetitions—and that this numerical transcription proves more reliable than the written testimony itself. The portfolio contained his proofs: decades of inventories, decades of conviction in numerical analysis.

The tongue of devotion and the tongue of counting share a teaching. The scholar expounded both in his chamber, moving between the vocabulary of literary examination and the discourse of statistical philology without hesitation. His conviction held that enumeration discloses what speech obscures—that the record of calculations constitutes the record of veracity, and that the venture of learning always stakes itself upon the figure rather than the utterance.

The portfolio preserved the numbered commonplace books of a career. Each inventory represented a small act of conviction—a trust that calculation would prove significant, that the sum of syllables in the Strasbourg Oaths would unveil something the scribal hand did not openly inscribe. The scholar's venture proposed that the tongue of Old French, examined through enumeration, would yield a testament different from what the copyists intended to transmit.

Every lecture in the seminar constituted a venture upon pattern. The scholar would compute the frequency of a particular expression, inventory its occurrence across the folios, and proclaim the result in the dialect of statistics. His trust in the count remained unshakeable. The speech might deceive, the testament might distort, but the figure—the pure, impartial figure—reveals truth. The portfolio serves as the reliquary of that conviction: a case of computations borne from lecture to lecture, from term to term, a stake that the teaching of calculations will outlast the teaching of expressions.
