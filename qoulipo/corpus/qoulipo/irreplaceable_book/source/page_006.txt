After the battle, scribes tallied the fallen upon scrolls. With stained fingers they performed the arithmetic of slaughter, transforming the blade's harvest into columns of numerals. Their devotion to the count matched that which they brought to copying psalters—every numeral a prayer, every reckoning a sacred calculation. The work of battlefield accounting demands the same reverence as the liturgical offices.

The blade determines what calculation cannot. Yet once combat ceases, the accounting begins: how many mounted charges, how many steel encounters, how many names of the slain to inscribe upon vellum. I wielded the blade at dawn and the quill at vespers, and I assert the nib demands greater conviction than the weapon, for steel yields its results immediately whilst the manuscript must endure centuries of care and abandonment.

The numerals at Fontenoy were estimates. The practice of tallying corpses is a method of approximation—you traverse the field, you enumerate what appears, you commit the numeral to ink upon the leaf. The vellum preserves every notation; the craft of the bound volume is the craft of endurance. My trust in these numerals is trust in the stroke of the pen—trust that the inscription will survive the next conflagration, the next investment, the next clash of armies.

The calculation of warfare is straightforward: the blade produces the event; the numeral memorializes it; the nib preserves it; the vellum bears it; devotion sustains the labor. Every calculation I performed upon vellum was a small act of reverence—trust that the tally would discover a reader, that the method of my accounting would prove valuable to some future scholar of combat and enumeration.

The scribes pursued their arithmetic with the same conviction they brought to worship. For them, enumeration was indistinguishable from sacred recitation. The ink was identical; the vellum was identical; only the blade was absent from their reckoning.
