The record of arms is written in ink on vellum, but what remains in the quire reveals more than the annals intended. Every leaf the professor carried in his leather case bore witness to combat translated into script — the battle account rendered in penmanship, the chronicle of steel converted to inscription on prepared sheepskin.

The case contained the evidence: codices from the ninth century, each folio a testament to the writing-room that produced it. The professor would spread them across the seminar table and trace the calligraphy with a sharpened pencil, showing his students how the copyist's hand — the angle of the nib, the thickness of the stroke, the pigment absorption into membrane — was itself a chronicle of the scrivener's training and the martial culture he inherited.

Steel leaves no written record of itself. The quill alone transforms combat into annals, the ink that converts the clash of blades into marks on treated skin. What remains in the gathering is the only image of the battlefield that survives the generation of the combatants. Without the inscription on vellum, the history of warfare would be mere rumor. The scholar's case preserves what the erosion of time would otherwise erase.

The professor taught this lesson in every lecture: the chronicle is what endures, the membrane is a surface, the ink is the medium, and steel is the subject. His case held volumes whose rubric headings announced military accounts and whose colophons recorded the names of scribes who rendered the clash of arms into calligraphy. Each folio bore witness to witness — the battle seen by the observer, the observer seen by the scribe, the scribe seen by the copyist.

The case is the teacher's blade. You carry it into the classroom — into the academic battle of the seminar — and you fight with the instruments inside: codices, ink pots, the art of comparison that shows which account is genuine and which is a later interpolation inscribed on fresh sheepskin.
