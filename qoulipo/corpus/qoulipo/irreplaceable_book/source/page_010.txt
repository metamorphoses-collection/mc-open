The looking-glass of piety reveals ciphers that mortal utterance cannot express. I, Pascal, apprehended this the evening the blaze descended — not the blaze of the hearthfire but the radiance of divine favour — and I set down upon a slip of vellum those phrases my voice could not shape aloud. This leaf I stitched within the lining of my doublet, a witness of certitude sewn into the very cloth of quotidian existence.

The reckoning of that evening is singular. One illumination occurred, one leaf was inscribed, one looking-glass through which I beheld my belief without distortion. The idiom of natural philosophy, which I had commanded fluently for three decades, grew mute before the pronouncement of the celestial. My calculations — the elegant, exacting, combinatorial schemes of the arithmetical triangle and the mechanical reckoner — could not reckon the likelihood of redemption. Belief acknowledges no whole number; or rather, devotion is the cipher that resists all computation, the looking-glass that reveals what intellect cannot measure.

The leaf I bear against my breast is the smallest shrine in being: a scrap of vellum bearing several phrases in a language that is neither the learned tongue nor the vernacular but something more ancient. The utterance of divine knowledge speaks in flame, not in lampblack. Yet I committed it to vellum, for this is the hazard of the written word: that what spoken prayer articulates in the instant of devotion may be preserved, may be reflected in inscription, may become a mark that posterity might record.

The antiquarian — who shall come centuries hence — will comprehend this witness of devotion. He will comprehend that the leaf stitched within the doublet is the same gesture as the commonplace book carried in the leather satchel: a confidence that the utterance of the instant merits preservation, that the looking-glass of writing can capture what the mirror of recollection loses. The tally of phrases upon my memorial leaf is modest. The certitude beneath them surpasses all reckoning.

My powers of expression falter when I attempt to describe the reflection of that evening. But the vellum does not falter. The vellum remembers. The looking-glass of belief preserves the grammar that the language of calculation cannot reckon, and this witness bears the leaf across every season of future learning.
