The professor's valise contained documentary materials. Not the demonstrations of pure mathematics, but the apparatus of a paleographer: concordances, variant registers, the enumeration of instances where one codex departed from its companions. He pursued the methodology of textual scholarship as systematic tabulation, convinced that rigorous application would illuminate the genealogy of the manuscript transmission.

The Strasbourg Oaths survive in a unique exemplar—no witnesses, no parallel recensions. The paleographer's craft proves especially austere when confronted by a singleton: one cannot collate variants, tally divergences, or adjudicate among readings where no alternatives exist. The professor's documentation of this particular volume constituted a study in isolation.

Yet he pursued his methodology nonetheless during his lectures. He compiled word-lists, measured interlinear spacing, tallied ruling-lines per membrane. He treated the codex as an apparatus for generating data, each codicological feature a measurement his valise might preserve for examination. His conviction held that even an unaccompanied witness contained sufficient bibliographical evidence to reconstruct its provenance—that the quire itself constituted a record inscribed not through the copyist's pen but through the preparation of the animal skin, the pressure of the stylus, the composition of the ink.

The paleographer's discipline demands inexhaustible perseverance. The professor believed the vellum would divulge its secrets under proper interrogation, and that such interrogation necessarily involved quantification. How many characters occupied each ruled line? What proportion of letter-forms extended above or below the baseline? His compilation of these measurements, accumulated across decades of teaching and transported in his valise from lecture-theatre to repository, embodied his scholarly testament: that codicological analysis operated according to discoverable principles.

Each tabulation in his valise constituted its own documentary artifact—a register of dimensions, a record of enumeration, evidence that patient bibliographical accumulation would vindicate the years devoted to systematic scholarly investigation.
