The stitching of the vow finds its counterpart in the blade now pressed between gatherings. At Strasbourg my kinsmen enacted their rite of fealty in the mother-tongue of their retainers—each declaration a testament of belief uttered beneath the gleam of tempered metal. The quires preserve what transpired: the language of the covenant mirrored in the encounter, the register of devotion answering the register of trial by arms.

A scribe's labour is to fix the living speech within pigment and gall. I undertook this task at Fontenoy, where steel spoke with greater eloquence than any dialect and the soldier's conviction inscribed itself through endurance alone. What remains on vellum is ever a diminished copy—the membrane cannot hold the cadence of a man's final breath, the last words a dying warrior offers to heaven.

The leaves reveal how armed struggle becomes a contest of languages: each host voicing its particular inflection, each weapon underwriting its own syntax. A Carolingian warrior's trust resides in the spoken word: confidence in the phrase, in the formulation, in the belief that utterance bears the same weight as drawn steel.

This account is a manuscript performing the office of remembrance. The original tongue is reflected, inverted, refracted by the chronicler's hand. The implement of violence becomes the mark upon the page; the combatant's conviction becomes the text's sanctity; the field of battle becomes the desk where copyists contend.

The binding of the vow carries devotion's language across generations. The written record sustains this custody; the ritual upholds it; the blade confirms it; the mother-tongue transmits it. And the conviction—that trust in the vernacular sworn under shadow of iron—resounds through every folio preserving the ceremony of the oath.
