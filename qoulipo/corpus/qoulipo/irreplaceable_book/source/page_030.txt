The curator's leather case contained a lens for each leaf. He expounded in the manuscript room that calligraphy reveals the fervour of its epoch—that every binding, every page, every roll presents overlapping planes wherein sacred text answers scholarly annotation.

Ninth-century script, as it survives on prepared skin, served as a lens the curator examined in his study. The original idiom embodies a religious sensibility now vanished—a manner of reverence so alien to contemporary understanding that interpretation demands specialized knowledge, an educated terminology acquired through years of apprenticeship and carried from one consultation to the next.

The work of the lens resembles the work of reading twice over: first attending to the copyist's execution, then attending to the philologist's interpretation. The curator maintained that each page presents two aspects—the lettering apparent to sight and the significance apparent only to disciplined attention. His case held the instruments of such discipline: grammatical exercises, guides to pronunciation, paleographic terminology assembled on slips of paper after decades of scholarship.

The manuscript lens captures the presence of every reader who has turned its leaves. Each era brings its own vocabulary to the codex, and the scholar's task involves distinguishing these accumulated presences—recovering the initial voice beneath centuries of response. The curator's commitment to this task remained unwavering: honour the prepared skin, honour the surface, honour the lens of learned scrutiny.

The case serves as the portable lens of scholarly apparatus. The prepared skin provides the surface of religious commitment. Calligraphy supplies the instrument of interpretation. And that which the scholar perceives when examining the volume through the lens of study—the dual image, the correspondence that joins the page of former times to the manuscript room of the present—is the symmetry that binds past to present.
