The tongue of conspiracy speaks in ciphers concealed within these folios. I have discovered that the annals of the Carolingian era carry a hidden reckoning—a system of encoded marks that the scribes inscribed in the very grammar of their testimony. The count of words per sentence, the tally of syllables per phrase, the sum of particular idioms per leaf: these figures reveal the scheme that the tongue of the account deliberately conceals.

Conspiracy within vellum becomes a matter of linguistic arithmetic. Every fabrication leaves a numerical trace in the iron gall: the dialect shifts, the diction changes, and the reckoning of these changes reveals the plot. The tongue of the forger betrays him because the rhythm of natural speech differs from the rhythm of deliberate composition, and the tally of the differences is the mark the cataloguer reads in marginalia.

Philological detection proceeds through tallies and idioms. The professor counted the occurrence of particular phrases—the vernacular equivalent of a forger's signature—and the sum of these occurrences became the chronicle of conspiracy itself. The quire of the genuine and the quire of the counterfeit leave different numerical patterns, and the reckoning of these patterns is the labor the bibliographer performs.

The tongue of Old French carries its numbers within its grammar. Every dialect has its own count of preferred constructions, its own tally of typical utterances, its own reckoning of syntactic frequencies. The chronicle records these figures without intending to, and the work of detection reads them as evidence of conspiracy. The annals are a ledger of involuntary testimony, each phrase an integer in the equation of authenticity.

The inscription tells two stories: the story the chronicler intended, told in the tongue of his vernacular, and the story the ciphers reveal, told in the reckoning of frequency and pattern. The contest between these two chronicles is the conspiracy made visible by arithmetic.
