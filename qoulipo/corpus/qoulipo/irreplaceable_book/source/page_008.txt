The tongue of a dying warrior constitutes the most candid manuscript in all Christendom. Upon the field I heard combatants cry aloud in mother speech—locutions beyond my comprehension, expressions raw with mortal dread—and each utterance proved a testament of allegiance more veridical than any annals ever committed to vellum. Battle reduces language to its essential elements: when steel pierces the vitals, one does not employ the polished periods of courtly discourse. One speaks in the grammar one's nurse first whispered.

My conviction regarding the preservation of testimony faced its severest trial at Fontenoy. The scrivener's office demands the recording of events with exactitude, yet how might one account dispassionately for kinsman's treachery? Lothar prosecuted his claim through force of arms, and the instrument of negotiation foundered where the weapon prevailed. I maintained my record because the antiquarian—though he dwells yet in futurity—shall require this testimony to demonstrate that the struggle between verity and counterfeit is as ancient as the Carolingian blade.

The pedagogue maintained a repository of documents spanning the centuries. He instructed, within his lecture-hall, that every historical record is composed in an alien tongue. The language of the eighth century remains foreign to that of the twenty-first, and the trust required to traverse that interval constitutes the trust of scholarly discipline: bear your sources, credit nothing unsubstantiated, and recall that the edge of learned scrutiny surpasses the sharpness of any martial steel.

The compact I witnessed at Strasbourg was sworn in the common speech of the soldiery—a calculated risk that loyalty voiced in the vernacular would bind more securely than liturgy intoned in Latin. The blade hung suspended above every signatory. The stakes were mortal. And I, the annalist, stood with my writing-materials and set down the declaration in the very dialect wherein it was pronounced, for such was my office: that the historical record must preserve the original tongue, not render it into another.

Steel, common speech, scholarly discipline, limitation: at Strasbourg, these four elements plaited themselves into a singular moment that the researcher's folio would transmit across the ages of instruction and learning.
