The volume arrives in my custody wrapped in linen, its boards warped from centuries of damp storage in some provincial archive. When I unwrap it, the smell rises—leather and mildew and something faintly metallic, like old ink corroding through vellum. The stitching along the spine has failed in three places. I open to the first gathering and find the quires misbound, their signatures running in wrong sequence: gathering D follows A, then comes F, while B and C seem lost entirely.

I begin my examination at the colophon, which declares the work completed on the Feast of Saint Giles in the year of our Lord 1482, but offers no scribe's name, no location, no patron. The catchwords at each page-bottom should guide me toward reconstruction—those small words meant to link one folio to the next, ensuring the binder's accuracy—but here they lead nowhere. "Summon" cries the recto of folio twelve, yet the facing page begins mid-sentence with "the harvest moon." I turn forward and back, searching for the missing thread. Folio eighteen proclaims "therefore" at its foot, but nineteen opens with an illuminated capital depicting a stag, starting some new tale entirely.

The vellum itself presents anomalies. Three folios are palimpsest, scraped clean of earlier text, though under raking light I discern ghostly majuscules beneath the current script. Two folios are paper, continental stock by the watermark, bound among the animal skins as if no one noticed the substitution. The binding—or rebinding, for this is surely not original—employs wooden boards covered in blind-tooled calf, the tools themselves suggesting Flemish work, though the text is clearly English.

What chronicle could demand such compromised preservation? What narrative survives in this disordered state? I lift folio seven and hold it to the window. The membrane glows amber. Through it I see my own hand, ink-stained, trembling slightly. I see the world beyond: the reading room, the other volumes waiting in their measured rows, each holding its portion of remembrance intact. But this book permits no such certainty. Its pages insist that incompleteness is also a form of witness. I set it on the lectern and begin to read.
