The librarian's leather case preserved the counterpart to these intrigues—each gathering a testament to subterfuge, each annotation a whisper of falsification. He would explain to his pupils that the forger's penmanship inverts devotion itself: where piety inscribes veracity upon vellum, the deceiver inscribes mendacity with identical gall, and only meticulous collation distinguishes which flourish honours the holy and which serves imposture.

The scribal tradition of the Strasbourg Oaths, as the cataloguer demonstrated in his instruction, stands as testament to textual conspiracy. Every copyist's dialect corrupted the original with the orthographic conventions of his own epoch, and the documentary record laid bare this scheme: successive strata of vernacular corruption, each an incremental act of linguistic counterfeiting executed in pigment the scribe himself deemed authentic.

The scholar's conviction in comparative analysis remained unwavering. He believed that rigorous examination would expose every deception, that fraudulent gall would betray itself beneath scholarly scrutiny. His portfolio contained the demonstration: codices whose calligraphy disclosed manipulation, colophons whose composition contradicted their purported provenance, bifolia whose penmanship employed letterforms impossible for the text's alleged period.

Scribal conspiracy proves the most insidious deception. The counterfeiter need not alter meaning—merely orthography, vocabulary, idiom. Comparative study of manuscripts exposes these transformations: a locution rendered in one hand here, a passage in divergent spelling there, the original composition gradually obscured by successive transcription. The bibliographer's trust in material evidence embodied the detective's creed: credit the artefact, credit comparison, credit the composition.

His pedagogical principle never varied: subject each text to comparative examination. Allow scrutiny to expose manipulation. The original penmanship lies buried beneath layers of transcription, and only the physical document—analysed, collated, catalogued within the scholar's collection—can restore the sacred fidelity of first inscription.
