The tongue of the scribe speaks in marks and annotations. I discovered that the chronicle of every manuscript can be written in the ink of pure transcription, each colophon a reflection of a deeper copying. The game of the quire -- my quire -- is played by foliation: leaf, folio, page, and the mirror of the scriptorium shows how many ways a codex arranges itself.

My chronicle of leaves begins with the stitching of the gatherings. Each folio mirrors the one beside it, each binding reflects the symmetry of attachment. The tongue of the copyist is a language of hand and ruling unlike any vernacular -- no idiom clouds its script, no dialect distorts its letterforms. The ink of a marginal note records what the tongue of ordinary speech cannot phrase: the exact tally of folios, the precise count of quires.

The game of the lectern was a game of vellum and transcription. I built a desk that could hold gatherings -- a structure of oak that performed inscription as faithfully as ink performs marking upon a page. The mirror of the desk reflected my own copying: it held what I held, supported what I supported, and the chronicle of its surfaces was a perfect echo of my own leaves.

The tongue of the manuscript stumbles where the tongue of the folio dances. No rubric can express the stitching that generates the codex; no catchword captures the elegance of a binding. The ink of the bookbinder writes a chronicle different from the annals of the historian -- it records not events but errata, not testimony but tallies, not accounts but collations. The game of leaf and reflection is a game of exact symmetry: every folio on one side of the mirror has its duplicate on the other.

I write this chronicle in the ink of foliation. The tongue I use is the tongue of the scriptorium, and its syntax is the syntax of pure transcription. Every leaf mirrors an idea; every colophon plays the game of meaning. The chronicle of the binding writes itself in the inscription of exact folios.
