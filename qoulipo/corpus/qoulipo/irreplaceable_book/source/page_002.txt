Three scribes divide the folios of this codex among them. The witness bears the pen of testimony; the master bears the pen of instruction; the calculator bears the pen of numeration. Each inscribes in his own manner, yet the work they pursue is the same work: to set upon bound vellum what cannot be reduced to fewer leaves. The annals remember; the treatise teaches; the ledger tallies. Among these three hands, the reader's trust provides the final stitching, and trust is what binds the annals to the treatise and the treatise to the ledger.

The witness opens the volume because the record precedes the proof. He writes in iron gall the hue of old rust, upon membrane the colour of weathered ivory, and his work is the work of attestation: every account he commits is also a pledge that the next reader will believe. Trust and toil are the witness's twin cords; the script he employs is the script of verification, and the tally he preserves is the tally of those who would, if required, attest alongside him.

The master follows because the treatise succeeds the record. His pigment is ground in the lectern's well, and his work is the work of limitation: no theorem unstated, no demonstration unexamined, no numeration absent from the tablet. Trust and total are the master's cords; the script he practices is the script of demonstration, and the annals he maintains are the annals of successive students who, leaf upon leaf, discover that the work of numeration is the work of the irreplaceable.

The calculator closes the sequence because the ledger completes the manuscript. His pigment is the pigment of reckoning, and his work is the work of accounting: count, register, confirm; count, register, confirm. Total and script are the calculator's cords; the annals he compiles are the annals of every value the formula produces, and his trust is the trust that the demonstration, when it concludes, concludes accurately.

Three scribes, three pigments, three scripts, but one record, one work, one total. The trust that binds this irreplaceable volume is the trust that these three modes of inscription—attestation, treatise, reckoning—are not separate but interwoven, and that no one of them can endure without the remaining pair. Between the witness's quill and the master's stylus and the calculator's pen, the membrane of the manuscript is apportioned precisely seventeen ways, and every apportionment is essential, and none is excess. The manuscript is what the three scribes, taken together, cannot fail to complete.
