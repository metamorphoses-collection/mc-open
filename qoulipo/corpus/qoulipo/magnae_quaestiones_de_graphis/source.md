# Magnae Quaestiones de Graphis

*Septuaginta-quinque quaestiones disputatae de invariantibus, dynamicis, isomorphismis, connectivitate, colorationibus, methodis probabilisticis, applicationibus modernis, spectro, algorithmis quantis, hypergraphis, decompositionibus, et praxi grapharum.*

> Expanded corpus. The original *Disputationes de Graphis* (30 quaestiones) and *Disputationes Extensa* (50 quaestiones, +20) are extended here to seventy-five disputed propositions, organised across fourteen blocks. The form follows Abelard's *Sic et Non*: each disputed proposition is defended by one paragraph (Sic) and refuted by another (Non). The disagreements are real; the author declines to resolve any of them.

## Block X — De spectro et eigenvalues

**¶0118. Praefatio in librum decimum.** The adjacency spectrum, the Laplacian spectrum, the singular value decomposition of the adjacency matrix, and the spectrum of the normalised Laplacian each define a family of invariants on a graph. Spectral graph theory unifies them via the relationship between eigenvalues and combinatorial structure (Cheeger inequality, expander mixing lemma, Hoffman bound, Lovász theta). The five propositions in this block dispute whether the spectrum of the normalised Laplacian is the canonical spectral object, whether the second eigenvalue is the right expansion measure, whether spectral bisection finds optimal cuts, whether quasi-random graphs are spectral, and whether the Lovász theta function tightly bounds the chromatic number.

**¶0119. Quaestio LI — Sic.** *The normalised Laplacian is the canonical spectral operator on a graph.* The normalised Laplacian $\mathcal{L} = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}$ has spectrum in $[0, 2]$, is symmetric (orthogonal eigenbasis), and generates the heat semigroup $e^{-t\mathcal{L}}$ that is the standard continuous-time random walk propagator. Chung's monograph treats it as canonical because all the standard inequalities (Cheeger, Buser, log-Sobolev, Poincaré) are formulated in its eigenvalues. Modern applications — graph signal processing, spectral clustering, diffusion maps — use the normalised Laplacian, not the combinatorial Laplacian or the adjacency matrix, as the primary spectral object.

**¶0120. Quaestio LI — Non.** *The normalised Laplacian is not the canonical spectral operator on a graph.* The choice between combinatorial Laplacian $L = D - A$, normalised Laplacian $\mathcal{L}$, signless Laplacian $Q = D + A$, and adjacency matrix $A$ is application-dependent and there is no a priori reason to elevate $\mathcal{L}$ above the others. For random regular graphs the four spectra are related by trivial scalings and the choice is conventional; for graphs of varying degree the four give substantially different answers. The chemistry literature uses $A$ (Hückel theory); the physics literature uses $L$ (vibrational modes, electrical network analogy); the network-science literature uses $\mathcal{L}$ (random walks). The "canonical" reading reflects one community's preference, not a mathematical necessity.

**¶0121. Quaestio LII — Sic.** *The spectral gap of the adjacency matrix determines mixing time up to log factors.* The mixing time of the random walk on a graph is $T_\text{mix} = \Theta(\log n / (1 - \lambda_2))$ where $\lambda_2$ is the second-largest eigenvalue in absolute value. Expander graphs have $\lambda_2$ bounded away from $1$, so $T_\text{mix} = O(\log n)$. The relationship is tight on every standard family: $G(n, p)$, random regular, explicit Ramanujan graphs all match the prediction. The spectral-gap–mixing-time correspondence is one of the foundational results of spectral graph theory.

**¶0122. Quaestio LII — Non.** *The spectral gap of the adjacency matrix does not determine mixing time up to log factors.* The relationship is $T_\text{mix} \leq O(\log n / (1 - \lambda_2))$ for *upper-bound mixing time*; the lower bound is $\Omega(1/(1 - \lambda_2))$, weaker by a factor of $\log n$. On graphs with non-vanishing spectral gap but pathological mixing-time-vs-graph-size scaling (the Aldous–Diaconis examples), the spectral bound is loose. Moreover, mixing time is determined by the *entire* spectrum, not just the second eigenvalue: the small-gap regime where many eigenvalues are close to $1$ has mixing time dominated by the bottom-$k$ eigenvalues for some $k$. The headline result is a useful heuristic, not a tight characterisation.

**¶0123. Quaestio LIII — Sic.** *Spectral bisection finds optimal balanced cuts up to a Cheeger-bounded approximation.* The Cheeger inequality bounds the optimal sparsest-cut value by $h(G)/2 \leq \lambda_2 \leq 2 h(G)$, where $\lambda_2$ is the second-smallest eigenvalue of the normalised Laplacian. The Shi–Malik spectral bisection algorithm thresholds the second eigenvector and produces a cut within a $\sqrt{2}$ factor of optimal sparsest-cut. The procedure is canonical, polynomial-time, and tight on every graph where the Cheeger bound is achieved.

**¶0124. Quaestio LIII — Non.** *Spectral bisection does not find optimal balanced cuts.* The Cheeger inequality bounds *sparsest cut*, not *balanced cut*; the relationship between the two is the well-known Arora–Rao–Vazirani gap, which is $\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})$. Spectral bisection finds a cut whose sparsest-cut value is approximately optimal; the balance of the cut (size ratio of the two sides) is not controlled. On graphs with multiple poorly-balanced sparsest-cuts (the "lollipop" and "bridge" graphs), spectral bisection fails to find any balanced cut at all. The approximation guarantee is for sparsity, not balance.

**¶0125. Quaestio LIV — Sic.** *Quasi-random graphs admit a complete spectral characterisation.* Chung–Graham–Wilson's classical theorem establishes that a graph sequence is quasi-random iff the second eigenvalue of the normalised adjacency matrix is $o(1)$. This is one of seven equivalent conditions, all spectral or counting-based; the equivalence is a foundational result in the theory of pseudo-random structures. Quasi-random graphs are exactly the spectrally-quasi-random graphs.

**¶0126. Quaestio LIV — Non.** *Quasi-random graphs do not admit a complete spectral characterisation.* The CGW equivalence is for dense graphs ($p \asymp 1$); for sparse pseudo-random graphs ($p = o(1)$), Friedman's theorem on random regular graph spectra and Murty's conjecture on Cayley-graph eigenvalues introduce notions of "weak" and "strong" pseudo-randomness that the CGW theorem does not encompass. The sparse-graph quasi-randomness theory has multiple competing notions (Sudakov–Szabó, Conlon–Fox–Sudakov), and the spectrum is one of several invariants in each. The "complete characterisation" reading applies to the dense case only.

**¶0127. Quaestio LV — Sic.** *The Lovász theta function tightly bounds the chromatic number on perfect graphs and most natural classes.* The theta function $\vartheta(G)$ sits between $\omega(G)$ and $\chi(G)$ by definition; on perfect graphs the equality $\omega = \vartheta = \chi$ is exact (Lovász, Knuth, Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver). On vertex-transitive graphs Lovász's bound is tight; on triangle-free graphs Kashin–Konyagin show tightness up to a constant; on most natural graph classes the bound is within a small constant of optimal. The theta function is the canonical SDP-relaxation of chromatic number with the strongest provable approximation properties.

**¶0128. Quaestio LV — Non.** *The Lovász theta function does not tightly bound the chromatic number in general.* On graphs that are far from perfect — random graphs at general $p$, expanders, Ramsey-type graphs — the gap $\chi(G) / \vartheta(G)$ can be $\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})$ (Feige) and is not bounded by any constant. On graphs of unbounded chromatic number and bounded clique number (Mycielski iterates), $\vartheta$ grows logarithmically while $\chi$ grows polynomially, and the gap is unbounded. The theta function is one approximation among several; calling it tight on "most natural classes" overstates the practical reach of the bound.

## Block XI — De algorithmis quantis

**¶0129. Praefatio in librum undecimum.** Quantum algorithms on graphs include Grover-based search, quantum walks (discrete and continuous), QAOA, adiabatic optimisation, variational eigensolvers, and the Rydberg-blockade approach used in this paper. The five propositions in this block dispute whether the Grover speedup applies to natural graph problems, whether quantum walks are the canonical quantum generalisation of random walks, whether QAOA achieves provable speedup on MaxCut, whether neutral-atom MIS achieves a quantum advantage on natural instances, and whether quantum-classical hybrid algorithms outperform their classical counterparts.

**¶0130. Quaestio LVI — Sic.** *The Grover quadratic speedup applies to natural search problems on graphs.* Search for a marked vertex, search for an edge satisfying a predicate, search for a vertex of given degree all admit $O(\sqrt{n})$ quantum algorithms via Grover. On graphs with $n = 10^9$ vertices the speedup is $\Theta(\sqrt{n}) \approx 3 \times 10^4$, which is substantial. The quadratic speedup is the canonical first-order result of quantum algorithms on graphs and the basis of nearly every quantum-algorithmic graph speedup in the literature.

**¶0131. Quaestio LVI — Non.** *The Grover quadratic speedup does not apply meaningfully to natural search problems on graphs.* The $O(\sqrt{n})$ bound presumes oracle access to the predicate; on real graph problems the oracle is the bottleneck and dominates the total time. For sparse graphs (the bulk of real graphs), simply scanning the edge list takes $O(m)$ time which is often $O(n)$ or $O(n^{1+\epsilon})$; Grover's $O(\sqrt{n})$ is sublinear and not achievable when the input must be read once. The "quadratic speedup" is theoretical; the engineering bottleneck of running Grover on a real graph cancels much of it.

**¶0132. Quaestio LVII — Sic.** *Continuous-time quantum walks are the canonical quantum generalisation of random walks.* The CTQW propagator $e^{-iAt}$ generalises the random walk propagator $e^{-Lt}$ in the most direct way: replace the dissipative semigroup with a unitary one. Childs's hitting-time results, Childs–Goldstone's spatial search, Farhi–Gutmann's continuous-time walk on the line, and the body of literature treating CTQW as the quantum generalisation of the Markov chain establish the canonical status. Every quantum-walk-based algorithm in the literature is either CTQW or a discrete-time analog with the same continuum limit.

**¶0133. Quaestio LVII — Non.** *Continuous-time quantum walks are not the canonical quantum generalisation of random walks.* Discrete-time quantum walks (Aharonov–Ambainis–Kempe–Vazirani, Szegedy) have richer structure — coin spaces, interferometric measurements, alternative quantisations — and are the more powerful framework on most concrete problems. The CTQW is one quantum walk among several, and the discrete-time variant gives provable polynomial speedups (Szegedy's Markov chain framework) that the CTQW does not. The "canonical" reading reflects historical priority, not algorithmic dominance.

**¶0134. Quaestio LVIII — Sic.** *QAOA achieves provable speedup on MaxCut at sufficient depth.* The Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm at depth $p$ achieves approximation ratios that exceed the Goemans–Williamson 0.878 bound on regular graphs as $p \to \infty$. Farhi, Goldstone, and Gutmann's original proposal proves the asymptotic $p \to \infty$ limit; recent analytical work establishes finite-$p$ improvements over classical heuristics on graphs of bounded degree. The "provable speedup" is real in the appropriate regime.

**¶0135. Quaestio LVIII — Non.** *QAOA does not achieve provable speedup on MaxCut.* The Farhi–Goldstone–Gutmann result requires $p = \Omega(\log n)$ to exceed Goemans–Williamson, and the resulting quantum circuit has depth that defeats the speedup. At depth $p = 1$, QAOA matches Goemans–Williamson; at higher $p$ the analytical results break down and numerical evidence on random graphs shows QAOA at $p = 5$ underperforms simulated annealing. The "provable speedup" reading is a theoretical statement about an asymptotic regime that no near-term hardware can reach.

**¶0136. Quaestio LIX — Sic.** *Neutral-atom Rydberg MIS achieves a quantum advantage on natural instances.* The Pasqal Henriet et al. demonstrations on unit-disk graphs reach problem sizes (256 atoms in the Leclerc et al. magnet paper, $\sim 200$ in industrial-MIS reports) where classical algorithms struggle to compute the same observable distribution. The recent post-quench resource-assessment study of Vovrosh et al. documents the regime where classical simulators (MPS, NQS, tensor networks) exit a tractable budget. Quantum advantage on natural MIS instances is empirically demonstrated.

**¶0137. Quaestio LIX — Non.** *Neutral-atom Rydberg MIS has not yet achieved quantum advantage.* The Pasqal demonstrations are on regular UDG instances where classical PTAS algorithms reach $(1-\varepsilon)$-approximations in polynomial time; the comparison to "classical struggles" misrepresents what the classical state-of-the-art delivers. The Vovrosh et al.~resource-assessment frames the regime where classical methods become expensive but the corresponding QPU output is itself dominated by noise and not yet a reliable signal. The "empirically demonstrated" reading describes the trajectory of the research programme, not a clean advantage at any specific instance.

**¶0138. Quaestio LX — Sic.** *Quantum-classical hybrid algorithms outperform classical counterparts on graph problems.* Variational quantum eigensolvers (VQE), variational QAOA, and quantum-classical iterative MIS methods systematically outperform pure-classical heuristic baselines on benchmark instances at problem sizes accessible to current hardware. The hybrid framework leverages quantum sampling for the cost-function evaluation and classical optimisation for the parameter selection; the combination achieves performance neither side achieves alone.

**¶0139. Quaestio LX — Non.** *Quantum-classical hybrid algorithms do not outperform classical counterparts on graph problems.* The "outperform" claim is benchmark-dependent: on randomly-generated instances calibrated for hybrid-QAOA strengths, the speedup exists; on adversarial instances or on instances drawn from real applications (industrial scheduling, register allocation), the hybrid algorithms match or underperform pure-classical methods. The pattern is consistent with overfit-to-benchmark: the hybrid framework is tuned on the instances on which it succeeds, and the literature is filtered by publication bias toward positive results.

## Block XII — De hypergraphis et structuris altioribus

**¶0140. Praefatio in librum duodecimum.** A graph constrains pairs; a hypergraph constrains arbitrary subsets; a simplicial complex constrains the structure of intersections of subsets. The five propositions in this block dispute whether hypergraph problems generalise graph problems naturally, whether the Lovász Local Lemma extends to hypergraphs without loss, whether spectral methods on hypergraphs are well-defined, whether persistent homology captures structure that graph theory misses, and whether tensor-network representations of higher-order interactions are tractable.

**¶0141. Quaestio LXI — Sic.** *Hypergraph problems generalise graph problems naturally.* The hypergraph MIS, hypergraph matching, hypergraph coloring, and hypergraph clustering all reduce to their graph counterparts via clique-expansion (or the related star-expansion), and the reductions are polynomial-time and structure-preserving. Results on graphs — Lovász Local Lemma for hypergraph coloring, the rainbow-matching theorem, Berge-cycle decompositions — extend cleanly to hypergraphs with appropriate definitions. The generalisation is natural and the analytical toolkit transfers.

**¶0142. Quaestio LXI — Non.** *Hypergraph problems do not generalise graph problems naturally.* The clique-expansion of a hypergraph blows up the number of edges by $\binom{k}{2}$ where $k$ is the typical hyperedge size, and the resulting graph problem is in a different complexity class. Hypergraph MIS is harder to approximate than graph MIS (Krivelevich–Sudakov); hypergraph coloring has hardness results that graph coloring lacks (Khot's hypergraph coloring gap). The "natural generalisation" reading is true at the definitional level and false at the algorithmic level.

**¶0143. Quaestio LXII — Sic.** *The Lovász Local Lemma extends to hypergraphs without loss.* The LLL was stated by Lovász for hypergraph coloring originally and applies directly to any setting where events depend on subsets of a random hypergraph. The Moser–Tardos algorithmic version works on hypergraph instances at the same complexity as graph instances. The LLL is a hypergraph-native theorem; the graph specialisation is a restricted case.

**¶0144. Quaestio LXII — Non.** *The Lovász Local Lemma extends to hypergraphs with substantial loss.* The constants in the LLL — the $\frac{1}{e}$ bound on bad-event probabilities times the dependency degree — are tight for graphs and not tight for hypergraphs. Shearer's tight LLL gives different constants depending on the hyperedge structure, and the "tight" bound is rarely achievable. The Moser–Tardos algorithmic version has worse performance on hypergraph instances: the expected resampling count grows as a function of hyperedge size. The "without loss" reading is a definitional truth that hides the analytical degradation.

**¶0145. Quaestio LXIII — Sic.** *Spectral methods on hypergraphs are well-defined via the matrix-flattening to the clique-expansion.* The clique-expansion adjacency matrix and its normalised Laplacian provide spectra on hypergraphs that recover the standard graph spectra when the hyperedges are pairs, and they support all the standard inequalities (Cheeger, Lovász theta, spectral clustering). The "well-defined" reading is a definitional choice — the clique-expansion is canonical — and the resulting spectral theory is a direct generalisation.

**¶0146. Quaestio LXIII — Non.** *Spectral methods on hypergraphs are not well-defined via clique-expansion.* The clique-expansion of a hypergraph with hyperedges of size $k$ produces a graph where each hyperedge becomes a $K_k$ clique, conflating $k$-wise interactions with pairwise interactions. On hypergraphs where the size-3 hyperedges differ structurally from triangles among size-2 hyperedges, the clique-expansion erases the distinction. Tensor-spectral methods (Lim, Qi) define hypergraph spectra without clique-expansion, and the two definitions disagree. The "well-defined" reading is one definition among several.

**¶0147. Quaestio LXIV — Sic.** *Persistent homology captures structural information that graph theory misses.* The Vietoris–Rips and Čech complexes built from a point cloud encode the topology of the underlying space across scales; the persistent homology summarises this topology in a finite barcode that is robust to small perturbations and provably informative on the underlying space. On graph-like data (sensor networks, biological networks), persistent homology has been used to identify holes, voids, and higher-order obstructions that no graph-theoretic statistic captures. The information content is genuinely beyond pairwise statistics.

**¶0148. Quaestio LXIV — Non.** *Persistent homology does not capture structural information beyond graph theory.* The barcodes of the persistence diagram on a graph-derived complex are determined by the underlying graph's structure at appropriate scales: the number of connected components, the cycle structure, and the higher-order Betti numbers. All of these are accessible from the graph alone via standard combinatorial algorithms. The "beyond pairwise statistics" reading describes one community's preferred presentation, not new information content. The same barcodes are recoverable from the graph at appropriate filtration scales.

**¶0149. Quaestio LXV — Sic.** *Tensor-network representations of higher-order graph structure are tractable.* Matrix-product states for graphs, tensor-tree networks for tree-structured higher-order interactions, and PEPS (projected entangled-pair states) for grid-structured ones all provide compact representations of distributions over graph-derived random variables with polynomial-bond-dimension cost. The framework extends to hypergraphs with bond-dimension cost scaling polynomially in the hyperedge size. The tractable representation is the basis of modern many-body physics simulations and applies directly to graph-structured data.

**¶0150. Quaestio LXV — Non.** *Tensor-network representations of higher-order graph structure are not tractable in general.* The polynomial-bond-dimension cost is achievable on graphs of bounded treewidth (or related parameters) and grows exponentially with treewidth on general graphs. The PEPS representation on irregular 2D structures has bond-dimension growth that defeats the tractability claim; the recent resource-assessment study of Vovrosh et al. documents the regime where this becomes expensive. The "tractable" reading applies to specific graph classes (tree-like, low-treewidth) and not to the general case.

## Block XIII — De decompositionibus

**¶0151. Praefatio in librum tertium decimum.** Decompositions of graphs into simpler pieces — tree decompositions, branch decompositions, modular decompositions, clique-tree decompositions — provide the foundation of many algorithmic results and the framework of structural graph theory. The five propositions in this block dispute whether tree decomposition is the canonical decomposition framework, whether modular decomposition recovers all natural community structure, whether the graph minor theorem yields constructive algorithms, whether algorithmic graph theory has been transformed by the SPQR-tree decomposition, and whether universal decomposition algorithms exist.

**¶0152. Quaestio LXVI — Sic.** *Tree decomposition is the canonical decomposition framework for sparse graphs.* Robertson and Seymour's graph minor theorem proves that every minor-closed graph family has bounded treewidth restriction to graphs that exclude certain minors, and Courcelle's theorem makes every monadic-second-order definable problem polynomial-time on bounded-treewidth graphs. The two results together establish tree decomposition as the canonical structural-decomposition framework: it is the most powerful decomposition that admits the most general algorithmic meta-theorem.

**¶0153. Quaestio LXVI — Non.** *Tree decomposition is not the canonical decomposition framework.* Alternative width parameters — clique-width (Courcelle–Olariu), rank-width (Oum), twin-width (Bonnet–Geniet–Kim–Thomassé), shrub-depth — capture tractability on graphs where treewidth is unbounded. On dense graphs (cliques and their colourings, threshold graphs, cographs) clique-width is bounded while treewidth is linear in the graph size. The Courcelle meta-theorem extends to clique-width (Courcelle–Makowsky–Rotics) but in different complexity classes. The "canonical" status is shared across multiple width parameters, each best for different graph classes.

**¶0154. Quaestio LXVII — Sic.** *Modular decomposition recovers the natural community structure of well-behaved graphs.* Gallai's modular decomposition theorem provides a unique tree decomposition that captures the recursive substitution structure of a graph: every module (a vertex subset whose external neighbourhoods are identical) is a node in the tree, and the leaves are the prime graphs that cannot be further decomposed. On cographs, comparability graphs, and interval graphs the modular decomposition recovers the canonical hierarchical community structure. On well-behaved graphs the structure is unique and constructive.

**¶0155. Quaestio LXVII — Non.** *Modular decomposition does not recover natural community structure.* On graphs without strong modular structure — random graphs, expanders, generic dense graphs — the modular decomposition consists of a single prime block at the root, providing no decomposition at all. On graphs with weak modular structure (most empirical networks), the decomposition exposes a single weakly-connected hierarchy with no clear semantic content. The "community structure" recovered on cographs is built into the definition of cograph and the recovery is tautological. The decomposition is canonical only on graphs designed to be canonically decomposable.

**¶0156. Quaestio LXVIII — Sic.** *The graph minor theorem yields constructive polynomial-time algorithms for minor-closed problems.* Robertson and Seymour's theorem is constructive in the sense that, for every fixed graph $H$, there is an algorithm to test in $O(n^3)$ time whether a given graph contains $H$ as a minor; the constant depends on $H$ but the polynomial degree does not. The resulting parameterised polynomial-time algorithm for every minor-closed family is the canonical application of the structural theory, and the framework is sufficiently constructive for engineering use.

**¶0157. Quaestio LXVIII — Non.** *The graph minor theorem does not yield constructive polynomial-time algorithms.* The Robertson–Seymour algorithm has an enormous constant in the leading $O(n^3)$ term — the polynomial degree is $3$, but the constant is tower-of-exponentials in $|H|$. For any practically interesting $H$, the algorithm is unimplementable. The result is constructive in a metamathematical sense (the existence of the algorithm is proven) and non-constructive in any engineering sense (the algorithm cannot be executed on real inputs). The "polynomial-time" claim is misleading without the constant.

**¶0158. Quaestio LXIX — Sic.** *The SPQR-tree decomposition has transformed algorithmic graph theory.* The SPQR tree, due to Di Battista and Tamassia, decomposes a biconnected graph into a tree of triconnected components — series, parallel, and rigid blocks — uniquely up to a canonical equivalence. The decomposition supports polynomial-time algorithms for planar embedding, graph drawing, connectivity augmentation, and counting Hamiltonian cycles on planar graphs. It is the canonical algorithmic decomposition for biconnected planar graphs.

**¶0159. Quaestio LXIX — Non.** *The SPQR-tree decomposition has not transformed algorithmic graph theory.* The SPQR tree is specialised to planar graphs and the algorithms built on it solve problems already solved by direct methods on planar graphs. The Di Battista–Tamassia framework is a useful tool in a specific niche (graph drawing) but its impact on the broader algorithmic graph theory is modest. The "transformed" reading reflects the perspective of the graph drawing community; outside that community SPQR trees are a footnote.

**¶0160. Quaestio LXX — Sic.** *Universal decomposition algorithms exist for sparse graphs in polynomial time.* The tree-depth decomposition (Nešetřil–Ossona de Mendez), the bounded-expansion framework, and the nowhere-dense classes provide unified polynomial-time decompositions that subsume tree-, clique-, rank-, and twin-width as special cases. On any graph class admitting bounded expansion, every monadic-second-order definable problem is polynomial-time solvable. The framework is the canonical "universal decomposition" theory.

**¶0161. Quaestio LXX — Non.** *Universal decomposition algorithms do not exist for sparse graphs.* The bounded-expansion framework subsumes other decompositions on specific graph classes but is not itself a decomposition: the algorithmic results require knowing in advance that the graph belongs to a bounded-expansion class. On adversarial sparse graphs not from any known bounded-expansion class, the framework provides no algorithm. The "universal" reading describes a theoretical-classification result, not a working algorithmic framework.

## Block XIV — De praxi et problematibus apertis

**¶0162. Praefatio in librum quartum decimum.** The final block returns to practice and open problems. The five concluding quaestiones dispute whether the canonical open problems of graph theory will be resolved in the foreseeable future, whether neural-network-based graph algorithms have settled the boundary between supervised and unsupervised graph learning, whether streaming-graph algorithms are now well-understood, whether quantum supremacy on graph problems is imminent, and whether the field of graph theory has been transformed by the available computational resources of the modern era.

**¶0163. Quaestio LXXI — Sic.** *The Erdős–Faber–Lovász conjecture on edge-coloring will be settled within the decade.* The 2023 announcement by Kang, Methuku, and Tomon resolving the conjecture for sufficiently large $n$ — every linear hypergraph with $n$ edges has chromatic index at most $n$ — is now formally verified and published. The remaining cases ($n$ below the threshold) are checked by computer enumeration; the conjecture is therefore essentially resolved. Edge-coloring on linear hypergraphs is now a closed problem.

**¶0164. Quaestio LXXI — Non.** *The Erdős–Faber–Lovász conjecture has not been settled and may not be within the decade.* The Kang–Methuku–Tomon result is for sufficiently large $n$, and "sufficiently large" is unbounded by the proof technique; the small-$n$ cases (essential to the original conjecture) are not addressed by the asymptotic result and are computationally intractable. The "essentially resolved" reading conflates the asymptotic with the universal statement; the universal conjecture remains open. The decade prediction reflects optimism in the community, not technical insight.

**¶0165. Quaestio LXXII — Sic.** *Graph neural networks have settled the boundary between supervised and unsupervised graph learning.* Variational graph autoencoders, contrastive graph embeddings (GraphCL, GRACE), and self-supervised graph representation learning have closed the gap with supervised methods on benchmark tasks: node classification, link prediction, graph classification. The supervised-unsupervised distinction is no longer the dominant axis of graph learning research; the relevant axis is now the choice of self-supervised objective (contrastive, masked, generative). The settled boundary is the field's current consensus.

**¶0166. Quaestio LXXII — Non.** *Graph neural networks have not settled the supervised-unsupervised boundary.* On benchmark tasks with abundant labels, supervised GNNs outperform unsupervised ones by 10–20 percentage points; on tasks with sparse labels, the gap reverses but the unsupervised methods still underperform principled semi-supervised approaches with calibrated label noise models. The "settled boundary" reading reflects a publication-bias filter; the practice gap remains and the methodological debate is active. The boundary is not settled.

**¶0167. Quaestio LXXIII — Sic.** *Streaming-graph algorithms are now well-understood.* The semi-streaming model with $O(n \log n)$ memory has matured into a complete algorithmic framework: connected components, spanning trees, sparsification, sketching for approximate cuts, all admit clean polynomial-time semi-streaming algorithms. The dynamic-streaming literature (insertion-deletion streams) has converged on the linear-sketching framework of Ahn–Guha–McGregor. The semi-streaming model is essentially understood.

**¶0168. Quaestio LXXIII — Non.** *Streaming-graph algorithms are not well-understood.* Many fundamental problems remain open in the streaming model: directed connectivity, exact minimum spanning trees with $o(n)$ memory, single-pass shortest paths, vertex connectivity above value 2. The dynamic streaming literature has converged in some areas and remains active in others (maintaining matchings under deletions, hypergraph streams). The "essentially understood" reading describes the state of one sub-area; the broader streaming landscape is alive with open questions.

**¶0169. Quaestio LXXIV — Sic.** *Quantum supremacy on graph problems is imminent.* The combination of $\sim 100$-qubit neutral-atom devices, $\sim 1000$-qubit superconducting devices, and $\sim 10^4$-photon photonic samplers has crossed the classical-simulation threshold on several physics benchmarks (random circuit sampling, boson sampling). The corresponding graph-problem benchmarks — combinatorial-optimisation post-quench dynamics, large MIS instances on UDG geometries, QAOA on adversarial Max-Cut — are within a small factor of the demonstrated quantum-physics supremacy regime. Quantum advantage on graphs is imminent.

**¶0170. Quaestio LXXIV — Non.** *Quantum supremacy on graph problems is not imminent.* The random-circuit and boson-sampling supremacy demonstrations are on artificial benchmarks designed for quantum hardware advantage; the corresponding graph-problem benchmarks have classical heuristic baselines that remain competitive. Recent resource-assessment studies (Vovrosh et al.) document the regime where classical methods become expensive on $\sim 100$-spin post-quench dynamics, but the quantum hardware in this regime is itself dominated by noise (correlated readout, dephasing) that current noise models do not capture. The "imminent" reading reflects a community optimism that the technical evidence does not yet support.

**¶0171. Quaestio LXXV — Sic.** *Graph theory has been transformed by the computational resources of the modern era.* The 2010s-2020s expansion of graph theory — random-graph models from network science, deep learning on graphs, large-scale empirical analysis of citation and social graphs, quantum-hardware implementation of graph algorithms — has been driven by the availability of computational resources that earlier generations did not have. The field's current questions, methods, and benchmarks are inseparable from the computing infrastructure that supports them. Graph theory of 2026 is a fundamentally different discipline from graph theory of 1996.

**¶0172. Quaestio LXXV — Non.** *Graph theory has not been transformed by computational resources.* The classical results of graph theory — connectivity, coloring, planarity, eigenvalues, Erdős–Ko–Rado, Ramsey, Tutte, Kuratowski — were proved with paper and pencil, and the central techniques (probabilistic method, structural decomposition, extremal arguments) remain the same in 2026 as in 1996. The expansion of computing has added tools, benchmarks, and applications, but the core mathematical content of the field — the theorems we prove and the structures we study — is essentially unchanged. The "transformation" reading describes the application landscape, not the discipline.

Epilogos magnus.** The seventy-five disputations of this volume — thirty original, twenty extended, twenty-five further extensions — span the canonical and the emerging questions of graph theory. The reader who has followed the form will have noticed that the disputations themselves form a graph: the questions are vertices, the conceptual dependencies are edges, the contradictions are signed edges. The Maximum Independent Set on this graph is the largest set of mutually compatible positions a graph theorist can hold. Its rigidity is zero by construction. Its alternatives are $2^{75}$ in number. The author has presented each side with equal vigour and declines, as before, to choose. The dialectical form does not resolve; it organises. The reader who wishes to commit is invited to embed the seventy-five disputations on a Rydberg register and let the signed pair-correlator at $T = 2\,\mu$s tell them which positions are jointly anti-correlated under blockade. The Maximum Independent Set there is one possible answer; the disagreement is the truer one.
