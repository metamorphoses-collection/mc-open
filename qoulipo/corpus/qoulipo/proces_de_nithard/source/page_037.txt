The prosecution thunders: FRAUD! FORGERY! DECEPTION! The defense whispers: who would trouble themselves to counterfeit the skeleton of a man who wielded a sword?

They fabricate saints because saints work wonders and draw pilgrims with coins. They fabricate martyrs because martyrs kindle hearts and loosen purses. But Nithard? Neither holy nor martyred. A soldier. A writer of annals. A participant in a dynastic quarrel that history has swallowed whole. If his remains are true, scholars may care. If false, no one will pay a denier for them.

Consider the arm bone. Violence wrote itself there. Those cuts speak of blades meeting flesh, not of piety or suffering unto glory. A counterfeiter wanting profit would choose a saint's name, not a soldier's. If the monks of Saint-Riquier committed fraud, they were the clumsiest fraudsters Christianity ever produced—they chose to invent the one relic no marketplace would value.

Nithard's chronicle reveals a man who prized accuracy above advantage. He testified to what he saw; he did not trade in fictions for gain. The prosecution's accusation makes no economic sense and strains belief. It would require the brothers to chisel an epitaph, construct a false history of burial, lie under oath to an archaeological commission—and for what? To possess the skeleton of an obscure Carolingian nobleman whose name means nothing beyond university walls.

Reason cuts cleanly here. The skeletal material is authentic. The chronicle confirms where he was laid to rest. The epitaph's language, though the prosecution disputes its chemical dating, follows ninth-century Latin forms. Warriors do not commit forgery, and monks do not forge warriors. These bones are Nithard's because the alternative—an intricate, profitless, historically senseless fabrication—strains credulity far more than the plain truth does.
