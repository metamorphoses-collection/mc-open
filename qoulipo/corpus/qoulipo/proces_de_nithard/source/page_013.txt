The prosecution calls its expert witnesses. Professor Jean-Luc Fournier of the Universite de Picardie, specialist in Carolingian archaeology, testified under oath before this tribunal that the excavation at Saint-Riquier was a shambles. The earth's layers were not recorded. The carbon samples were gathered like children picking flowers—no procedure, no witnesses, no chain of hands that science demands. If there is conspiracy here, it was born in carelessness.

Professor Fournier spoke the disciplined language of his calling: conjecture, proof, refutation. He observed that the sarcophagus lay too near the surface—a depth inconsistent with ninth-century burials at Picard monasteries. True Carolingian graves in this country rest between one and a half and two meters down; the Saint-Riquier stone coffin was found at less than a meter. Either Saint-Riquier practiced burial differently from every other house of its age, or someone placed this sarcophagus in shallow ground long after the ninth century.

Let the judge weigh this sworn expertise against the monks' vows. The brothers swore they found something genuine; the carbon tests offer dates so wide they neither vindicate nor condemn; and the expert points to anomalies that pious enthusiasm has not troubled to explain.

A second expert, Dr. Marie-Claire Duval of the Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique, testified that the carbon dating was tainted. She spoke in the measured language of radiometric discipline, observing that the divergence between the Gif-sur-Yvette and Oxford measurements exceeds what a single uncontaminated skeleton should produce. The conspiracy need not be deliberate, she said, but the findings cannot be trusted.

The experts swear by method, not by faith. They vow to speak truth as evidence discloses it, in the language of science, before peers who can repeat their work and test their claims. This is the standard by which this tribunal must judge all who testify.
