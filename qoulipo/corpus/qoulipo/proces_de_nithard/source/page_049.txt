The prosecution sets before us a Viking warrior, fallen in some forgotten skirmish. The defense lifts its pen and strikes this phantom from the record.

Yes, the body may have entered the earth between 810 and 1050, and yes, Vikings burned their way through these lands in 881. But the bones themselves refuse this story. The minerals locked in childhood teeth tell us where this person first drew breath: the Ile-de-France, not the fjords of Scandinavia. Had a Viking raider died here in Picardy, his teeth would carry the signature of northern stone and water. The earth does not lie: this was a child of Francia, not of Denmark or Norway.

The sword cuts on the left arm, which the prosecution ascribes to a Viking blade, could just as easily have come from a Frankish weapon. No chronicler of ninth-century warfare can distinguish Frank from Norseman by the shape of a wound. The prosecution's theory demands we read the evidence selectively: accept the dates when they whisper "Viking," ignore them when they say "Nithard."

The dates are uncertain—this we have never denied. But uncertainty should favor the older claim. Before any modern test was performed, the monks who kept this place said these were Nithard's remains. The dates do not contradict them. The prosecution's Viking appeared only after the dates were known, conjured specifically to cast doubt on monastic memory. Science trusts the theory that existed before the evidence, not the explanation invented to deny it.

The battlefield itself testifies: these are the bones of a Frankish soldier, not a Viking raider, laid to rest in a Frankish monastery by Frankish hands. Ancient testimony and modern science speak as one. Suspicion alone cannot break their agreement.
