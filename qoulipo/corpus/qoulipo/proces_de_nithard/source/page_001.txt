Members of this tribunal, witnesses, archivists of the ecclesiastical record: I rise to open the case against the Abbey of Saint-Riquier. On the fourteenth of April, 1989, the monks of that house announced to the press, to the Direction Régionale des Affaires Culturelles, and at last to this commission, that they had unearthed beneath the east garden the bones of Nithard — grandson of Charlemagne, author of the Historiae, killed in battle in the year 844. They produced a stone sarcophagus. They produced an inscription. They swore an oath. The prosecution intends to prove that every link in this chain — the bones, the inscription, the oath, the discovery itself — is a fabrication.

The discovery is not impossible; it is suspicious. Saint-Riquier had spent the preceding decade in financial decline. The roof of the abbey church leaked. The pilgrim trade had drifted toward Amiens. In 1988 the French government opened a heritage restoration fund for monasteries with authenticated relics. Fourteen months later, the bones appeared. The grant followed in 1990. This is not a coincidence the tribunal can dismiss; it is the seed of the case the prosecution will plant before this court.

The forensic record will tell the rest. Carbon dating from the laboratory at Gif-sur-Yvette returned a span of two hundred and ten years — wide enough to swallow Nithard, wide enough to swallow any anonymous fighting man who fell on Picard soil between Charlemagne's reign and the reign of Robert the Pious. A second analysis at Oxford drifted later still, toward the tenth century, contradicting the first by margins that no single uncontaminated skeleton should produce. The ink in the letter grooves of the inscription contains zinc — a metal absent from authenticated ninth-century iron gall recipes. The handwriting on the chiselled letters bears, under twenty-times magnification, the unmistakable serif of a hand that lived in the twentieth century.

I will show this tribunal that the chronicle is silent on Saint-Riquier's possession of these bones for eleven centuries; that the chain of custody from discovery to laboratory was broken at every link; that the abbot wrote to his bishop in March 1988 announcing his intention to dig, and then in 1990 swore the discovery had been accidental. The bones may be old. The bones are not Nithard's. That is what the prosecution intends to prove.
