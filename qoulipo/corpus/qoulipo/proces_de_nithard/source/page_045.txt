The defense calls the stone coffin itself to testify. The prosecution dismisses the Picard limestone as unworthy of royal interment. But the written record of Carolingian burials is fragmentary, and we must not force every particular into a rigid mold. Not every grandson of Charlemagne was laid to rest in marble. Nithard was a soldier who fell far from the imperial workshops; his companions likely buried him hastily in whatever stone the countryside offered.

The ink analysis of the carved words demands perspective. Iron gall ink was the common medieval writing fluid, mixed in countless local variations. The prosecution claims the tannin-to-vitriol proportion is wrong for ninth-century production, relying on a catalogue of fifteen examined manuscripts. Fifteen is not a multitude; it is barely a handful. To mistake a meager sampling for absolute law—this is the prosecution's methodological failure, not the monks' deception.

Carbon dating of the limestone coffin itself has not been undertaken, for limestone cannot be so tested. But the patina coating the carved letters, examined under powerful magnification, reveals a weathering sequence matching eight to twelve centuries of burial. This evidence favors our cause: no modern deceiver could counterfeit such prolonged erosion.

The parchment scrap found within the coffin, traced to the thirteenth century, aligns with a ceremony of recognition. The ink upon this scrap bears a prayer for the dead man's soul, written in liturgical language matching thirteenth-century Picard practice. This is not forgery; this is a monk praying for one he believed entombed before him. The deception the prosecution imagines would demand the monks falsify not only the inscription but also the mineral crust, the weathering, the elemental signatures, and the devotional prayer. No scheme runs so deep.
