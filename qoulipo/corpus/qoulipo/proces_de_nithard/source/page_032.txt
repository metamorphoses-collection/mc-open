The prosecution brandishes carbon dating as though it were the executioner's blade. But carbon-14 analysis is an instrument, not a sentence. The defense welcomes this instrument and asks the tribunal to read its testimony honestly.

The Gif-sur-Yvette laboratory placed the bones between 810 and 1020 in the common era. This span contains 844, the year Nithard perished. The Oxford laboratory examined a tooth and placed it between 890 and 1050. The combined probability, calculated by methods of calibration, centers near 880 — thirty-six years after Nithard's death, well within the uncertainty attending a single mortal. The bone speaks through carbon, and what it declares is: I am reconcilable with Nithard.

The creed of science is the creed of likelihood, not of certainty. No carbon date is precise. Every result carries an uncertainty, a range of confidence, a statistical qualification. The prosecution seizes upon the later boundary of the range (1020-1050) as though it demolished the identification. But the earlier boundary (810-890) possesses equal validity, and it corroborates the identification perfectly.

The parchment fragment discovered within the sarcophagus belongs to the thirteenth century. The defense acknowledges this. A recognitio — a ceremonial inspection of relics — was customary in the thirteenth century, precisely when veneration of relics reached its summit. The fragment was likely inserted within the sarcophagus during such an inspection, as a label, a prayer, or a devotional marker. The blade of the prosecution turns against their own hand: the parchment does not demonstrate the sarcophagus was opened fraudulently. It demonstrates it was opened reverently, in an era when the faith of the monks was vigorous enough to open a tomb and record what they found.

The bone, the carbon, the faith: they converge upon a single conclusion. These are the remains of a ninth-century warrior, interred at Saint-Riquier, identified by tradition as Nithard. Science does not refute this. Science upholds it.
