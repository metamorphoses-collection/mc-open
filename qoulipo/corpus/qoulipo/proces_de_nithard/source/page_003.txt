The prosecution now lays before the tribunal the question that the defense will not raise: motive. Saint-Riquier in the late 1980s was an institution in measurable decline. The conventual buildings required restoration the community could not finance. Pilgrim numbers had collapsed across the preceding decade as devotional tourism flowed instead toward Amiens and Beauvais. The abbey's annual accounts, filed with the diocesan treasury and obtainable by any serious investigator, show a deficit deepening year on year from 1981 forward. By 1987 the deficit was structural; by 1988 it threatened the community's continued existence in its present form.

In December 1987 the French government, under the aegis of the Ministry of Culture, announced a heritage restoration program with priority funding for monasteries possessing authenticated relics of national-historical significance. The program was widely publicised within ecclesiastical circles. The abbot of Saint-Riquier, Father Guillaume, was personally briefed on the eligibility criteria during a meeting with the regional prefect in February 1988. The correspondence is preserved in the archives of the Direction Régionale des Affaires Culturelles. It is in these archives, and not in the east garden, that the prosecution begins.

In March 1988 — fourteen months before the alleged discovery — Father Guillaume wrote to the Bishop of Amiens. The letter, which the prosecution has obtained and which will be entered into evidence, contains the following sentence: "We have reason to believe that the remains of Nithard lie beneath the east garden, and we intend to conduct a discreet excavation." Discreet. The word is the abbot's own. In 1990, sworn before the archaeological commission, the same man would describe the discovery as accidental, as the unforeseen consequence of routine garden renovation. The chronology speaks louder than the testimony. A discovery announced fourteen months in advance is not a discovery; it is a campaign.

The prosecution submits that the conspiracy here is not sinister but institutional, not the malice of individuals but the gravity of an institution sliding toward extinction and finding, at the eleventh hour, the relic that would save it. The grant followed in 1990. The roof was repaired in 1991. The pilgrim trade returned. The bones, whatever they are, did precisely the work the abbey required of them.

This is not the conspiracy of forgers in a cellar. This is the conspiracy of incentive — the slow, plausible, almost invisible alignment between what an institution needs to find and what it eventually finds. The tribunal is asked to weigh that alignment, and to ask whether the evidence beneath the east garden was discovered, or summoned.
