The prosecution turns at once to the question of method. The monks of Saint-Riquier presented this tribunal with a discovery; we present a methodology by which that discovery must be tested. A bone is not a name. A sarcophagus is not a man. The chronicle of forensic archaeology proceeds by measurement, by reproducibility, by chain of custody — none of which the monks observed when they cleared the east garden in April of 1989.

Consider the carbon evidence first. The laboratory at Gif-sur-Yvette returned a calibrated range of 810 to 1020 of the common era. The Oxford laboratory, working from a separate sample, returned 890 to 1050. The defense will say these ranges include 844, the year Nithard fell at Angoulême. So they do. So do they include the year the Vikings burned Saint-Riquier in 881; so do they include the death year of every anonymous Frankish soldier who lay down in Picard soil across two and a half centuries. The carbon does not name the dead. It dates them. To convert a date into an identification requires further evidence — paleographic, epigraphic, documentary — and it is precisely this further evidence that the monks have failed to provide.

Consider next the inscription. The five capitals NITHARDVS were chiselled, the monks claim, by a Carolingian stone-cutter in the ninth century. The prosecution has examined authenticated ninth-century inscriptions in Reims, Metz, and Aachen. None display the regularity, the textbook fidelity, the absence of local idiosyncrasy that the Saint-Riquier letters display. A genuine Carolingian inscription bears the mark of a workshop tradition transmitted from master to apprentice; it shows ligatures, abbreviations, the small private signatures of a craft. The Saint-Riquier inscription shows none of these. It is the lettering of a paleography manual, not of a gravestone.

The protocols of excavation tell the same story. The bones were lifted from the sarcophagus before the archaeological team arrived. The monks cleaned them, arranged them anatomically on a table in the sacristy, and photographed them. The carbon samples were collected five months later, after the bones had been displayed at a parish exhibition. There is no chain of custody. There is only a sequence of well-intentioned hands. By the time the laboratories received their material, every protocol that distinguishes archaeology from devotion had been broken.

The prosecution does not allege malice. The prosecution alleges method — the absence of one, the substitution of another. And on the question of method, the case is already half made.
