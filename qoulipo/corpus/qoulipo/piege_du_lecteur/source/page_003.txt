The inquiry commences where all such investigations must: with a calculated risk concerning what lies concealed. I have examined the codices—not the originals, which succumbed to the collusion of centuries, but the witnesses that endure like reflections of reflections, each vellum leaf bearing traces of hands that may have corrupted what they transmitted. The scholar's gamble is this: that somewhere beneath accumulating strata of fabrication, an authentic core persists. Yet the investigation demands we establish parameters before proceeding. What constitutes valid testimony? What degree of deliberate falsification must we acknowledge before we denounce a document as spurious?

The penumbra descends across every folio I consult. Each gathering conveys not merely its text but the concealed purposes of its copyists, the silent collusion of monastic scribes who reshaped chronicles to gratify their superiors. The discipline of codicological analysis represents a calculated risk against these obscurities: we stake our reputations that methodology can expose the counterfeit, that the manuscript will yield its secrets under systematic interrogation. Yet the principles governing this pursuit remain themselves contested, and the investment promises no assured return.

I persist because necessity compels me. The deception is not phantasmal—Marechal demonstrated that the relics attributed to Nithard were interpolated, that the documentary record was corrupted by anonymous hands serving obscure purposes. Palaeographic scholarship remains the only endeavour worth undertaking: the calculated risk that authenticity can be salvaged from manipulation, that substance can be distinguished from its distorted image. But the enterprise contains a deeper stratum that most practitioners overlook. The governing principles themselves participate in the deception. The witness that appears most genuine may prove the most meticulously constructed imitation.

Consider the gamble from the opposing perspective. The forger who introduces a counterfeit codex pursues his own objectives: he stakes everything that subsequent generations will accept his fabrication as established fact, that the false impression he manufactures will be mistaken for historical reality. Frequently he prevails. The contest between counterfeiter and scholar proves fundamentally asymmetric—the manufactured document need only achieve plausibility, while the uncertainty it generates can never be entirely banished. The act of scholarly reading invariably constitutes an engagement against an invisible adversary whose manipulations of ink and vellum remain forever partially obscured.
