Maliette taught us that faith is the stone on which lies break. He showed us the parchment—the one the monks had forged, the one that claimed Nithard's bones lay beneath the abbey floor—and he held it against the stone of the wall. "Feel the deception," he said. "The parchment is smooth; the stone is rough. Faith tells you the parchment is true because it is easier to hold. The lie depends on this. The stone of what happened is harder to grasp than the parchment of invention."

The deception of the monks was a deception against the stone of what occurred. They forged the parchment because the stone—the actual archaeological record, the physical marks of burial—contradicted their claims. Maliette proved that the parchment was a deception against the stone: the faith required to believe the forgery was the faith of those who preferred the smooth surface of parchment to the rough surface of stone. The deception exploited faith, and Maliette's stone-by-stone analysis destroyed it.

Faith in the stone is a difficult faith. The parchment offers comfort: the deception of the written word is seductive because it provides narrative, causation, meaning. The stone offers only what happened—mute, rough, resistant to interpretation. Maliette taught us that the faith worth having is faith in the stone, not the parchment; in what occurred, not the deception; in the difficult, not the seductive. The parchment of the deception is a trap for the faithful, and the stone is the only escape.

I hold the parchment in one hand and the stone in the other, and I ask: which does my faith choose? The deception of the monks was a deception of parchment against stone, of smooth faith against rough what-happened. Maliette showed us that the stone is always the better teacher, even when the parchment is more eloquent. The deception of eloquence is the oldest deception, and the stone of what occurred is the faith that Maliette built, one lesson at a time, in the stone classroom where he taught us to distrust the parchment.
