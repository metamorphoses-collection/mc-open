The codex of conviction remains a testament inscribed in ash. Every scholarly gamble on authenticity mirrors this ancient tension—the searing certainty that burns through hesitation, the martyrdom of reputation that consumes the critic who challenges received wisdom, the ecclesiastical purging that destroys the heretical volume to preserve doctrinal purity. This work of discernment operates always under such illumination: the assumption that examination will clarify rather than obliterate, that the record of genuine transmission will endure whatever scrutiny tests its provenance.

The palimpsest of such inquiry has its own layered history. Pascal understood this well: his famous calculation was an intellectual venture conducted under eternal stakes. If the divine exists, the light of belief reveals truth; if not, the seeker is merely consumed by darkness. The manuscript tradition of Pascal's reasoning constitutes the documentary witness of Western philosophy itself—a sustained engagement between trust and evidence that no copyist or forger has yet definitively resolved, no scribe conclusively authenticated.

I pursue a more modest investigation, with gentler instruments. My hypothesis holds that careful structural analysis of vellum and ink reveals what cursory inspection cannot perceive. The method demands patience, the slow comparison of gatherings and watermarks. It lacks the spectacular flourish of those dazzling interpretations I privately favor, the bold attributions that make reputations, but this painstaking forensic attention serves scholarship more honestly than brilliance ever could.

The codex itself stands as the primary witness to any attribution. Critical examination tests every claim. What survives such scrutiny—what remains legible after the forger's craft is stripped away—that alone matters to the careful scholar.
