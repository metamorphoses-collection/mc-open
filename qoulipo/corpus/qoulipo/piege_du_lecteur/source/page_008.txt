The fire consumes the page, but the count survives in memory. When the library of Alexandria burned, the number of scrolls destroyed was itself a kind of written testimony—a record preserved in the recollections of those who remembered what the flames had taken. Each witness who spoke of the catastrophe brought different figures: three hundred thousand scrolls, seven hundred thousand, a number that grew with each telling as the depth of the loss demanded ever-larger sums. The fire was real; the numbers were a performance of mourning, each speaker enlarging the count to match the scale of grief.

Numbers are precise, but they rely on belief. Pascal understood this: his arithmetic machine was a device that calculated in numbers, a mechanism of brass and ivory on which results appeared with apparent certainty. But even Pascal's calculations required belief—belief that the machine translated correctly, that mechanical process spoke the same language as mathematical truth. His intellect burned through received wisdom, but the numbers he produced still required the reader's acceptance.

The page burns and memory fades, but the numbers remain as testimony. I count the words on each page—the number is my method, my way of speaking about texts without interpretation consuming the appearance of objectivity. The digital scholar speaks in numbers, and calculation preserves what close reading might complicate. Belief in numbers is belief in a method that does not deceive, though it may not say everything the text contains.

But the fire reminds us that written numbers die. Every method eventually fails, every number eventually loses its context, every page eventually burns. The belief that numbers can capture the text is a belief tested by time, and the page on which we write our calculations is no more permanent than the page on which Nithard wrote his chronicle. The fire comes for all methods, all numbers, all belief, all pages.
