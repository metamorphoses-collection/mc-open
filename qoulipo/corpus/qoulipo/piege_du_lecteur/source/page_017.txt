Maliette kept a ledger of his scholarly gambles. Every vellum sheet he examined became a numbered entry in this peculiar accounting of intellectual risks: bet number one, that the document before him was a forgery; bet number two, that the historical narrative it purported to contain was pure fabrication; bet number three, that the corpus of authentic Carolingian witnesses was far smaller than his colleagues dared admit. His notebook of suspicions was itself a handwritten codex—each page numbered in sequence, the testament of a paleographer who staked his entire career on unmasking the false.

The ledger of Maliette's wagers is a manuscript I have personally handled. The sheer volume of his bets staggers belief: three hundred and forty-seven numbered entries, each a challenge leveled against a specific witness, each supported by meticulous forensic notes. The vellum of his notebook has grown supple from decades of consultation, the numerals inscribed in the fading blue of fountain pen ink, the record of a life spent gambling against the comfortable certainties of institutional consensus. Each figure represents a skirmish won or lost, and the gathering bears visible scars of this prolonged campaign.

The wager of the master is ultimately the wager of the codex itself. Maliette bet that his accumulated investigations—his catalog of discoveries, his forensic annotations—would outlast the academic forces arrayed against him. His adversaries were numerous, and the palimpsest of his professional standing remained perpetually vulnerable to erasure. Yet his work endured because the underlying gamble proved sound: his tallies withstood scrutiny, his examined witnesses revealed precisely what he claimed, and the record of his scholarly existence transformed into the chronicle of a quiet revolution.

I carry forward his work now. Each manuscript I scrutinize becomes a fresh entry in the wager he initiated, another line in this ongoing inventory of structural doubt. The numbers multiply, the documentary evidence accumulates, and the essential bet remains unchanged: that patient enumeration and careful forensic attention will ultimately defeat comfortable institutional assumptions. Maliette taught me that counting is the scholar's sharpest instrument, the scriptorium is our contested ground, and the scribe's careful record outlives every fleeting reputation.
