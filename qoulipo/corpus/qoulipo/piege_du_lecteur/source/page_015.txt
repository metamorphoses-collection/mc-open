The counting of things reveals what the surface refuses to show. Every word carries weight, and every weight leaves its mark on what lies beneath. I have measured: the leaves in the manuscript, the lines on each leaf, the places where someone scraped away the old text to write something new. Cold calculation shows what pretty handwriting tries to hide—that the sums are wrong, that the old writing underneath tells a different story than the one you can see.

The page itself lies by omission. Where ink has bled away, something remains—not the word itself but the hollow where it used to be, the tally of what has been lost. The wooden boards of the binding hold everything together, and patient study shows the extent of the ruin: how many leaves torn out, how many lines scraped clean, how many hollows where writing once stood. Numbers speak plainly, and damage leaves evidence.

The boards survive where parchment crumbles, and you can measure the space between them. I have laid one manuscript against another, marking where they differ, noting where they agree. Comparison shows what a single copy conceals: how many versions exist, what path it traveled, which family of texts it belongs to. Each manuscript reflects every other manuscript, and the measure of their differences carries weight that scholarship must reckon with.

The tally of things falls across the page like reflection falls across wood. I count, therefore I understand. Mathematics shows the skeleton of the text—how often phrases repeat, what patterns emerge, the frame that the surface obscures. Numbers see through pretty writing to the structure underneath.
