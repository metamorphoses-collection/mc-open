The tongue stakes what the blade cannot guarantee. Every oath is a gamble cast in the penumbra of possible betrayal—the voice speaks what fidelity demands, yet steel remains ready should the voice prove false. At Strasbourg, the specter of fratricidal conflict fell across every syllable. The risk was enormous: two armies, two vernaculars, one trust that spoken words could bind what warfare had failed to settle. The voice offered what the menace of violence had made necessary—a formula of peace uttered beneath the glint of drawn weapons.

Confidence in the spoken word is the most perilous undertaking. The shade of meaning falls differently across languages: what the Roman script calls *fides*, the Frankish copyist renders as *triuwa*, and the distance between these shadows is the distance between two civilizations. The venture of translation is a venture of trust—that the penumbra cast by one tongue's term will overlap sufficiently with another tongue's equivalent. Force enforces what speech cannot guarantee, and the threat of force gives weight to every verbal compact. I have examined witnesses that testify to this: palimpsests where one oath was scraped away to make room for another, vellum bearing the residue of abandoned promises.

I have studied the tongues of the dead. Each word I render is a wager that my modern vocabulary can reach back across the obscurity of centuries. The original context is lost—like ink faded past recovery, like gatherings scattered from their codex. My interpretation stakes everything on reconstructing the intent of scribes whose meanings have shifted beneath later hands.

The gamble is always lost in part. The voice cannot fully recover what darkness has absorbed. Trust in translation is trust in approximation. The scholar tries. The manuscript resists. The question remains open, like a forger's work awaiting detection.
