Maliette taught us that ink is a game played against stone. The teacher stood before us—this was in Lille, in the stone classroom that echoed with the shadow of generations of students before us—and he held up a manuscript page. The ink on it was nine centuries old. "This is the game," he said. "The ink wants to disappear. The stone of the building wants to endure. The shadow of the writer falls between them. Your game is to read what the ink has hidden in the shadow of its own disappearance."

Reading, as Maliette taught it, was always played this way. The shadow of meaning lives not in the stone of the permanent record but in the fleeting inscription. The stone endures but says nothing; the ink speaks but fades. You must catch the ink before time absorbs it into silence. Maliette played with the joy of a man who knew the rules were stacked against him—the ink always fades, the stone always endures, and the shadow always deepens.

He taught us that scholarship is made of shadows. The ink on the page casts a shadow of meaning, and your task is to read that shadow against the stone of evidence. Maliette never used the word "game" lightly—for him, it was sacred, the ink was holy, the shadow was the space where truth lived. The stone provided the rules, the ink provided the moves, and the shadow was the board on which it was played.

I remember the stone walls of that classroom, the shadow of the window falling across Maliette's desk, the ink stains on his fingers from what he played every day with ancient manuscripts. He taught us that ink is mortal, stone is indifferent, shadow is where it happens, and the teacher's work is to show the student how to read the shadow that the ink casts upon the stone. What he taught us is the only thing worth doing: the shadow work, the ink work, the practice of reading against the stone of forgetting.
