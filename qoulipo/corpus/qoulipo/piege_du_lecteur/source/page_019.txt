Maliette forged his arguments as a swordsmith forges a blade: in heat and precision, shaping each claim until it could withstand scrutiny. His fire was intellectual, not physical—the burning attention that consumed centuries of commentary to reach original intention. The blade he wielded was pedagogical: it cut through false scholarship to reveal what lay beneath.

Every manuscript is a surface that holds the scribe's intention. Maliette taught us to read variations as testimony—to see in them different hands, different pens, different centuries. What burned in the writing was the writer's mind, and what shaped the letters was the writer's training. The finished page holds both.

Heat produces the blade, and the polished steel shows the making. Maliette saw this parallel between metallurgy and philology: the manuscript is forged in composition, shaped through revision, and the completed text bears traces of the process that created it. His practice was the swordsmith's: he held the manuscript to the light and read in it the history of its production.

I follow his method. My work traces his thought, and my analysis continues where his began. Maliette's teaching appears in the next generation's practice—his attention, his notes, his criticism, all present in how we work. The heat never dies; the writing never dries; the surface never stops holding meaning; and the blade of good scholarship, forged in Maliette's example, remains sharp enough to cut through any forgery.
