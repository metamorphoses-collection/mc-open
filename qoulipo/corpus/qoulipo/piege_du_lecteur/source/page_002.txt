The manuscript arrived in October, carried by a dealer whose credentials I had no reason to doubt. He unwrapped it with theatrical care: a chronicle purporting to record the succession disputes following Charlemagne's death. The vellum bore the correct aging, the ink had faded in patterns consistent with nine centuries of existence, and the script matched exemplars from the monastery at Corbie. Yet something in the binding troubled me from the first examination.

I photographed the spine under raking light. The boards were oak, appropriate for the period, but the grain ran perpendicular to what tradition demanded. Medieval binders understood that wood splits along its fibers; they positioned boards to resist this tendency. Here, someone had made a choice that invited decay rather than prevented it. The thread securing the quires showed similar peculiarities—too regular in its spacing, too uniform in thickness, as though measured by instruments rather than judged by practiced hands.

The text itself presented no obvious anachronisms. Names aligned with genealogical records, battles occurred on dates confirmed by other sources, and the Latin reflected Carolingian orthography. But I began to notice patterns in the marginalia. Certain folios bore glosses that commented on passages appearing thirty pages later, as though the annotator had known the entire text before beginning his notes. Other margins remained pristine where one would expect the most vigorous scholarly debate. The distribution felt choreographed, designed to suggest authenticity without risking contradiction.

I requested provenance documentation. The dealer produced letters of transmission spanning a century, each describing the chronicle's journey from abbey library to private collection to auction house. The signatures looked plausible, the seals bore proper heraldry. But when I examined the watermarks in the twentieth-century correspondence, I found paper manufactured three years after the letters were dated. Someone had constructed not merely a false manuscript but an elaborate lineage to support it. The question remained whether the deception originated recently or whether I had inherited a forgery already accepted as genuine by previous generations of scholars. Either possibility threatened the foundation of my catalog.
