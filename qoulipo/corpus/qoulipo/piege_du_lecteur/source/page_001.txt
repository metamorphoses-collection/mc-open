The manuscript arrived on a Tuesday, which should have told me everything. Forgeries always arrive on Tuesdays, carried by couriers who avoid eye contact, wrapped in cloth that smells faintly of desperation and library paste. This one came from a dealer in Bruges whose previous offerings included a "lost" Gospel fragment that turned out to be written on paper manufactured in 1923, and a supposedly tenth-century psalter whose ink still transferred to my gloves. Yet here I was again, unwrapping brown paper in my study, while outside the November rain made its usual accusations against the window.

The document itself is extraordinary—or wants to be. Seventy-three folios of vellum, each page dense with what purports to be a fourteenth-century commentary on Boethius, complete with marginalia in three different hands. The script is convincing at first glance: the abbreviations plausible, the orthography appropriately inconsistent, even the wormholes strategically placed. Someone has done their homework. Someone has studied the dispersed fragments of the *Consolatio* tradition with real care, has perhaps even handled genuine exemplars. But study is not the same as truth, and care can be the forger's most damning quality. Authentic scribes made mistakes born of fatigue, distraction, the ordinary entropy of human attention. These pages are too careful, too aware of what they should be.

I have arranged my tools on the desk: the microscope, the ultraviolet lamp, the reference works that catalog pigments and binding techniques. But before I begin the technical examination, I must read. This is the paradox of my profession: to detect a lie, one must first listen to it completely, must enter into its logic and let it make its case. The forger has gone to enormous trouble to create not just an object but a world, a fourteenth-century scriptorium where scholars bent over their tasks and disagreed about Boethius. I must visit this world, must walk its invented halls, before I can identify the anachronism—the single word, the impossible substance—that will collapse it.

Tomorrow I will begin with the binding. Tonight, I read.
