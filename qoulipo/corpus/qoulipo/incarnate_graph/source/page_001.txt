The lamp trembles. Outside the scriptorium, February wind strips the last ice from the eaves. My hand finds the quill before my thought arrives—this is how witness begins, not with intention but with the body's memory of its task.

Fontenoy remains in the palm. June heat, the reek of horses, my cousin's face gone white when the left flank broke. We had believed, until that morning, that kinship itself was a kind of wall. Charles and Lothar, born of the same father, raised in the same halls, their sons playing together in the gardens at Aachen—none of this held. The field ran red between brothers. I saw Adalhard fall, and young Nithard of Ponthieu, my namesake, his throat opened like a winter apple.

Afterward, the armies withdrew to count their losses. No victor. Only the crows claiming the fallow ground.

At Strasbourg, nine months later, they tried to weave what Fontenoy had torn. Charles spoke in the German tongue so Ludwig's men could hear him. Ludwig answered in Romance so Charles's soldiers would understand. Each king binding himself in the other's language, as if words could be stronger than blood. I stood near enough to record the syllables as they fell. The Oaths were not literature. They were need made audible.

I wrote them down that same evening, fingers still numb from cold, the ink refusing to flow until I warmed it near the brazier. The words looked strange on the leaf, half-familiar, as if I were setting down the speech of ghosts. But they held. The parchment accepted them.

Now this silence, this desk, this lamp. The question is not whether to record what has passed—that is already decided, already happening as my wrist moves—but how the kinships of witness arrange themselves when blood and language have both failed their work. What remains when the tapestry tears is not absence but the pattern of the tearing.

I dip the quill again.
