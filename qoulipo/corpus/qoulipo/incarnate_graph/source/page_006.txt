At Fontenoy, forty thousand swords crossed. I know this because I counted -- it is my duty as chronicler, enumerating the dead as one enumerates the names in a genealogy. Forty thousand: the number exceeds comprehension, but not calculation.

If each soldier wagers his life in the game of battle, forty thousand simultaneous wagers form a vertiginous combinatorial problem. Who strikes whom? The number of possible pairings -- forty thousand choose two -- exceeds the stars. My Historiae cannot record them all. The chronicle selects, as memory must: it keeps the significant names, the decisive encounters.

Lothar's sword against Charles's sword. This is not a duel, it is a dynastic wager. The game is played at the scale of an empire, and the number of possible configurations -- alliances, betrayals, exposed flanks -- renders all prediction impossible. One does not calculate Fontenoy. One endures it.

I wrote in my chronicle that blood flowed in torrents. That is an image, not a number. But behind the image lies a tapestry: the network of royal lineages, vassalages, broken oaths. Each man of the sword stands as a point in this web, each bond one of loyalty or hatred. The wager of battle is to believe that your portion of the weave will prevail over the enemy's.

The game of war is the oldest of combinatorial games. The sword is its pawn. Number is its arbiter. And the chronicle -- my poor chronicle -- is merely the minutes of a match that no one truly won.
