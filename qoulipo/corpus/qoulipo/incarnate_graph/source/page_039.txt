"Pro Deo amur et pro christian poblo et nostro commun salvament." The words of the Strasbourg Oaths resound in my maliette -- not the original parchment, which I keep in safety, but the memory of the tongue, that immaterial sword that severs the centuries.

The wager of the vernacular tongue is the wager of the sword: cut clean. No Latin flourishes, no diplomatic subjunctives. The tongue of the oath is a sword tongue -- direct, cutting, irreversible. The faith of the one who swears commits everything: his tongue, his sword, his maliette, his life.

The sword raised at the moment of the oath is a symbol of faith -- faith in the given word, faith that the tongue speaks the truth. The wager of the oath is that the man is worth his word, that tongue and sword are inseparable.

My maliette contains the draft of the Oaths -- that page torn from my chronicle where I noted the words in Romance for the first time. The scribe's sword hesitated: should one write the tongue of the people? The wager of transcription is also a wager of faith -- faith that this tongue deserves the parchment.

Faith in the tongue is the most fragile faith. Words change, pronunciations drift, meanings shift. The wager of Strasbourg is that these words -- "pro Deo amur" -- will keep their force in a thousand years. The sword of time has corroded some, but the core holds: the love of God and common salvation.

My maliette carries this faith, this tongue, this invisible sword, from century to century. The wager holds.
