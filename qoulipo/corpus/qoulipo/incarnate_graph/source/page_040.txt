Grace, says Jansenius, is a gift no game can merit. The parchment of his Augustinus proclaims it in a tortuous tongue -- a theologian's Latin, bristling with distinctions, armed with a scholastic sword the Jesuits will turn against him.

The game of grace is a game without a player: God gives, man receives (or not). Faith consists in accepting this unequal game, this single-edged sword. The parchment of Port-Royal testifies: we pray not to merit grace, but to dispose ourselves to receive it.

The tongue of grace is a purified tongue. No excess words, no flourishes. The parchment bearing it should be as bare as possible -- white as the Host, the sword of ink tracing the necessary minimum.

The game I play in these Pensées -- this game of fragmentary writing -- is also a game of grace. Each fragment is a throw of the dice: some land true, others do not. The tongue guides the quill, but grace guides the tongue. The parchment receives what God wills it to receive.

The sword of polemic -- that of the Provinciales -- is a sword of game: I play the naive, I play the candid, and the tongue of satire cuts more deeply than the tongue of argument. The parchment of the Provinciales is a battlefield where faith and game interweave.

Grace is not a game. But the game of writing, when guided by faith, can become an act of grace -- the parchment illuminates, the tongue sings, the sword turns to pen.
