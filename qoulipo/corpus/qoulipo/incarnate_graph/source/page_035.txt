I return to the number of tongues. Seven -- I have written it, and my chronicle confirms it -- but is this number exact? The parchment of my preparatory notes contains hesitations: is Basque an eighth tongue? Does Breton make nine? The chronicler's faith in his own classification wavers.

The parchment does not lie, but the chronicler can err. The tongue is a fluid object -- it flows between categories as ink between the fibers of vellum. The number of tongues depends on the threshold one sets: how many different words does it take for a dialect to become a tongue? The parchment does not settle this question.

My chronicler's faith is that the number matters less than the relation between the tongues. Their lineage -- how they influence, copy, combat each other -- is more instructive than their enumeration. The parchment of my chronicle attempts to capture this web, but the tongue resists: it is always more complex than the number that claims to measure it.

The chronicle of the empire's tongues is a chronicle of faith -- faith in diversity, faith in unity, faith that the parchment can contain both. The number seven is a pious approximation, a biblical figure imposed on a reality that defies its simplicity.

The parchment of my Historiae is itself in two tongues -- and these two tongues are an act of faith: the faith that Latin and Romance can cohabit on the same vellum, that the number two is not a division but an enrichment.
