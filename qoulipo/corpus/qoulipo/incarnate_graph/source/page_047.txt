At Pentecost, tongues of fire descend upon the apostles and each speaks in a tongue the others understand. The parchment of the Acts recounts this miracle—God's game with human tongues, the sword of grace that severs the barrier of Babel.

If this text is a tapestry, then Pentecost is the moment when the weave becomes whole—each page linked to every other, each leaf comprehensible by all the others. The game of perfect communication is a divine game; the human game produces incomplete tapestries, missing leaves, mutually opaque tongues.

The parchment of our incarnate work is not Pentecostal—it has lacunae, unconnected pages, zones of silence. The sword of selection has severed certain links, leaving a partial, imperfect, human weave. The faith of the writer is that this imperfection is richer than perfection.

The game of tongues is a game of crossed swords. The French of this page crosses the Latin of Nithard, the probabilistic language of Pascal, the Professor's argot. The parchment supports these crossings with its habitual patience. The writer's faith is that the reader will follow, that the tongues will interweave without canceling each other.

The sword of Pentecost is a sword of understanding—it does not sever, it links. The game of this text aspires to the same miracle: linking the pages, the tongues, the leaves, in a parchment that would be a tapestry, a tapestry that would be a machine, a machine that would be a prayer.

Faith in tapestries is my geometer's faith. The parchment is its proof.
