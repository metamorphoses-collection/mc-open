The maliette lies open on the hotel desk, a leather mouth confessing its secrets to the lamplight. I lift each object as though handling radioactive isotopes—the Nithard fragment wrapped in tissue, Pascal's gear-wheel sketch folded into sixteenths, the photograph of my grandfather posed beside a barn door that no longer exists. Every placement matters. If the inkwell touches the compass, something fundamental collapses; if the rosary winds too near the railway timetable, causality itself protests.

Three objects cannot share proximity without one receding into shadow. This is the law I've discovered through years of packing and unpacking, city to city, archive to archive. The bronze key, the moth-eaten ribbon, the postcard of Strasbourg Cathedral—arrange them in a triangle and watch how they pulse with mutual repulsion, each demanding its sovereignty of space. I've learned to respect the distances they require, to honor the invisible boundaries they assert. Some artifacts refuse all neighbors. The miniature hourglass insists on solitude; placed beside anything else, it seems to drain faster, as though time itself accelerates in the presence of witnesses.

My grandfather understood this principle without articulating it. He kept his tools hung on the barn wall in precise intervals, each saw and plane maintaining its territorial claim. "Things need room to breathe," he told me once, though he was speaking of lumber stacked to dry. The maliette is my barn wall, portable, collapsible, a chapel of careful separations. When I arrange it correctly—when the prayer book stands exactly far enough from the mechanical bird, when the letters form their proper constellation—the past doesn't resurrect but it does resonate. Frequencies align.

Tonight I place the Nithard chronicle on the left, Pascal's calculations on the right, my own notebook in the center but slightly back, forming a shape I cannot name in mathematical language but which feels, somehow, maximal. Complete. Each voice preserved in its integrity, none overshadowed, the spaces between them charged with what they refuse to say to each other. Tomorrow I'll pack it differently. The pattern will shift. But tonight, this configuration holds everything I know about distance, memory, and the impossible geometry of simultaneous truths.
