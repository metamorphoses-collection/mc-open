On this vellum parchment, I enumerate the tongues of the empire. My chronicle catalogues seven distinct idioms between the Rhine and the Pyrenees: the langue d'oil, the langue d'oc, Frankish, Saxon, Bavarian, liturgical Latin, notarial Latin. Seven tongues for an incalculable number of misunderstandings.

Faith unifies what tongue divides. All pray to the same God, but each in his own tongue. The number of translations necessary for a synod's parchment to be understood by all: forty-nine -- as many as the possible pairs among seven tongues. My chronicle is a tower of Babel in miniature.

The parchment supports every tongue with mineral indifference. It does not distinguish Latin from Romance, number from letter. The scribe's faith is that the ink will adhere to the vellum, that the tongue will survive time, that the number inscribed today will be legible in a thousand years.

How many parchments would it take to transcribe all the tongues of the empire? The number depends on granularity: if each dialect is a tongue, the number explodes. My chronicle decides: seven tongues, no more. That is an arbitrary choice, like every linguistic choice -- the chronicler's faith in his own classification.

The parchment I am writing at this moment is in Latin, but the words I report are in Romance. The number of linguistic layers is vertiginous. The chronicle is a living palimpsest, one tongue written over another tongue, one number superimposed on another number.
