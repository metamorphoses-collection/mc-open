Queneau was right: constraint is a liberator. I did not know the OuLiPo -- it was born three centuries after my death -- but I practiced its spirit. My Provinciales were constrained by satire; my Pensees, by brevity. The writer's maliette is the tongue itself, with its rules and its prohibitions.

The chronicle of constrained literature is a chronicle of formal conspiracies. Each constraint is a conspiracy against facility -- Perec's lipogram, Queneau's hundred thousand billion poems, and now this incarnate work whose leaves are witnesses and whose themes are bonds.

The game this text plays is a game of dissimulation. The tongue must flow as if it were free, while every word is chosen to satisfy the hidden pattern. The maliette of constraint is invisible -- the reader sees only the text, not the threads holding it.

The conspiracy is joyful: we conspire against the arbitrary. The chronicle of this conspiration is this book itself -- these pages telling the story of their own birth, their own discipline, their own game.

The French tongue is rich enough to support this conspiracy. Its maliette of words -- sixty thousand according to the Academy -- contains enough combinations to satisfy any pattern, any rule.

The game continues. The chronicle writes itself. The conspiracy reveals itself to those who know how to read between the lines. And the maliette remains open -- for in the OuLiPo, the secret is the constraint itself, proudly exhibited like a badge of honor.
