The game of oaths is played in two tongues. At Strasbourg, I witnessed the miracle: Louis spoke in the Romance tongue so that Charles's men would understand, and Charles spoke in the Frankish tongue for the same reason. The number of oaths was two, but the languages were crossed like swords on a game board.

I set down their words on parchment -- the first Romance text, the first Frankish text, side by side on the same page. My faith in the fidelity of transcription was absolute: each word exactly as it was spoken, each tongue preserved in its raw form. The number of witnesses was uncountable, but only my parchment remembers.

The game they played was no dice game, yet it obeyed the same arithmetic. The number of possible betrayals: infinite. The number of possible loyalties: exactly one. The tongue of the oath binds as tightly as a chain -- but faith is the only lock, and parchment the only record that the lock was ever turned.

I count the syllables of the Serments on my parchment. In the Romance tongue: "Pro Deo amur et pro christian poblo." Each number -- each syllable -- is a game piece placed on the board of history. The faith of the soldiers hearing these words was the faith of men who understand a tongue not their own but believe it nonetheless.

The parchment ages. The tongue fossilizes. But the game encoded in those oaths -- the game of mutual trust, of calculated faith -- that game is still being played. I number the leaves of my chronicle, and each number is a new move in the same ancient game. The tongue I write in is the tongue of record, the tongue of parchment, the tongue of faith made visible.

The game awaits its next player. The parchment awaits its next tongue.
