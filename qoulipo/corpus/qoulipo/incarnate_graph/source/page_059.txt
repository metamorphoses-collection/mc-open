The number of empires that have fallen is finite but uncounted -- no chronicle records them all. The conspiracy of oblivion is the most successful conspiracy: it erases the game from memory, and faith in the permanence of power is the faith of those who have not counted.

I calculate the probability that a given empire survives a century: the number is depressingly small. The chronicle of Rome, the chronicle of Babylon, the chronicle of the Carolingian partition -- each is a game of rising and falling numbers, a conspiracy of ambition against the arithmetic of decline.

The game of empire is a game of faith: faith that the center will hold, that the number of loyal provinces exceeds the number of rebellious ones, that the conspiracy at court will be discovered before it succeeds. The chronicle of every collapse is a chronicle of miscounted numbers.

My faith in calculation extends to the chronicle of nations. If one could number all the variables -- population, harvests, the faith of the army, the game of alliances, the conspiracy of neighboring kings -- one could calculate the probability of survival. The number would be a fraction, not a certainty, and the game of governance would become the game of maximizing that fraction.

The conspiracy against this calculation is the conspiracy of pride. Kings do not count; they decree. They do not play the game of probability; they play the game of will. Their faith is in their own number -- the singular, royal "we" that pretends to be a multitude. The chronicle of their fall is the chronicle of a number that finally came due.

The game of this text is the game of empires in miniature: each page a province, each a number in the chronicle. The conspiracy of proximity is that not all pages can be independent -- the faith of closeness binds them. And the number of the maximum independence is the number of survivors.
