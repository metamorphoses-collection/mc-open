I read Nithard's chronicle and see a tapestry in it. Each tongue mentioned is a knot; each translation a crossing of warp and weft. Seven languages, according to Nithard -- the vernaculars rising against Latin like subjects against an emperor.

When peoples cease speaking Latin, they cease understanding each other. The tongues multiply, the tapestry's weave loosens, the chronicle splinters into mutually unintelligible dialects. Communication becomes betrayal -- each tongue divides what Latin once joined.

I counted: between Nithard's ninth century and my seventeenth, the written European tongues rose from seven to over thirty. The tapestry grew denser yet more fragmented -- more knots, but the weave itself weakened. The chronicle of this splintering is also the chronicle of power: royal courts impose their dialect, academies fix grammars, printers choose their medium.

Linguistic standardization is a matter of numbers: how many speakers does it take for a dialect to become a tongue? The number is political, not linguistic. The truth is obvious: the French of Paris prevails over Picard, the Tuscan of Florence over Venetian -- not by linguistic merit, but by the cannons supporting it.

The chronicle of tongues is a chronicle of silent power, mute struggles, numbers that extinguish dialects one by one.
