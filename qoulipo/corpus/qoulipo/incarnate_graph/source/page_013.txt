The tournament is a sword game where conspiracy hides behind chivalry. My chronicle reports the one of 842: the sons of Louis confront each other in simulations of war, wagering their honor and sometimes their lives on the point of a lance.

The tournament's wager is codified -- so many passes, so many falls, so many victories determine rank. But behind the official game, another game is played: the conspiracy of vassals who wager on the results, who rig the fights, who grease the swords so they slip at the critical moment.

The chronicle cannot say everything. The tournament's conspiracy is a clandestine web superimposed on the official web. The visible connections -- the combats -- hide invisible connections -- the secret agreements, the rigged wagers, the sabotaged swords.

I recorded in my Historiae the official results. But the true game, the real wager, was played in the tents, far from the chroniclers. The conspiracy's sword is quieter than the combat's sword: it strikes in the back, it severs nocturnal alliances, it wagers on the judges' greed.

The tournament of 842 lasted three days. Three days of public game and private conspiracy. Wagers accumulated like wounds. The sword does not distinguish the honest player from the cheater -- it cuts equally in both directions.

My chronicle is the only impartial witness. But the chronicler too wagers -- he wagers that truth has value, that the game of writing is worth the game of the sword. That is a wager I have never regretted.
