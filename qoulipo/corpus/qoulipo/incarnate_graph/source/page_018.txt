My father Angilbert's sword hung on the abbey wall, beside the crucifix. Two objects of faith: one severs bodies, the other saves souls. What led to Fontenoy mingled the two -- monks blessed the swords, warriors prayed before the massacre.

My chronicle records this obscene mixture. The warrior-bishops believed one could serve God and Mars simultaneously, that faith and the sword were merely the two edges of one blade. The Carolingian heresy was not merely political -- it was theological.

Lothar's sword bore an inscription: "In nomine Domini." Faith engraved in steel. Murder justified by the sacred. My chronicler's task is to denounce this confusion without losing my own faith -- to separate the sword from the crucifix, slaughter from prayer.

The armies of the imperial court believed in divine blessing. Their certainty rested on the conviction that God was on their side -- each camp believed it, with equal and contradictory faith. The sword settled what theology could not resolve.

My chronicle is witness to this paradox. Divided faith begets rivalry. Rivalry begets the sword. The sword begets mourning. And the belief -- the insane belief that violence has meaning -- continues from generation to generation.

My father's sword is still on the wall. The crucifix too. The alliance has changed form, but faith and the blade remain intertwined, like two strands of the same cloth.
