The parchment of the Strasbourg Oaths is the only one that survives. I transcribed it in my Historiae -- the first text in the Romance tongue, say the future philologists. But for me, it was a writing game, a chronicle forced to transcribe in phonetic symbols a tongue that had never been written.

How many words in the Oaths? The exact number depends on what one calls a "word" in a tongue that does not yet have a grammar. The parchment is ambiguous -- the spaces between letters are irregular, the tongue hesitates between Latin and Romance. My chronicle normalizes, but the game of normalization is always a game of power.

Each word of the Oaths is a witness in the web of the nascent tongue. The connections link words that appear together -- "pro Deo amur" forms a triangle, a complete knot with three voices. The number of these triangles, across the entire text, measures the tongue's cohesion. The more there are, the more structured the tongue, the more legible the parchment.

The game of the chronicle is to count these structures. How many word pairs recur together? How many triplets? The number increases with the text's length, but the parchment is short -- the Oaths fit in a few lines. The tongue there is compressed, dense, like a tapestry where each witness is linked to nearly every other.

My parchment is a photograph of the tongue at the moment of its birth. The number of words: finite. The number of interpretations: infinite. The chronicle continues.
