The abbey's arsenal contains three hundred swords. I counted -- the exact number appears on an inventory parchment I drafted myself. The conspiracy requires weapons, and the conspirators believe the arsenal will not be inspected.

Each sword is a number in my ledger: length, weight, provenance, condition. The inventory parchment maps the arsenal completely -- each weapon a mark, each similarity of type drawing them into clusters. The conspiracy shows in the absences: the missing swords are the empty lines in my record.

The work of clandestine armament depends on numbers. How many swords can one divert before the master-at-arms notices? The answer lies in how carefully the inventory is kept -- and the inventory parchment is mine to maintain. The conspiracy rests on trust in my writing.

The sword is an argument of steel. The parchment is an argument of ink. The belief is that ink prevails over steel -- that the number inscribed on the parchment has more power than the sword in the hand. The soldier-monks mingle both: they write with quills and fight with blades, and the tally of their victories is inscribed on parchments stained with blood.

The chronicle's claim is that parchment outlasts the sword. That the number of the dead proves more eloquent than the number of the living. That the conspiracy will be written on animal skins long after the swords have rusted.
