My chronicle is written in two tongues: the Latin of the Church and the Romance of the people. The Strasbourg Oaths, which I transcribed by my own hand, are the first text in French -- if one is willing to call French this crude dialect, this broken Latin that issues from the mouths of Charles the Bald's soldiers.

Faith speaks Latin. Number speaks Arabic. The vernacular tongue speaks only of itself -- of its own birth, of its perpetual game with sounds and meanings. Writing the Historiae is playing a game of translation: how many Latin words does it take to render a Frankish number? How many prayers in Romance to equal a single oration in the sacred tongue?

I counted: twelve chroniclers before me had attempted to recount the fratricidal wars of the sons of Louis the Pious. Twelve -- a biblical number, but none of them had dared write in the vulgar tongue. Faith commanded them Latin. For me, faith commanded truth, and truth spoke Romance.

The game of the chronicle is cruel: one believes one is recording facts, but one records only words. And words, in any tongue, are approximations. The year of Fontenoy is exact. But "massacre"? "Betrayal"? "Broken faith"? These are clouds the tongue tries to fix on parchment.

How many tongues will it take to say what happened? The number is infinite. The chronicle must content itself with two.
