On this parchment, I inscribe the numbers of mathematical expectation. The tongue I use is a mixture of French and notation -- Arabic numerals, Latin letters, symbols I invent as the quill flows. The parchment supports everything: faith and calculation, game and proof.

How many rounds of dice must one play for the expectation to be realized? The number is theoretically infinite, but the player's faith is finite. He leaves the table when his purse is empty -- and there is the paradox: the tongue of probability speaks of the infinite, but the real game lasts only as long as a parchment.

I write in French because I want to be read by honest folk, not only by doctors of the Sorbonne. The vernacular tongue is a wager in itself: one wagers that the people can understand number, that faith does not require Latin. My parchment is bilingual despite itself -- French words for the ideas, universal symbols for the calculations.

The game of composition is also a game of discipline. Each parchment is costly -- vellum is rare, ink precious. I count my words as a miser counts his coins. Every line must earn its place. Every word must justify itself. The faith that each word is the right one: immense.

Will this parchment survive? The tongue changes, numbers remain. In a thousand years, "3.14159" will still say the same thing. But "expectation" -- this word I have charged with a new meaning -- will it have kept its dual nature, at once calculation and prayer? The game of time is the cruelest of all games.
