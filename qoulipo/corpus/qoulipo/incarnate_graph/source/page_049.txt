There is a psalter in my maliette -- small, worn, bound in brown leather. The parchment is so fine it lets light through, and the letters can be read from both sides like an involuntary palimpsest. The tongue is Latin, but a hand has added glosses in Romance in the margins -- a mute chronicle of the transition of tongues.

The psalter's faith is the Professor's oldest faith. Before the maliettes, before the roads, there were the psalms. Each morning, I read one -- in Latin, for it is the tongue of the original, and in Romance in the margins, for it is the tongue of my heart.

The psalter's parchment is a parchment of memory. Each page is a chronicle of faith -- from David to me, three thousand years of prayer on different animal skins but identical in their welcome of ink. The tongue changes; the psalm endures.

My maliette protects the psalter as a reliquary protects a bone. But the psalter's parchment is more precious than any relic -- it contains the voice of God translated into human tongue, a chronicle of the covenant between heaven and earth.

The tongue of the psalms is a tongue of combat and tenderness. The parchment vibrates when I read: "Dominus illuminatio mea et salus mea, quem timebo?" Faith trembles in these Latin words, and the Romance gloss murmurs: "The Lord is my light and my salvation, whom shall I fear?"

My maliette is a tabernacle. The parchment is its host. The Professor's chronicle is the chronicle of this portable faith, this mobile tongue, this God who travels in a leather maliette.
