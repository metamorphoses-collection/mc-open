The tongue of the sword is a tongue without words -- it speaks in the number of blows struck, the record of wounds inflicted, the silence that follows the final thrust. Steel has its own grammar: the parry is a conjunction, the riposte a verb, the feint a subjunctive.

The number of fencing masters in Paris is forty-two -- I have counted them, as I count everything. Each master speaks a different tongue of the blade, and the record of their rivalries is written in competing schools. The Italian tongue of the rapier contends with the French tongue of the epee, and the number of duels between their students is the true measure of their disagreement.

The sword is a tongue that translates thought into consequence. The number of possible moves in a fencing bout is finite but enormous -- a combinatorial game not unlike chess. The body calculates faster than the mind: the blade knows its target before the fencer's tongue can name it.

I record these observations because the tongue of probability applies to the sword as to the die. The number of possible outcomes in a duel -- wound, death, draw, reconciliation -- is small, but the variables are vast. The speed of the blade, the number of hours of practice, the tongue of the master, the secret weakness concealed within the swordsman's stance.

This work is a record of intellectual swords -- each page a thrust, each chapter a parry, each connection a number recording which blades crossed. The tongue of the text is the tongue of form, and what it hides is this: a web of numbers that determines which swords may cross and which must pass in silence.

The sword speaks. The record endures. The number remains. The tongue translates. And the silence holds.
