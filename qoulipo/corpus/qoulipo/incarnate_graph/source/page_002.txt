I count. That is all I know how to do now -- count. The years since Fontenoy, the monks who copy, the conspirators who whisper in the corridors of the abbey. Number is my chronicle, each digit a testimony.

There were forty-six brothers at Saint-Riquier when the conspiracy began. I enumerated their alliances on a table I traced in the margin of my Historiae, a tapestry before the word existed. Each monk a witness. Each nocturnal meeting a bond. Faith bound them as much as the conspiracy: they believed that forging relics was an act of grace, that God pardons fabrications made in His name.

I understood, by counting the possible combinations, that their network formed a tangle no blade could sever cleanly. Too many bonds to disentangle the guilty from the innocent. Too many connections to excise the rotted heart without killing the whole body.

The game they played had no written rules. It was a game of trust and betrayal, where faith served as currency and number as proof. How many are they? Forty-six, then forty-three after the purges, then thirty-seven. The numbers descend like a staircase toward the hell of suspicion.

I record everything in my chronicle. I note the witnesses and their bonds. I am the first to map such webs, without knowing it, nine centuries before Euler. My faith in God has not wavered, but my faith in man -- that faith is measured in decreasing numbers.

The game continues. The conspiracy mutates. Numbers, for their part, never lie.
