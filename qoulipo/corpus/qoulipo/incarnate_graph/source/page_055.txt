I number the traitors. After Fontenoy, after the carnage where sword met sword and the field ran red, I counted the dead and found the conspiracy: Lothar's men had wagered on our defeat and hidden their swords until the battle turned.

The number of conspirators is always uncertain -- that is the nature of conspiracy. One counts the swords drawn, but not the swords concealed. The wager of the traitor is a calculated wager: he risks the blade if his side loses, but his faith is in the winning odds. The conspiracy of Lothar was a conspiracy of arithmetic -- he counted our numbers and calculated that his hidden swords would tip the balance.

My faith in the count is the faith of a chronicler who knows that numbers can be falsified. The conspiracy against truth is the oldest conspiracy: inflate your numbers, diminish the enemy's, and the wager on posterity is half won. But my sword -- the sword of honest testimony -- cuts through these fabrications. I number what I saw, not what I was told.

The wager of Fontenoy was a wager of steel against steel, faith against faith. The number of dead: forty thousand, they say, though no one truly counted. The conspiracy of round numbers is a conspiracy of convenience -- forty thousand sounds grave, and the sword of rhetoric requires gravity.

The faith of the soldier is a simple faith: the blade will hold, the number of allies will suffice, the wager on courage will pay. But the conspiracy undermines all calculation. The hidden sword changes the arithmetic; the secret wager alters the odds; the concealed plot makes the faithful man a fool.

I number the leaves of my Historiae and each number is a wager against forgetting, a sword against the conspiracy of silence, an act of faith in the stubborn power of counting.
