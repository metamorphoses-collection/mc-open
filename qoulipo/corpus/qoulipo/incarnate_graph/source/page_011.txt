The bones of saints are materialized wagers. Each relic is a number -- a certificate of authenticity, a date, a place of discovery -- that faith transforms into an object of veneration and that conspiracy can transform into an object of fabrication.

I calculated the probability that a given bone is authentic, given the number of known falsifications. The result is damning: less than one in ten. Should faith rest on such probabilities? The sword of reason decides: no. But faith does not obey reason -- that is precisely why it is faith.

The conspiracy of false relics is a problem of numbers: how many forgeries for each authentic? The forger-monks' wager is that the number of believers will always exceed the number of skeptics. And this wager, historically, they have won -- the sword of credulity is sharper than that of doubt.

But I, Pascal, doubt methodically. Number tells me there are too many saints' bones for them all to be true -- a single saint cannot have seven femurs. Faith tells me that grace inhabits not bones but hearts. The conspiracy, for its part, mocks both faith and number: it knows only profit.

The authentic wager -- mine -- is not on bones. It is on the invisible. On that God whom the sword cannot touch and whom number cannot measure. The relic conspiracy is grotesque precisely because it materializes what should remain immaterial.

The probability that God exists is not calculated with bones. It is calculated with the heart. And the number of the heart is one -- one single, infinite.
