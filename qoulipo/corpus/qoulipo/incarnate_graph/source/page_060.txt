The promise of the courier is the simplest bargain: I bet that the parchment in my maliette will arrive intact, that the tale of the road will not include bandits, floods, or customs officers with excessive curiosity. Each delivery is a roll of managed dice.

The parchment I carry today is heavier than usual. The secret it contains—or so the client hinted—is a chronicle of debts, a ledger of favors owed, a web of influence spun across three dioceses. The promise is that this parchment reaches its destination before the bishop's audit.

I do not read the parchment. That is the Professor's first rule, the oath of willful ignorance. The secret passes through me as light passes through glass—I am transparent to the plot, and the tale of my involvement is a tale of surfaces. Innocence is the most sophisticated shield I carry.

Each delivery is a promise made to the road. The parchment is fragile; the secret is urgent; the tale of delays is the tale of the courier's shame. Reputation demands punctuality: one late parchment, one broken promise, and the clients vanish like morning frost.

This work too is a tale of deliveries—each page a parchment delivered to the reader, each echo a secret shared in whispers, each link a promise that two moments belong together. Reading is the courier's work reversed: the reader carries the parchment through his own mind, and the secret reveals itself—or does not—at the journey's end.

The promise stands: the parchment will arrive. The secret will be delivered. The tale will be complete. What the Professor began, what the reader continues, what the work asks of us all—this remains.
