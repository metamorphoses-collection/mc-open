The conspiracy is read in the parchment as much as in the sword. When a document is falsified, the vellum itself testifies: ink too fresh on parchment too new betrays the mendacious chronicle. The forger's wager is that no one will look closely.

The sword and the parchment are the two pillars of Carolingian power. One constrains bodies, the other constrains minds. The conspiracy uses both together: a false charter drafted on old parchment, sealed with the stolen imperial seal, and backed by loyal swords.

My chronicle denounces these falsifications. The parchment of my Historiae is authentic—I can prove it by dating the ink, by my handwriting, by the wager of my reputation. The sword that protects me is that of my rank: grandson of Charlemagne, I have no need to falsify.

The conspiracy of false Carolingian diplomas is a conspiracy of parchments. Each forgery is a wager—the wager that the community will accept the vellum as proof of a right, a privilege, a donation. The sword punishes those who contest the parchment. The chronicle records the failed contestations.

The parchment is more dangerous than the sword, for it lasts longer. A sword stroke kills a man; a false parchment steals an estate for centuries. The conspiracy knows this, and the forgers' wager is a wager on duration—that the vellum will outlast the memory of witnesses, that the true chronicle will be lost while the forgery prospers.
