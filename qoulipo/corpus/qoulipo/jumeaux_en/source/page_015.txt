# The Chronicle's Wager

Nithard staked everything upon his chronicle. He set down the history of his own days—the civil wars, the betrayals, the conspiracies—upon vellum he knew might not outlast the next sacking. The wager was existential: if the manuscript were consumed by fire, if the abbey fell to ruin, if the brothers who guarded the vellum forsook their calling, then Nithard's testimony would perish with it, and with that perishing the sole witness to the Oaths of Strasbourg.

The conspiracy Maréchal afterwards brought to light was aimed not at Nithard himself but at the veracity of his chronicle. Brothers at Saint-Riquier, three hundred years beyond Nithard's death, professed to have unearthed his relics—a scheme to magnify the monastery's standing by conjuring a royal saint. The vellum authenticating this discovery was counterfeit; the devotion sustaining Nithard's cult rested upon invention. Yet the chronicle itself—Nithard's true history—remained authentic. The conspiracy wound itself about a pillar of truth like ivy upon stone.

Every chronicler wagers that someone shall read what he has written. Nithard laid this wager in the darkest season of Carolingian history, when confidence in the empire was crumbling, when every compact might prove a conspiracy, when the vellum bearing history could be scraped clean and pressed into service for a psalter. The gamble was not whether posterity would care, but whether the manuscript would endure in the flesh.

The faith required to compose a chronicle during civil war is the faith of absurdity. You record what you witness, knowing the conspiracy you are chronicling may strike you down before you complete the vellum. Nithard perished in battle in eight hundred and forty-four, two years after Strasbourg. His chronicle breaks off mid-sentence—the surest proof the peril was genuine, the wager no mere figure of speech.

The conspiracy of silence following his death endured three centuries. Then the brothers gambled upon his bones, the vellum rose again to light, and the chronicle resumed its improbable survival.
