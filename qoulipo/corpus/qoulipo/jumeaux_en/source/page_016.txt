# The Grammar of Victory

The Strasbourg Oaths yield readily to the scholar's reckoning. Sixty-three words stand in proto-Romance, eighty-one in Frankish. One may count the syllables, enumerate the stressed vowels, catalogue the inventory of sounds—all such measurements transform living speech into columns of figures. Yet this very transformation constitutes the scholar's vocation: the conviction that language surrenders to numbering.

The sword determines which tongue shall endure. After Fontenoy, the victors' vernacular became the language of chancelleries and scriptoria, whilst the vanquished tongue withdrew into the countryside, dispersed among scattered villages, fated to perish within three generations. The battle was a trial whose spoils included linguistic survival itself: the prevailing tongue would be inscribed in schoolbooks and charters; the defeated would persist only in devotion, in the private orisons of a dwindling faithful.

The oath's substance was martial: each warrior swore upon his weapon, palm upon sword-hilt, that he would uphold his lord's claim to sovereignty. The ceremony of feudal allegiance reduced conviction to arithmetic—how many blades might each brother muster? Lothar counted his adherents and discovered them insufficient. Charles counted his own and found them adequate. The tally of armed retainers determined the resolution of that crisis of loyalty which was the Carolingian fratricidal war.

The tongue of the oath was itself weaponry in this contest. By swearing in the vulgar speech—in the soldiers' own language rather than the clerics' Latin—Charles and Louis transformed utterance into armament. The oath represented a calculated wager: would the warriors' faith in a promise spoken in their native tongue outweigh their mistrust of promises spoken in the ecclesiastical language? The paucity of defections following Strasbourg suggests the wager proved sound.

Every word of the oath may be counted against its progeny in modern French. The scholar's labour of etymological reckoning reveals an underlying conviction: that language persists unbroken, that the battle-speech of 842 and today's tongue remain joined by traceable descent, that the patterns of phonetic transformation may be enumerated as one enumerates the patterns of warfare.
