The chronicle records what the sword creates and the parchment preserves. In the archives of the Carolingian empire, manuscripts and weapons rest side by side—inventories of arms beside histories of their deployment. The tally of swords in the royal armory was inscribed on vellum with the same care that the librarian bestowed upon the psalms in his psalter. Trust in the record is trust in the reckoning: if the count be true, the chronicle may be believed.

The history of Fontenoy may be recovered from parchment alone. The military dispatches, the chronicles of Nithard and Angelbert, the administrative accounts of troop movements—each document offers figures that, when set against one another, yield a portrait of the battle. The sword performed the slaughter, but the manuscript performs the remembrance, and the conviction that these two correspond forms the cornerstone of military history.

Fewer manuscripts record the battle of Fontenoy than swords were drawn there. This disproportion haunts every chronicle: the parchment captures but a fragment of the event, and the historian's conviction is that the fragment stands for the whole. The reckoning is never precise. The sword creates a surfeit of reality that no manuscript can contain, and the chasm between event and record is bridged by the belief that enumeration suffices.

In the archive, each parchment bears its number. The system of shelf-marks—letters and figures combined—is itself a chronicle of the archive's evolution: every manuscript acquired alters the tally, every manuscript lost changes the sum. The sword of time—decay, fire, flood, war—labours against the parchment of memory, and the archivist's conviction is that the surviving manuscripts suffice to reconstruct what has passed.

The chronicle is an act of reckoning: tallying events, enumerating causes, calculating consequences. The parchment is the medium. The sword is the cause. The conviction is that number binds them together.
