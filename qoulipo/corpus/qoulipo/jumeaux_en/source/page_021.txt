# The Tongue as Blade: Language and Oath at Strasbourg

The chronicle of Fontenoy preserves an account wherein speech itself became weaponry, and allegiance a hazard staked upon comprehension. When Charles the Bald addressed his host and pronounced his oath in the common tongue, he entered into a compact governed by rigorous conditions: his soldiers must grasp the words entire, and such understanding demanded the speech of the encampment rather than the language of the scriptorium.

The idiom of the oath served as a blade in its own right. Speaking in the proto-Romance vernacular, Charles severed the veil of Latin that had long divided those who commanded from those who obeyed. The soldiers' faith—their conviction that the oath constrained their lord as surely as it bound them—rested upon intelligibility. An oath delivered in Latin is a trial among the learned; an oath in the common speech is a trial among all who bear witness. The chronicles preserve this distinction, though they do not invariably fathom its weight.

The conventions of feudal trial required that every instrument of war receive consecration through faith. The sword received the priest's blessing; the shield bore the cross; the standard carried forth a saint's likeness. Yet the tongue proved the most hallowed weapon of all, for language is the very substance through which faith passes from man to man. The oath at Strasbourg constituted a linguistic consecration: by speaking in the vernacular, Charles proclaimed that this tongue possessed sanctity sufficient to bear the burden of political covenant.

The office of the chronicle is to preserve such moments without embellishment, yet this duty proves impossible to discharge with perfect fidelity. Nithard's history is partisan in character: he wrote as Charles's adherent, and his record of the oath in both Frankish and Romance serves his patron's political design. The faith that Nithard's chronicle offers neutral testimony is itself a wager—the reader stakes his trust that the partisan witness speaks truth despite his allegiance, that the chronicler who bears the sword may yet prove a faithful scribe.

The terms of the oath and the terms of the chronicle are one: the demand that language must serve an end beyond mere utterance, that the tongue must pierce, that the trial must be played for stakes that signify.
