# The Reckoning Before Battle

War begins with enumeration. Before Fontenoy, the commanders took their tally: how many blades, how many shields, how many mounted men? The counting came before conviction—before any man dared believe in victory, he must first number his resources. The vellum dispatches that survive from the Carolingian campaigns overflow with such reckonings: rosters of soldiers, inventories of provisions, calculations of marching days and leagues.

The discipline of warfare submits to arithmetic necessity. One blade demands one bearer; one horse consumes three days' fodder; one siege engine requires forty men's labor. These figures are inscribed upon vellum with the same reverence that scribes lavish upon Scripture, for the commander's confidence rests upon accurate reckoning. War is lost when the numbers deceive.

The parchment orders of battle observe conventions as rigid as any scribal practice. The dispatch must enumerate forces, reckon distances, and declare requirements: strike at dawn, hold the left wing, count the enemy's blades before engagement. Every military manuscript is arithmetic wagered against the hope that numbers can foretell outcomes.

Yet the sword introduces what cannot be reckoned. No figure accounts for courage, for terror, for the instant when a soldier's nerve shatters and he casts down his weapon and flees. Battle is enumeration interrupted by the innumerable. The vellum records the design; the field obliterates it. The commander who tallies his forces before engagement discovers, after the fighting, that the numbers have transformed in ways no reckoning could foresee.

The warrior's conviction is conviction in discipline: that the rigor of combat, the formation's geometry, the art of strategy will impose order upon the chaos of blades. The parchment preserving the battle plan is a manuscript of aspiration—a belief that number can govern the ungovernable, that war may be won by whoever calculates most shrewdly.
