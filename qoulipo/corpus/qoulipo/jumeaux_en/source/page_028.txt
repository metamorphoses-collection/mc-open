Maréchal's weapon was the vulgar tongue. While his fellows taught in the solemn speech of the French academy, the professor spoke the language of the street—direct, combative, sometimes impious. His seminar was combat: the students formed his company, the chronicle was his field, and the blade he wielded was the power to cleave pretension with a single phrase of plain speaking.

The wager was linguistic: Maréchal wagered that familiar speech could convey learned matters more forcefully than formal language. The history of this wager reaches back to Nithard: the Carolingian chronicler who chose to record a vulgar oath within a Latin chronicle, thus demonstrating that the sword-tongue of soldiers could dwell alongside the school-tongue of monks. Professor Maréchal was heir to Nithard—a scholar who wagered that the people's language could bear the burden of scholarship.

The lesson concerned combat and speech. Maréchal taught that every chronicle is a dispatch from war, and every dispatch from war is written in a language that betrays the dispatcher's allegiance. Nithard wrote as Charles's man; his history is a blade drawn to defend one brother against another. The vulgar oath—recorded in Frankish and Romance—was itself a weapon: by demonstrating that soldiers understood their lord's words, the chronicle proved that the army's loyalty was real, not merely ceremonial.

The professor's pedagogy was adversarial. He attacked his students' assumptions with the blade of Socratic method: What do you mean by "authentic"? How do you know the chronicle is accurate? In what language was the original composed, and does the rendering betray or preserve? These questions were weapons, and the seminar was a battlefield where the stake was intellectual survival.

Maréchal's own chronicle—the history of his teaching—is written in the vulgar tongue of affection: the language of a professor who loved his students enough to fight them, blade in hand, lesson after lesson.
