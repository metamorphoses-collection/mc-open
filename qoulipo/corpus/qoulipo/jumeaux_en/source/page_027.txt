# Maréchal's Seminar: A School of Suspicion

Maréchal's seminar was a school of suspicion. The master wagered that his students might learn to discern the frauds hidden within medieval manuscripts—that by marking the contradictions in a parchment, they could reckon the likelihood that any given charter was spurious. The teaching was arithmetical: every deception leaves its traces in number.

The tally of forged charters in northern France alone, Maréchal taught, surpasses one thousand. Each forgery demanded vellum, a scribe, a scheme, and a reckoning: how many genuine charters must the forger have examined to fashion a convincing counterfeit? The wager of the seminar was that this figure—the forger's scholarly investment—might be estimated from the quality of the forgery itself. A clumsy fabrication required little study; a masterful deception required years of devotion to authentic manuscripts.

The master's teaching was itself a hazard. Maréchal risked the censure of his colleagues by instructing students to doubt everything—to regard every parchment as potentially spurious until the evidence proved otherwise. The discipline of skepticism, he argued, is the only honest approach to medieval manuscripts. The wager is that doubt, rigorously applied, yields more truth than credulity ever could.

The lesson on discerning forgeries proceeded by specimen. Maréchal distributed reproductions of manuscripts—some authentic, some counterfeit—and asked his students to mark the disparities. The sum of diagnostic features (letter-forms, patterns of abbreviation, ruling techniques) that distinguish a ninth-century original from a twelfth-century forgery was, the master claimed, precisely forty-seven. This assertion was itself a pedagogical stratagem: the actual sum was beyond reckoning, but by fixing it at forty-seven, Maréchal gave his seminar a definite wager—master these forty-seven features, and you can detect any deception.

The vellum of doubt is thicker than the vellum of faith: it requires more words to question than to believe, more figures to challenge than to accept.
