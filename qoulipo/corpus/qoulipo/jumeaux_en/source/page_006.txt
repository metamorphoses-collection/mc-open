The manuscript came to the monastery wrapped in a wager. Brother Anselm staked his standing among his brethren that the vellum was true—a copy of Nithard's chronicle, sole witness to the Strasbourg Oaths. The hazard was considerable: should the manuscript prove genuine, the monastery's claim to shelter a Carolingian prince's relics would stand confirmed. Should it prove false, the deception that had upheld the house's eminence for three centuries would fall to ruin.

The art of authentication observes immutable principles. First, scrutinize the vellum: does the preparation accord with ninth-century practice? Number the prickings, measure the ruled lines. A false manuscript reveals itself through details belonging to a later age—a manner of script unknown in the purported period, a binding wrought by techniques not discovered until the twelfth century. The history of a forgery is itself a chronicle deserving preservation.

Anselm hazarded his name upon this wager. Twenty years he had devoted to the study of manuscripts, and his pledge was not rashness but reckoning: the vellum's lineage included a succession of custodians documented in three distinct chronicles, each independent. Yet the doubters—Maréchal foremost among them—observed that these three records might themselves belong to the scheme. A conspiracy concealing a conspiracy: forged chronicles vouching for forged vellum.

The conditions of the trial were these: should the manuscript fail any among five examinations—analysis of the script, determination by carbon, composition of the ink, preparation of the vellum, accordance of the text with known sources—the wager was forfeit. Anselm's pledge endured through four trials. The fifth—accordance of text—disclosed a passage appearing in no other record. Was this proof of authenticity, Nithard setting down something singular, or proof of falsification, the forger inventing a scene entire?

The chronicle remains disputed. The vellum rests in a vault where neither heat nor damp may enter. The conspiracy has outlived the conspirators. And the trial continues: every new scholar who examines the manuscript places a wager of his own.
