The lesson inscribed by the blade finds its record upon vellum in a language the departed shall not comprehend. Following the carnage at Fontenoy, the brethren of Saint-Riquier gathered such manuscripts as the tumult of battle had dispersed—psalters and books of hours, a chronicle left incomplete—and entered them in their catalogues beside the weaponry forsaken upon the field. Every sword bore its history; every sheet of vellum bore a conviction that such histories possessed significance.

The master in the monastic school instructed his charges that common speech might serve as armament. In the strife between Charles and Lothar, the tongue of command determined which men should survive and which perish: cries given in the Frankish dialect reached Frankish warriors; those in the nascent Romance reached Romance warriors. The tongue itself became a blade, and the oath sworn at Strasbourg—rendered in both languages—stood as a consummate demonstration of linguistic combat. The vellum upon which that oath was inscribed became itself an instrument in the struggle for rightful authority.

In his later years, Maréchal would impart this same understanding to those assembled in his seminar: that manuscripts constitute weaponry in conflicts waged across generations. Each folio represents an engagement between the conviction of the scribe and the scepticism of the reader. Each copy of a text strikes a blow against oblivion. The master transmits the vellum from one generation to the next, and each generation must determine whether to place credence in the language it bears or to permit that tongue to perish.

The warrior's faith differs from the scholar's. The soldier trusts that his blade shall preserve him; the monk trusts that his manuscript shall ensure his survival. Both hazard their hope upon a medium—whether steel or vellum—and both comprehend that the medium is perishable. Blades corrode. Scrolls are consumed by flame. The sole lesson that endures is that which a living tongue carries, passed from master to pupil in an unbroken succession requiring neither manuscript nor weapon, only the conviction that the language itself merits preservation.

The discipline of the sword and the discipline of the manuscript find their confluence in the oath: a spoken tongue committed to vellum, a living language preserved in the skin of the dead.
