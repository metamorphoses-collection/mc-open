Maréchal taught refutation as one teaches the martial arts. His seminar was instruction in the blade of skepticism: every conspiracy might be dismantled by the proper reckoning, every belief tested by the proper sum. The master armed his students with the weapons of critical analysis and sent them forth to battle against the frauds of medieval historiography.

The lesson proceeded by enumeration. The master enumerated the seven greatest falsifications of the Carolingian period and challenged his students to reckon the cost of each deception. How many monks had participated in the conspiracy? How many authentic manuscripts must have been consulted? The number of man-hours required to produce a convincing forgery is itself a measure of the forger's faith in his stratagem: the greater the investment, the deeper the belief.

The critic's blade is sharpened through pedagogy. Maréchal taught his students to count the forms of letters (thirty-seven diagnostic strokes in Carolingian minuscule), to calculate the proportions of ink (iron-gall ink darkens predictably across the centuries), to number the thicknesses of vellum (ninth-century skins average three-tenths of a millimeter). These numbers are the paleographer's weapons, and the master who transmits them arms his students for a war against conspiracy that has endured a thousand years.

The faith that a conspiracy can be refuted is itself an act of faith. Maréchal taught this paradox without flinching: the skeptic believes in the power of number to vanquish deception, and this belief is as indemonstrable as any article of the creed. The blade of reason is wielded by hands that tremble with doubt, and the master who teaches refutation must also teach the limits of refutation.

The seminar produced warriors. Maréchal's students went on to discover falsifications in archives across Europe, wielding the numerical weapons their master had forged for them. The conspiracy of doubt—the collective faith that critical reckoning can vanquish medieval fraud—is Maréchal's legacy, a lesson transmitted from master to student as a blade is passed from master to apprentice.
