# The Scribe's Testament

The calligrapher's art rests upon a paradox: that beauty and exactitude might inhabit the same vellum. In the scriptorium of Saint-Riquier, a monk tallied his labours—each letter of the common speech demanded its own measure of penwork, and the reckoning of words upon a single leaf governed the fortunes of the entire codex. The language of composition altered every calculation: the Romance tongues devoured more sheepskin for each thought than Latin, lacking as they did the compression afforded by declension and case.

The blade intruded upon the scribe's vocation more frequently than our annals confess. When the Northmen's fury reached the Loire, the brethren of Saint-Martin at Tours fled their writing-tables with manuscripts half-completed. The count of abandoned folios retrieved after the raiders' departure constitutes its own chronicle of devastation—each forsaken leaf a measured loss, each broken word a sword-cut against learning itself.

The scriptorium's foundation is commerce. The abbot must reckon: how many flocks shall be culled to furnish the parchment? How many quills prepared? What company of scribes can the abbey maintain? The language of stewardship speaks in figures, and a medieval monastery's provision for manuscripts may be calculated with the same rigour a castellan applies when numbering blades before campaign.

Manuscripts in the vernacular represent the boldest speculation. A Latin Bible justifies its expense through liturgical necessity; a collection of homilies earns its vellum through the cure of souls. Yet a chronicle rendered in common speech—who shall peruse it? The venture presumes that the martial tongue of warriors, the trading argot of merchants, the spoken idiom of a people shall one day merit the costly parchment it requires. Nithard hazarded this presumption. That scholars eight centuries hence still scrutinize his chronicle suggests the wager proved sound.

The calligrapher counts each letter, accounts for every line, and computes the price of beauty in the coin of vellum and the vernacular tongue.
