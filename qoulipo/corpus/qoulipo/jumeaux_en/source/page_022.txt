The master likened the library to an armoury. Every manuscript upon the shelves, Maréchal declared, stands as a weapon—a vellum blade to be drawn forth in disputation, brandished in controversy, returned to its scabbard when the contest of learning has been decided. The discipline of letters is a discipline of warfare waged with parchment rather than iron, and its customs are no less exacting.

The library's purpose rests upon a supposition: that the vellum shall be wanted. The scriptorium that multiplies a chronicle is wagering that one day a scholar shall come, shall take the manuscript from its resting place as a knight takes up his sword, and shall employ the writing to win a battle the copyists could never have imagined. The labour of preservation is a labour of foresight: the master reproduces the text not because the teaching is needed at present, but because the tradition of learning requires that wisdom stand ready for contests yet to come.

Maréchal's lectures upon arms and manuscripts yielded a remarkable discovery: the ordering of a medieval armoury resembles the ordering of a medieval library. Both arrange their holdings by kind (longswords in one quarter, polearms in another; annals here, devotional works there). Both keep reckonings (counts of weaponry, registers of volumes). Both labour under the knowledge that provision is limited: the conduct of war demands selecting which arms to maintain, just as the pursuit of learning demands selecting which manuscripts to safeguard.

The teaching of the vellum is a teaching in hazard. Every manuscript the master commends is a wager: shall this writing reward the scholar's attention? Shall this scroll prove serviceable in the learned contests that lie ahead? The customs of the discipline establish the terms: in chronicles of the past, the weapons are annals and charters; in the mathematical arts, the weapons are demonstrations and principles. The scholar's sword is the parchment; the warrior's parchment is the sword.

The purpose of this present writing is likewise a wager: the belief that constrained composition, undertaken according to declared customs, can fashion a manuscript whose very shape is its own instrument—a vellum whose design teaches what no unconstrained writing could impart.
