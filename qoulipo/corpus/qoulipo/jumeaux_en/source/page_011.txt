The chronicle records a conspiracy against faith. In the year 864, the monks of Saint-Medard's abbey set forth a parchment purporting to preserve the confession of the Emperor Lothar—a document wherein the vanquished sovereign was made to acknowledge his war against his brothers as a sin against God. The forgery was accomplished with great cunning: the script bore all the marks of ninth-century chancery hands, the vellum had been aged with proper artifice, and the confession adhered so faithfully to the forms of penitential literature that three bishops received it as genuine.

The faith required to sustain a conspiracy surpasses the faith required to sustain a religion. The forger must hold faith in his forgery with absolute conviction; the merest shadow of doubt corrupts the parchment with signs that a discerning eye may perceive. The chronicle of medieval fraud thus becomes a record of failed belief: every forgery that was at length detected reveals some moment wherein the fabricator's faith in his own contrivance faltered.

The rules were established by the monks themselves. A genuine manuscript obeys certain constraints which a forgery must replicate: the proportion of abbreviations to complete words, the pattern of corrections and alterations, the natural rhythm of the scribal hand. These are the rules of a game played across the centuries by generations of copyists, and the conspiracy against authenticity is but the conspiracy of one who has learned the rules sufficiently well to subvert them.

Maréchal studied this chronicle with the pleasure of a detective pursuing his quarry. He compiled the fraudulent parchments of northern France, recording each specimen in a ledger he termed his "anti-chronicle"—a catalogue of deceptions which, taken together, disclosed a deeper truth concerning the faith that communities required for their survival. Every monastery stood in need of a relic; every relic demanded a parchment to authenticate it; every parchment necessitated a conspiracy to produce it and a collective faith to sustain it.

The practice of authentication is faith pursued in reverse: where faith inquires "do you believe?", authentication demands "can you prove?" The chronicle of proof resides in the parchment itself, and the conspiracy lies in the very assertion that proof is attainable.
