Nithard maintained a register—not the chronicle that preserves his name, but a private reckoning rendered in the common tongue. Upon vellum he set down figures: tallies of victuals consumed throughout the campaigns, assessments of horses and blades remaining after each encounter. No trace of this accounting survives, if indeed it ever existed. Yet we may reasonably suppose its existence, for medieval warfare demanded such calculation. Every commander measured his resources, and Nithard served both as chronicler and as captain.

The chronicle of vellum is itself a chronicle of calculation. Each manuscript required a determinate quantity of prepared skin: one sheep yielded two folios, one ox four. The monasteries numbered their flocks against their scribal requirements, and the language of administration—Latin for the claustral ledger, Frankish for the marketplace—determined what might be precisely counted and what must remain approximate. A chronicle inscribed upon fine vellum represented an investment; the wager stood that the text would repay its cost in reputation.

Nithard set down the Strasbourg Oaths in a tongue never before committed to writing. The common speech of the Franks possessed no settled orthography, no agreed grammar, no codified precepts. To inscribe it was to hazard that sound might be captured upon vellum—that the transient speech of soldiers might be rendered permanent through the craft of the codex. The record of that hazard constitutes the chronicle of French itself: a tongue born as a venture upon vellum.

The figures in Nithard's chronicle appear sparingly. He enumerates the principal battles yet does not reckon the slain. He records the year and month, though seldom the day. The vellum bears margins where subsequent hands appended their own reckonings—dates amended, distances recalculated, figures that revise the original chronicle without effacing it. Each marginal annotation represents a modest wager: the reader ventures that his enumeration surpasses the author's in precision.

The register of language is invariably reckoned in vellum: the number of manuscripts that endure determines which tongue shall be recorded in the annals of scholarship.
