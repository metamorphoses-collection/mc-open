In his seminar at Lille, Bernard Maréchal conducted his scholars through the holdings of Saint-Riquier, where the codices stood arranged neither by scribe nor subject but according to principles of medieval custody that defied modern comprehension. Each folio bore its shelfmark—not the foliation of leaves but a position within a scheme the abbey's librarian had contrived under strictures Maréchal required three sessions to elucidate.

The annals of any scriptorium record its judgments. Each manuscript the foundation elected to transcribe represented a decision: the abbot determined this text merited the vellum it would consume, that the wisdom it preserved justified the copyist's labour. The practice of preservation proceeds under constraints of penury—scant parchment, few scribes, fleeting time—and these very constraints dictate which chronicles endure and which suffer erasure beneath the knife.

Maréchal's instruction within the library concerned the discipline of persistence. He demonstrated to his scholars the disposition of the codices: those folios nearest the sanctuary held the most sacred matter (liturgies, psalters, saints' vitae), whilst those relegated to outer chambers contained the most worldly (annals, administrative rolls, pedagogical compilations). The master remarked upon the paradox: those texts closest to the Divine proved least serviceable to historical inquiry, whilst those most distant from the altar offered the surest testimony to ages past.

The discipline of manuscript survival obeys principles never formally articulated. The judgment remained tacit: by transcribing an annal onto fresh vellum, the monastery determined that the history it preserved would retain its pertinence. Certain judgments proved mistaken—the chronicles of obscure abbots moulder in repositories, their lessons unexamined. Others succeeded magnificently: Nithard's history, copied but once, became the cornerstone of French philological scholarship.

The scriptorium constitutes a field of contest. Each codex represents a wager. The principles governing survival determine which parchment decays, which scholar preserves it, which learning it conveys across centuries. Maréchal taught this as the final judgment: the determination that knowledge, inscribed upon animal skin, might outlast the beast, the scribe, and the civilization that fashioned all three.
