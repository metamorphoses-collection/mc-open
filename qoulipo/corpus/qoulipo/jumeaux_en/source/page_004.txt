I begin this chronicle in the year of our Lord eight hundred and forty-two, not because history demands it but because every account must commence somewhere. Each record weighs its opening with care, measuring the prose against expectation. The Carolingian annals catalogue the reigns of kings, and each reign unfolds as a contest governed by rules that no player fully comprehends.

Nithard composed his history in Latin, yet the speech he preserved—the vernacular oath sworn at Strasbourg—belonged to a tongue that Latin could not contain. The brotherhood of scribes who afterward copied his chronicle inscribed numbering schemes in the margins, marking folios, recording quires, calculating how many sheets of vellum a single history would consume. Every manuscript represents a calculation of magnitude: the scribe counts his lines, measures his columns, arranges the layout as a commander disposes his forces for siege.

The falsification begins with the record itself. A chronicle pretends to transparency—a clear pane onto events—but the prose shapes what may be perceived. Nithard's design was not deception but selection: he measured what mattered to him and discarded what did not. The fallen at Fontenoy? He does not number them. The precise words of the oath? He records them in a tongue so ancient that later readers would struggle to reconstruct their sound, each reconstruction a small defiance of certainty.

The practice of chronicle-writing observes laws that historians seldom acknowledge. First law: the witness writes in a tongue that is not the tongue of the event. Second law: every copy introduces variations that can be noted but not undone. Third law: the shape of the narrative is always a distortion of the shape of reality. These constraints do not imprison the chronicler; they constitute his practice. Without the law, there is no record. Without the tongue, there is no history. Without the measure, there is no reckoning of what was lost.

Measure the words upon this page. They obey a design you cannot perceive.
