The oath is a delicate architecture: a weaving of words held fast by faith and sealed by the blade. At Strasbourg, the chosen tongues—proto-Romance, proto-Frankish—were no accident: Charles reckoned how many soldiers understood each language and selected the vernacular that would reach the most ears. The design was not to deceive but to convince: Charles needed his brother's men to hear the words, to count themselves among the bound, to understand that the blade of alliance cuts deeper than the blade of separation.

The profusion of oaths sworn in the Carolingian years tells its own story: the more pledges a ruler made, the less each one carried weight. The erosion of the oath follows a cruel pattern: each new promise diminishes all that came before, and the tongue that speaks too many vows becomes the tongue that no one heeds. The blade enforces what repetition erodes—when words falter, steel answers.

The faith that holds an oath in place is measured. The soldier reasons: if my lord honors this pledge, my foes grow fewer; if he breaks it, they multiply. The weaving of alliance is a weaving of reckoning—each man counts the others, tallies the blades at hand, and weighs whether the league is worth the hazard. The tongue of the oath merely gives form to what cold assessment already decides.

The design of the Carolingian civil war was itself a reckoning. Lothar joined with Pepin against Charles and Louis; Charles joined with Louis against Lothar and Pepin. The possible leagues among four brothers can be enumerated, and the faith needed to sustain any one diminishes with each alternative in view. The blade settles what the tongue cannot broker and what cold reason cannot perfect.

The secret of the oath is that it claims eternity when it is, by nature, fleeting. The tongue speaks of forever; faith endures a season; the blade marks the span the alliance will last.
