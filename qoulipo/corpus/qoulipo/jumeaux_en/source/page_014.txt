The master devised a conspiracy. Maréchal's seminar transformed into a theatre of deception: each pupil received a tongue—Old French, Latin, Picard, Occitan—and was charged with fabricating a medieval document so persuasive that their fellows could not unmask the imposture. The stakes were academic: the most accomplished forger would claim the highest distinction. The design was pedagogical: in learning to counterfeit, one learns to discern counterfeits.

Conspiracy speaks always in the vernacular. Plotters do not contrive in the language of authority; they converse in cipher, in dialect, in the cant of the initiated. Maréchal taught this precept through demonstration: he revealed to his pupils how the monks of Saint-Médard had prosecuted their campaign of forgery in a private idiom that left no mark upon the official chronicle. The language of the plot remained invisible precisely because it was uttered, not inscribed.

The art of forgery observes laws which the master enumerated with relish. First: the vocabulary must answer to the period. Second: the grammar must bear deliberate archaisms. Third: the vellum must be artificially aged. Fourth: the conspiracy must furnish a plausible provenance—a narrative concerning how the document came to be "discovered." Fifth: the wager stands that the fraud shall survive its initial scrutiny. These constraints, Maréchal maintained, are identical to those governing any creative endeavour: they circumscribe the player's liberties and thereby compel invention.

The lesson yielded remarkable fruit. One pupil fabricated a charter in Old Picard so convincing it deceived a visiting scholar for twenty minutes. The conspiracy was exposed only when the master indicated an anachronistic orthography—a single letter that betrayed the tongue of a modern hand. The wager was forfeit, yet the game was won: the pupil had acquired more knowledge of medieval vernacular through forgery than through a twelvemonth of lectures.

Maréchal's design remained constant: hazard failure to gain understanding. The conspiratorial tongue of the schoolroom was the very language of learning itself.
