# Disputationes Wittgensteinianae

*Sex quaestiones de Tractato Logico-Philosophico — standard versus resolute readings.*

> A pilot benchmark: six contested propositions of the Tractatus, each defended on both
> the standard metaphysical reading (Hacker, Glock, Anscombe) and the resolute reading
> (Diamond, Conant, Goldfarb, Crary, Read). The disagreement is real and 25 years old;
> the author declines to resolve it. Form: Aquinas-style Sic et Non, Latin titling, English body — *clin d'œil* to Wittgenstein's own Latin-titled vernacular treatise.

---

**¶0001 Proem.** *De duobus modis legendi Tractatum.* Two interpretive traditions divide
Wittgenstein scholarship. The *standard reading* (Hacker, Pears, Glock, Anscombe) takes
the Tractatus to advance substantive theses about logic, language, ontology, ethics, and
the mystical — theses that may turn out, by the book's own lights at 6.54, to be
strictly speaking nonsense, but which carry real philosophical content along the way.
The *resolute reading* (Diamond, Conant, Goldfarb, Kremer, Crary, Read) holds that the
Tractarian propositions are *plain* nonsense, in the strict sense that they say nothing
at all; the book's purpose is therapeutic, to bring the reader to recognise their own
attempt to make sense of metaphysical pseudo-questions, and to set those attempts down.
The propositions are not steps in an argument; they are rungs of a ladder that loses
its rungs the moment one tries to step on them. Six propositions follow on which the
two readings most sharply diverge.

---

**¶0002 Praefatio I.** *De silentio et de eo quod ostendit se.* The block addresses
the cluster of propositions through which the Tractatus speaks about what cannot be
spoken: 4.116 ("everything that can be thought clearly..."), 6.522 (the mystical
shows itself), 6.53 (the right method of philosophy), 6.54 (the ladder), 7 (one must
be silent), and the Preface ("what can be said at all..."). On the standard reading,
each is a substantive doctrine about the limits of language; on the resolute reading,
each enacts its own self-cancellation.

---

## Quaestio I — TLP 4.116

**¶0003 Quaestio I — Sic.** *Everything that can be thought at all can be thought clearly; everything that can be put into words can be put clearly* — this expresses the **principle of clarity**, the substantive Wittgensteinian doctrine that language has a determinate logical form and that any genuine thought admits, in principle, a fully perspicuous formulation. The thesis is the bridge between the picture theory and the doctrine of silence: clarity is what *can* be said; everything else falls outside language. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*, ch. 1; *Analytical Commentary* vol. 1) reads this as Wittgenstein's claim that ideal logical analysis terminates, that there is a final, transparent form for any expressible content. Pears (*The False Prison*) takes 4.116 as the formal counterpart of the ethics of clarity that Wittgenstein took from Loos and Karl Kraus: what is shown by a clear proposition is its truth-conditions; what is shown by an unclear proposition is the speaker's confusion. The proposition is doctrinal, and substantive.

**¶0004 Quaestio I — Non.** 4.116 is not a thesis about language but an *invitation* — to attempt to put what one might call "philosophy" into clear words, and to find, in the attempt, that one has either said something falsifiable by physics or said nothing at all. Diamond (*The Realistic Spirit*, "Throwing Away the Ladder") argues that the standard reading turns Wittgenstein into a transcendental linguist who delineates the boundaries of sense from outside language, exactly the position the Tractatus is engineered to make incoherent. There is no position from which one can speak of "what can be put into words" *as a class*; the proposition gestures at a putative boundary while quietly emptying both sides of content. Conant ("The Method of the Tractatus") treats 4.116 as a particularly pure case of nonsense that masquerades as a substantive thesis. The reader is meant to find the proposition tempting, then to recognise the temptation as the trap.

---

## Quaestio II — TLP 6.522

**¶0005 Quaestio II — Sic.** *There is indeed the inexpressible. This shows itself; it is the mystical.* — this is the **mysticism thesis** of the Tractatus, the substantive doctrine that there exists a realm of value, ethics, and the absolute that cannot be expressed in propositions but is *shown* in the structure of expressing them. Anscombe (*An Introduction to Wittgenstein's Tractatus*, ch. 13) reads 6.522 as committed to a genuine distinction between saying and showing, with the inexpressible occupying a definite metaphysical position — what Wittgenstein calls "the mystical" — accessible only through the right kind of attention to facts, not through assertion. McGuinness ("The Mysticism of the Tractatus") traces the proposition to William James, Tolstoy, and Wittgenstein's own wartime religious experience; it expresses a positive doctrine about the existence and structure of the ineffable.

**¶0006 Quaestio II — Non.** The proposition is meant to dissolve, not to assert. Conant ("Mysticism and Nonsense in the Tractatus") and Diamond ("Ethics, Imagination and the Method of Wittgenstein's Tractatus") argue that 6.522 is a piece of *gesture-nonsense*: it appears to refer to "the inexpressible" as if that phrase had reference, only for the reader to recognise — once they ask what the inexpressible *is* — that the phrase has no content. There is no doctrine of the mystical in the Tractatus; the appearance of one is what the resolute reading calls the temptation of the book. Kremer ("The Purpose of Tractarian Nonsense") treats 6.522 as the most sustained example of the Tractatus's strategy: a proposition that *seems* to express a thought, designed precisely to lead the reader to notice that nothing has been expressed. The mystical *shows itself* in the failure of the proposition to mean anything determinate.

## Quaestio III — TLP 6.53

**¶0007 Quaestio III — Sic.** *The right method of philosophy would be this. To say nothing except what can be said, i.e. propositions of natural science, and then whenever someone else wished to say something metaphysical, to demonstrate to him that he had failed to give a meaning to certain signs in his propositions.* — this is the **demarcation thesis** of the Tractatus, the substantive Wittgensteinian programme that anticipates and grounds logical positivism. Hacker (*Wittgenstein's Place in Twentieth-Century Analytic Philosophy*, ch. 2) reads 6.53 as a doctrinal proposal: natural science is the totality of meaningful discourse; philosophy is the activity of unmasking metaphysical pseudo-propositions. Carnap's *Logical Syntax of Language* and Ayer's *Language, Truth, and Logic* both descend from this reading, and on it Wittgenstein endorses, with reservations, the Vienna Circle's programme.

**¶0008 Quaestio III — Non.** 6.53 is itself a piece of metaphysical pseudo-discourse it cannot consistently endorse. Conant ("Wittgenstein's Later Critique of the Tractatus") and Goldfarb ("Metaphysics and Nonsense") observe that to "say nothing except what can be said" presupposes that one stands outside language with a god's-eye view of what *can be said* — exactly the position 4.116 and 6.54 declare impossible. The proposition is a transparent self-defeating remark, and the resolute reader takes Wittgenstein to have intended it as such. The right method of philosophy, on this reading, is not demarcation (which presumes a vantage from which the demarcation can be drawn) but *therapy*: helping the philosopher recognise that they have failed to give meaning to their own propositions. There is no positivist programme in the Tractatus; there is a programme of self-recognition.

---

## Quaestio IV — TLP 6.54 (the ladder)

**¶0009 Quaestio IV — Sic.** *My propositions serve as elucidations in the following way: anyone who understands me eventually recognises them as nonsensical, when he has used them — as steps — to climb out beyond them. (He must, so to speak, throw away the ladder after he has climbed up it.) He must transcend these propositions, and then he will see the world aright.* — On the standard reading, this is the **doctrine of transcendence**: the Tractarian propositions, though strictly nonsensical by their own criterion, nonetheless *do something* — they elucidate, in a way ordinary propositions cannot, the structure of language and world. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*, ch. 1) and Pears (*The False Prison*, vol. 1) read the ladder metaphor as Wittgenstein's frank admission that his book breaks its own rules in service of a higher pedagogical end; the reader climbs to a vantage from which the climb is seen as illegitimate, but that vantage was the destination all along. Anscombe defends this as Wittgenstein's *resolute paradox*: meaningful nonsense that elucidates by failing.

**¶0010 Quaestio IV — Non.** There is no vantage at the top of the ladder. Diamond ("Throwing Away the Ladder") argues — in the essay that gives the resolute reading its name — that there is no such thing as "meaningful nonsense" in the Tractatus: nonsense is nonsense, full stop, and the reader who *understands* Wittgenstein understands that there was no proposition there to begin with. The "throwing away" is total: not just the propositions but the temptation to think the propositions said something, that the climb yielded any view, that there is a world to "see aright". Conant ("The Method of the Tractatus") frames this as the difference between the *substantial* and the *austere* conceptions of nonsense: the standard reader thinks Tractarian nonsense is "deep" or "illuminating"; the resolute reader holds that there is only one kind of nonsense, the ordinary kind, and that the book's task is to dissolve the wish for a deeper kind.

## Quaestio V — TLP 7

**¶0011 Quaestio V — Sic.** *Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.* — On the standard reading, this is the **doctrine of the unsayable**: the final proposition of the Tractatus expresses the substantive metaphysical thesis that there exists a realm of value, ethics, religion, and the absolute that lies outside the limits of language, and the appropriate response to this realm is silence. McGuinness, Anscombe, and Hacker read 7 as the closing thesis of a complete philosophical system: language has limits, those limits coincide with the world (5.6), and what lies on the far side is the proper domain of silence rather than of false speech. The proposition has been read as endorsing apophatic theology (Kierkegaard, Eckhart), Schopenhauerian renunciation, and the ethical primacy of action over assertion.

**¶0012 Quaestio V — Non.** 7 says nothing — and the reader who has worked through the preceding 525 propositions is meant to recognise this. Conant ("Mysticism and Nonsense") observes that "whereof one cannot speak" appears to refer to a definite *somewhere* about which one cannot speak; but once one tries to specify what that somewhere is, the reference collapses. There is no domain of the unsayable to be silent about; there is only the recognition that certain forms of words, including 7 itself, lack content. Crary ("Wittgenstein's Philosophy in Relation to Political Thought") and Read ("Throwing Away 'the Bedrock'") press the point that 7 enacts what the rest of the book has been preparing the reader for: not the silence of someone who has glimpsed an unutterable truth, but the silence of someone who has stopped trying to utter pseudo-truths. The proposition is the book's full stop, not its conclusion.

## Quaestio VI — Preface (the work's aim)

**¶0013 Quaestio VI — Sic.** *The book will perhaps only be understood by those who have themselves already thought the thoughts which are expressed in it — or similar thoughts. It is therefore not a textbook. Its purpose would be achieved if it gave pleasure to one person who read and understood it. The book deals with the problems of philosophy and shows, as I believe, that the method of formulating these problems rests on the misunderstanding of the logic of our language. Its whole meaning could be summed up somewhat as follows: What can be said at all can be said clearly; and whereof one cannot speak thereof one must be silent.* — On the standard reading, the Preface frames the Tractatus as a **completed philosophical work** that solves the problems of philosophy by exhibiting their logical-grammatical confusion. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*, ch. 1) treats the Preface as the author's authoritative gloss on his own substantive doctrine. The thoughts the book expresses are the ones the reader must have anticipated to follow Wittgenstein; *expressed* implies content; the work is a sustained philosophical argument with a determinate thesis.

**¶0014 Quaestio VI — Non.** The Preface enacts the same self-cancelling structure as the body of the work. Diamond and Conant note that "the thoughts which are expressed in it" is itself a Tractarian sentence — and by the book's own criteria, the Tractarian sentences express no thoughts at all. The Preface is part of the ladder; it is the first rung the reader must throw away. Goldfarb ("Das Überwinden") argues that Wittgenstein's gesture toward "the problems of philosophy" being dissolved by attention to the logic of language is not a doctrine but a programme of *self-overcoming* (*das Überwinden*): the reader is invited to commit to thoughts they will later be brought to recognise as non-thoughts, and the act of recognition *is* what the book aims to produce. The Preface promises a doctrine in order to take that promise back.

**¶0015 Transitio.** *Sex propositiones, duodecim explicationes, una controversia.* The six propositions traverse the four most contested moments of the Tractatus's self-presentation: 4.116 (clarity), 6.522 (the mystical), 6.53 (philosophical method), 6.54 (the ladder), 7 (silence), and the Preface. The Sic and Non paragraphs draw on Hacker, Pears, McGuinness, Anscombe for the standard side; Diamond, Conant, Goldfarb, Kremer, Crary, Read for the resolute side. The disagreement is the central one in Tractatus studies post-1995; the Wittgensteinian *clin d'œil* in our titling is meant in the spirit of the *Tractatus* itself, whose own Latin title (chosen by Moore in homage to Spinoza's *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus*) wears a sober Latin coat over a vernacular German body. We engineer the antithesis pairs in English so that multilingual sentence embeddings (e5) and visual–text embeddings (CLIP, for the multimodal extension) place each \emph{Sic} and \emph{Non} as the closest-cosine pair within its quaestio.
