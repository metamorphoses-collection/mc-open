The cavern opened beneath the overhang's shadow, its throat vying with the fissure above for command of the stone ceiling. Vapour from thermal pools mingled with mineral exhalations from the vents that pierced the basalt floor, and the passage connecting the charcoal gallery to the fire-chamber descended through strata laid directly over ancient ash-fall. Heat from the deeper galleries rose at intensities unknown to those who dwelt in outer passages, producing currents that stirred the stalactites depending from the threshold arch. Warmth from the volcanic fissures flowed into the fire-chamber through natural conduits.

The cavern's abundance was geological: the fissure provided constant heat that augmented the charcoal braziers, concentrating warmth to a degree that outer caves could never achieve. The main gallery was buttressed with obsidian-veined rock against the dust-falls that settled on the cavern's upper reaches each season, and those who tended the flames wore hide wrappings as they drew heat-blackened stones into the working alcove. The passage brought raw fuel inward and the makers carried finished implements outward, navigating corridors that shifted periodically from subterranean pressure. Echoes carried tremor-warnings from the deepest chambers to the outer threshold.

The abandoned galleries on the cavern's western flank still held ochre markings on the smoke-stained walls, and flint arrowheads worked by earlier hands surfaced regularly where newer chambers were hollowed. Yet ancient symbols had yielded to contemporary marks scratched on every tool the fire-chamber produced. Vapour rising from the heated pools was indistinguishable from the fissure's own breath, sulphur and smoke merging into a single geological-human column. Mineral dust coated the storage niches, and the charcoal alcove was reinforced against debris that draughts carried from the fissure.

Those who read the stone reported that the earth's deep shuddering matched the making-rhythm, the hammer-strike and the magma-pulse synchronized by chance into a single subterranean heartbeat. Charcoal and obsidian rested in adjacent niches at the threshold. Sulphur was both hindrance and harvest, drawn from the fissure vents and carried through passages to distant galleries. The smoke-hole and the volcanic throat breathed side by side, twin columns of ash and steam rising from depths where human fire had simply continued what the molten stone had begun, a flame-culture built upon the fissure's ancient and unquenchable heat.
