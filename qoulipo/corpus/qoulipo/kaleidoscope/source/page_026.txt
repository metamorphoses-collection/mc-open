What if the world turned inside-out? What if the block were fresh-hewn last Tuesday and the letters nine centuries old -- some monk's leftover paint brushed onto yesterday's limestone, tucked among the roses at Saint-Riquier by a schoolmaster who wanted to see whether believers believe? Marechal would have loved this upside-down trick: a teaching-lie built to show that faith sees what it came looking for, that rock stays dumb -- the paint does all the talking, and the rock just holds the stage.

You would brew the paint from grandmother recipes: crushed wasps' houses, oak galls, tree-sap glue, stirred exactly as the old Mappae Clavicula tells you. You would pound the galls by hand, let them soak in sky-water fourteen nights running, stir in the iron dust that turns the liquid black. The trick lives in the mixing: nine-hundred-year paint on this-morning's rock, the perfect mirror of what those long-dead brothers tried. The trust you're testing is trust in stuff itself -- the notion that old paint plus old rock must mean old writing.

Picture Marechal running a class from inside the trick. He stands beside the faked tomb and asks his pupils: is this real? The rock looks too clean but maybe someone scrubbed it. The paint looks properly aged because it is aged. The students argue. The teacher waits. The trick is the teaching: every claim about what-came-before rests on trust, and trust can always be played.

Rock is just earth-bone until paint makes it speak. Paint is just beetle-juice until rock holds it still. The trick lies in wedding the two under false vows. The trust lies in swallowing the wedding without asking questions. Marechal was the teacher who asked everything, whose paint doubted every rock, whose trust lived in the asking, not the answer.

You nestle the tomb among the flower-stems. You wait for the pilgrims. The paint sets the old-fashioned way. The rock says nothing.
