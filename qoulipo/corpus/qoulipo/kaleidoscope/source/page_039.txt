I write this chronicle on parchment pressed against a stone writing-desk that was once a Roman altar. The ink flows from a reed cut with the same technique the sword-maker uses to forge a blade: you split the shaft, shape the point, harden it in fire. The stone beneath my hand has supported Roman sacrifices, Frankish legal proceedings, and now my chronicle of the wars between my cousins. The parchment receives the ink; the ink records the sword; the sword has shaped the world that the chronicle describes; and the stone holds it all.

You must recognize that every chronicle rests upon foundations—the physical stability on which the parchment lies. Without the stone writing-desk, the parchment wrinkles and the ink bleeds into shapelessness. Without the sword, the chronicle has nothing to record: peace is silent, and only the sword creates the events that require the chronicler's ink. You understand this because you are both the swordsman and the scribe. Your hands have held both instruments. The stone has supported both activities.

Once upon a time, there was parchment made from sheepskin, gathered from flocks near Centula. There was ink of iron gall, dark as the blood seen on sword blades at Fontenoy. There was stone of gray limestone, quarried from the same beds that supplied the foundation stones of the monastery. Everything in the act of writing—the parchment, the ink, the stone—was local, drawn from the same landscape that the chronicle described. The sword alone came from elsewhere: it was Rhenish steel, forged in the foundries of the east, imported along the same trade routes that the empire's division would sever.

I dip the reed in ink and continue the chronicle. The sword rests beside me on the stone. The parchment unrolls before me, waiting for the next sentence. The foundation of my work is this: that the stone endures, that the ink marks, that the parchment preserves, and that the sword gives me something worth preserving. Without any one of these—without stone, ink, parchment, or sword—the chronicle cannot exist.

The reed scratches the skin. The ink darkens. The stone holds.
