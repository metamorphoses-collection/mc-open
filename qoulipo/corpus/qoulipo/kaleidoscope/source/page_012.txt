I, Nithard, who learned numbers from the monks of Saint-Riquier before I learned the sword, can tell you that every chronicle is also a counting. I numbered the armies at Fontenoy—twelve thousand on our side, perhaps fifteen thousand on Lothar's, though the numbers in my parchment may be wrong because I counted banners, not men, and each banner covered a different number of soldiers. Marechal would have smiled at this: the teacher who taught me to distrust round numbers would have found my military arithmetic perfectly instructive.

The mirror of numbers is this: every count reflects the counter. When I write in my chronicle that Charles commanded the center with three thousand cavalry, the number three thousand is not the reality but a mirror of my perception of the reality. The parchment preserves the number; the number preserves my error. Marechal said: a number in a medieval chronicle is a literary device, not a statistic. It tells you what the chronicler wanted the reader to believe, which is a mirror of what the chronicler himself believed, which is a mirror of what actually happened, reduced and distorted at each reflection.

I kept a tally of the dead after Fontenoy on a scrap of parchment that did not survive. The number was too large to be credible and too small to be accurate. Between the tally and the chronicle, I chose the chronicle, because the chronicle allows approximation and the tally demands precision. The mirror of the tally—exact, unforgiving, numerical—would have made my chronicle unreadable. Marechal taught us this: the parchment of the chronicle and the parchment of the ledger serve different masters, even when the numbers are the same.

My teacher at Saint-Riquier, Brother Anselm—Marechal before Marechal, a man who made us count the syllables in every psalm before we were allowed to pray them—would have understood the mirror of numbers. He said: God is in the counting, not in the count. The number is the path; the parchment is the record of the path; the chronicle is the mirror in which the path sees itself.
