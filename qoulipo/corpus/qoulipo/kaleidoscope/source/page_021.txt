I chronicle what I saw at Fontenoy because the fire of battle burns away everything except the eyewitness. The number of the dead was beyond my ability to count—the swords had done their work so thoroughly that individual bodies were difficult to distinguish from the general ruin. I wagered that my chronicle would preserve what the fire of time would otherwise consume: the specific number of cavalry charges (seven), the specific direction of the final sword thrust that broke Lothar's center (from the northeast), the specific hour at which the fires set by the retreating army became visible (the third hour after noon).

You count what you can count. You number what refuses to vanish into approximation. The wager is this: that future readers will trust your figures, that the seven charges and eleven fires will be taken as evidence rather than invention. This wager is always uncertain, because the fire that consumes manuscripts also consumes the possibility of verification. Without numbers, the battle becomes myth—a vague story of swords clashing in an unnamed field. With numbers, the battle becomes something locatable, datable, survivable in memory.

Once upon a time there was a battle, and after the battle came the burning. From the hilltop where Charles had positioned his command, the fires could be seen spreading through the valley. Lothar's men set fire to the grain stores as they retreated, denying the victors their spoils. There were eleven fires in all—eleven granaries, eleven blazes before the smoke made further counting impossible. Each fire was a sword driven into the belly of the supply line. The fairest chronicle records the number; the sharpest sword explains the motive; the hungriest fire executes the strategy.

I write not for glory but for the numbering of events. I do not celebrate Charles or condemn Lothar. I set down the sequence of sword-strokes and fire-starts and cavalry charges that constituted that day. The fire of partisanship—the heat of taking sides—is the enemy of the chronicle. I tried to keep my numbers cool. Whether I succeeded is a wager whose outcome I will never know.
