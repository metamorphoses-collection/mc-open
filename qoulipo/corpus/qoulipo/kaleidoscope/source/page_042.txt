I heard Marechal speak of the conspiracy in his own tongue—the rapid, allusive French of a man who had spent fifty years reading parchment and distrusting everything written on it. The fire in his voice was not anger but passion: the passion of a teacher who had discovered that the tongue of the medieval forger and the tongue of the modern scholar are the same tongue, spoken with different accents.

He held up a photocopied page of the Nithard parchment and said: this is a conspiracy between two fires. The first fire is the fire of the original event—the battle, the oath, the political crisis that compelled Nithard to write. The second fire is the fire of transmission—the passion of the copyists who preserved the text, each one a co-conspirator in the enterprise of historical memory. The tongue that speaks across the centuries is not Nithard's tongue; it is the tongue of the conspiracy of transmission, a parchment-fire that burns from scribe to scribe.

Marechal taught us to read the parchment as a conspiracy of tongues. Every manuscript is written in at least two tongues: the tongue of the author and the tongue of the copyist. Between them lies the fire of interpretation—the heat generated by the friction of two minds working on the same text. The conspiracy is that we read the result as a single tongue, when in fact it is a dialogue, a parchment duet, a fire of competing intentions.

The fire of Marechal's teaching was the fire of the vernacular—the tongue of plain speech, stripped of academic jargon, aimed directly at the student's understanding. He spoke French the way Nithard wrote proto-Romance: not because it was elegant but because it was effective. The parchment of his teaching was the photocopy; the fire was his voice; the tongue was French; and the conspiracy was that he made it all seem simple when in fact the complexity was immense.

The fire in his voice has not gone out. The tongue still speaks. The parchment endures.
