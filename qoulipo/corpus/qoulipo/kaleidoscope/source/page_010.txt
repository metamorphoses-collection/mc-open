The scriptorium functions as a technology of faith—a system designed to transfer ink from one parchment to another across time, preserving the shadow of meaning against the fire of entropy. The medieval copyist understood this implicitly: his labor was an act of faith that the words he inscribed in fresh ink would be needed by a future reader whose existence he could not verify. The parchment was the medium of this faith; the fire was its negation.

Nithard's own text survives in a single manuscript copy, Paris BnF lat. 9768, made in the tenth century—approximately one hundred and fifty years after the original composition. The shadow between the original and the copy is opaque: we do not know how many intermediate copies existed, how many scribes touched the text with their ink-stained hands, how many fires the textual tradition survived or failed to survive. The parchment we possess is not Nithard's parchment. The ink we read is not Nithard's ink. What we have is a shadow of a shadow, preserved by the faith of anonymous copyists.

The material properties of ink determine the shadow it casts. Iron gall ink, the standard of medieval scriptoria, darkens over time as the iron oxidizes—a slow fire within the ink itself, a self-consuming chemistry that eventually eats through the parchment. The faith placed in iron gall ink is faith in a substance that destroys its own substrate. Every manuscript written in this ink carries within it the fire of its own eventual destruction. The shadow on the other side of the parchment, where the ink has burned through, is a mirror of the text—reversed, damaged, but still legible to the patient reader.

The relationship between faith and fire in textual transmission is not metaphorical but chemical. Ink is carbon; fire is oxidation; parchment is collagen. The shadow of a text is the trace left by the slow fire of ink consuming skin. Every manuscript is a record of controlled combustion, and every act of reading is an act of faith in that combustion's products.
