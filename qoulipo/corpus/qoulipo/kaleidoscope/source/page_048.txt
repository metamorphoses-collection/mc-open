I played the game of mirrors with Marechal one afternoon in Lille. The rules were his invention: each player writes a sentence in ink on a piece of paper, then holds it up to a mirror. The other player must read the mirror image and respond with a sentence that makes sense both forward and in the mirror. The tongue must work in both directions. The game is won by the player whose sentence is most beautiful in its reflection.

Marechal wrote: *Le jeu de l'encre est le miroir de la langue.* The game of ink is the mirror of the tongue. In the mirror, the sentence read backwards, letter by letter, as a stream of consonants and vowels that made no sense in French but suggested, to a willing ear, a phrase in no known tongue—a mirror-language, an ink-language, a language that exists only in the game of reflection. The teacher laughed and said: the mirror creates a tongue that no one speaks, and the ink records a tongue that everyone reads. The game is in the gap.

I responded with a sentence that I have since lost—the ink faded, the paper was discarded, and the mirror-image is gone forever. But I remember Marechal's reaction: he held my paper to the mirror, squinted at the reflected ink, and said: *Pas mal.* Not bad. The tongue of his approval was the same tongue he used for everything—the plain, direct, game-playing French of a man who believed that the mirror reveals more than the face and the ink records more than the word.

The game of mirrors is a game about the tongue's relationship to its own reflection. Every word we speak is mirrored in the listener's comprehension, and the ink of the listener's response is a further mirror, reflecting back a version of our words that is simultaneously ours and not-ours. Marechal understood this: the teacher's game is always a mirror game, because teaching is the art of reflecting the student's understanding back to the student in the ink of a more articulate tongue.

The mirror hung on the wall of his office. The ink dried on the paper. The game continued in memory.
