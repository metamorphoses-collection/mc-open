What if you could rewrite Nithard's chronicle with perfect numbers? You stand on the hilltop at Fontenoy with a modern instrument of counting—a drone, a satellite, a divine omniscience—and you number every sword, every fire, every drop of blood. The chronicle you produce would be numerically impeccable: the exact number of dead (eight thousand four hundred and twelve), the exact duration of each fire (the third granary burned for forty-seven minutes), the exact angle of each sword thrust (the killing blow to Count Adalbert entered at thirty-two degrees below the horizontal).

Would this perfect chronicle be a better wager than Nithard's imperfect one? The fire of pedantry burns differently from the fire of battle, but it burns just as completely. A chronicle of perfect numbers would be unreadable—a sword made entirely of blade, with no handle for the reader to grasp. The wager of Nithard's approximate numbers is that approximation creates narrative space, that the gap between the number and the reality is where meaning lives.

You imagine a number that could capture the fire of Fontenoy in a single expression: the total thermal energy of all eleven blazes, measured in joules, compressed into a single numeral that the chronicle could inscribe in its margin. That number would be the sword of physics—precise, devastating, and utterly uninformative about the human experience of watching grain burn while men die around you.

The chronicle needs fire to be approximate. The sword needs the chronicle to be approximate. The wager needs both fire and sword to be approximate, because precision eliminates risk, and without risk there is no wager. Nithard's imperfect numbers are not a failure of counting but a strategy of narration. The fire of Fontenoy was not eleven blazes; it was the end of an empire told in flames. No number can count that.

You put down the instrument. You pick up the quill. The fire of the chronicle needs no perfect numbers.
