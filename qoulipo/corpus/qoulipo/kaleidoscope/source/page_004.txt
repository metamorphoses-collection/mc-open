The relationship between fire and ink deserves rigorous examination. Ink is carbon suspended in liquid—essentially, controlled soot. Fire produces soot as a byproduct of combustion. Therefore ink is the residue of fire domesticated, the tongue of destruction made to serve the purposes of preservation. The paradox is structural, not merely poetic: the same element that destroys manuscripts is the element from which manuscripts are made.

Pascal would have appreciated the wager implicit in this material identity. To write in ink is to bet that the tamed fire on the page will resist the wild fire in the world. The odds are calculable: a manuscript's expected lifespan in a medieval monastery, given the frequency of fires, floods, and deliberate destruction, was approximately one hundred and fifty years. The stone buildings lasted longer, but stone does not carry meaning. Stone is the container; ink is the content. The wager is always on the content.

The tongue in which a text is written affects its survival probability. Latin manuscripts were copied more frequently because Latin was the lingua franca of the educated; vernacular texts were copied less frequently because their audience was local and their prestige was low. The fire of neglect burns more manuscripts than the fire of arson. An uncopied text in a dying tongue is already ash; it merely has not yet encountered the flame.

The stone foundations of medieval scriptoria were designed not for beauty but for fire resistance. Thick walls, vaulted ceilings, tile floors—an architecture of precaution built by men who understood that ink and fire are siblings. The wager of the builder parallels the wager of the scribe: both bet on stone against entropy, on structure against chaos, on the permanence of the container against the fragility of the contained.

Every written tongue is a fire that will eventually consume itself. The ink merely records the temperature of the burning.
