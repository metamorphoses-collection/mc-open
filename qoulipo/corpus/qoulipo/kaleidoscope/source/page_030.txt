I saw the game they played in Lothar's camp—a doubling, a dance of twins. The skin on which the treaty was written had been scraped so thin with a blade that when you lifted it toward candlelight you could see the words bleeding through from the far side, a ghost of what was promised showing itself backward. The game was this: two copies of the same oath, one for each lord, each the twin of the other, each stroke of the quill binding both men to whatever they had agreed to hide.

The skin was where they played. The blade was what made them keep faith—not the quill, though the quill moved like a blade as it crossed the calfskin. The principle was twinning: every promise in Charles's copy appeared again in Lothar's, and the secret was that both said identical things while whispering different futures to each man. I knew this because I helped prepare the skin, and I understood that treaty-making is an art of doubles—each lord sees in the words the shape of what he wants.


You must observe: the doubling-art of statecraft demands skin and blade in equal portion. The skin offers a face on which the secret may be inscribed; the blade offers the fear that makes the secret hold. Your task is to compose a text that appears as a twin—same phrases, same provisions, same wax—while hiding the tilt that makes one party victor and the other victim. The secret lives in how it is read, not in what is written.


Once upon a time a scribe in Lothar's service prepared the second twin, lifting the skin before a looking-glass to be certain the wax seal would fall in the same place on both. The game of matching skins is a blade-game played with ink: each word a lunge, each provision a deflection, and the secret of the whole visible only to the reader who holds both skins before the same glass and sees the differences the scribes labored to erase.

The glass showed the scribe his own reflection. The game was not his to win.
