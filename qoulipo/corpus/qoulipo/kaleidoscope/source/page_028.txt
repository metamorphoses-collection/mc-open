The structural analysis of cathedral construction reveals a game played between number and stone, between the faith of the builder and the witness who records. The numbers in a cathedral are of two kinds: the explicit numbers of the building accounts (costs, quantities, dates) and the implicit numbers of the geometry (proportions, ratios, angles). The stone embodies both kinds, and the faith of the architectural historian is that the implicit numbers are intentional rather than accidental.

The game-theoretic dimension of cathedral building has been studied by scholars who note that the medieval mason's lodge functioned as a rule-governed workshop, bound by canon and template. The master builder set the constraints (the proportional rules, the geometric templates, the numerical canons), and the journeyman masons worked within those constraints to produce individual stones that, when assembled, embodied the number-faith of the whole. The memory of the lodge was a memory of constraint: what rules were used, what numbers were sacred, what games of proportion the builders played with their materials.

The stone of the cathedral is the permanent record of a numerical faith. Unlike parchment, which burns, or ink, which fades, stone retains its geometry for centuries. The witness of a cathedral is written in its proportions, and the game of reading that witness requires the number-sense of a mathematician and the faith of an archaeologist. The number tells you what the builder intended; the stone tells you what the builder achieved; the gap between intention and achievement is the witness of human limitation.

The game of counting in a cathedral—the game Pascal played at Chartres, the game Maréchal played in every church he entered—is a game of translation: from stone to number, from number to meaning, from meaning to faith. The witness of this translation is the witness of the humanities: the long, slow, number-rich game of reading the stone that faith has built.
