I saw the burning mirror at the Academie des Sciences—a great concave disc of polished metal, large enough to reflect a man's full figure, designed to concentrate the sun's fire to a point hot enough to melt stone. Records of its construction told of opticians and metalworkers binding their knowledge together, the science of reflection married to the art of fabrication. The mirror was both instrument and spectacle: it revealed the fire hidden in ordinary sunlight and the stone-melting power hidden in ordinary reflection.

I stood before the burning mirror and saw my own face reflected in its curve, distorted by the concavity into a version of myself that was simultaneously larger and smaller, closer and farther. My reflection lasted only a moment—the mirror was being adjusted for a demonstration—but in that moment I understood what all mirrors accomplish: they show us ourselves transformed, and the fire of self-recognition is always the fire of distortion.

The stone they melted with the burning mirror was limestone—the same stone used to build the churches where I prayed, the same stone that formed the foundation of Port-Royal. The fire of the mirror reduced the stone to powder in minutes, focused light turned against focused permanence. Records of the demonstration noted the temperature (estimated by the color of the molten stone), the duration (three minutes to liquefaction), and the astonishment of the assembled academicians, who understood that the mirror's fire was not magic but mathematics.

The burning mirror is a marriage between the sun and the optician. The sun provides the fire; the optician provides the curve; the mirror concentrates both into a point that can melt stone. The record of this marriage is the record of optics itself—the long history of humans learning to redirect the fire of the heavens against the permanence of the earth. The mirror burns because it focuses. The stone melts because it is focused upon. The record contains both the burning and the melting.
