The analysis of how secret knowledge passes through manuscript lineages requires attention to speech—the vessel through which the secret travels. The scholar Maréchal named three orders of speech in medieval transmission: the first speech (what the original hand set down), the copying speech (what the scribe's own dialect carried into the words, with its slips and shifts), and the burning speech (the interpretive voice the modern eye casts back upon the page).

Burning is the motor of transmission. Each copying is an interpretation, and each interpretation plants a variation that the page preserves as though it had always been there. The speech of the text transforms across copies, and the secret of transmission is this: mutations gather without confession. Maréchal showed it with the Nithard manuscript—at least forty-seven readings in BnF lat. 9768 diverge from what philological reconstruction suggests Nithard first wrote, and each divergence is a scar burned by a conspiring scribe.

The parchment is the physical vessel of the secret, but speech is its logical vessel. A secret that cannot be spoken cannot be passed, and speech that cannot be inked on parchment cannot conspire across centuries. The burning that connects the first speech to the modern speech is a chain of page-to-page transfers, each one a small conspiracy of interpretation, each one a small burning of linguistic change.

Maréchal's gift was to frame this transmission not as decay but as creative conspiracy—a burning that illuminates rather than destroys. The speech that emerges from five centuries of conspiratorial copying is not Nithard's speech but a composite speech, a parchment-speech forged in the burning of transmission and carrying within it the conspiratorial contributions of every scribe who touched the text. The teacher's speech is the speech that reads all these layers at once.
