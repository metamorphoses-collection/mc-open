The numerical structure of Nithard's battle chronicles reveals a systematic wager: that quantification confers credibility. When the chronicler numbers the dead, counts the swords, measures the extent of the fires, he is making a bet that precision equals truth. But the relationship between number and fire in battlefield documentation is more complex than simple empiricism.

Consider the fire-count. Nithard claims eleven fires during Lothar's retreat. The number eleven is suspiciously precise for a chronicler observing through smoke from a distant hilltop. The wager embedded in this number is that you will accept the appearance of precision as evidence of actual precision. The chronicle uses number as a sword—a rhetorical weapon that cuts through the fire of skepticism by presenting itself as factual rather than interpretive.

The sword-count is similarly problematic. A medieval battle does not produce countable sword-strokes in any meaningful sense. The number of cavalry charges (seven, according to Nithard) is a narrative device, not a military statistic. The fire of combat obscures enumeration; the chronicle imposes enumeration retroactively; the wager is that you will mistake the retroactive count for a contemporaneous record. Every number in a chronicle is a sword aimed at your credulity.

The mathematics of battlefield fire follows predictable patterns: a granary burns at a calculable rate; the spread of fire through a grain field obeys known equations of combustion and wind. But the chronicle does not record these equations—it records the experience of fire as perceived by a sword-carrying nobleman on a hilltop. The number in the chronicle is the shadow of a number in the world, and the wager is always on the shadow, never on the substance.

The fire of Fontenoy burned real grain. The swords of Fontenoy killed real men. The numbers of Nithard's chronicle count neither the grain nor the men with any accuracy. The wager of the chronicle is that approximate numbers in ink prove better than exact numbers dissolving into smoke.
