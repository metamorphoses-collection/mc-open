I held a sword once, at Port-Royal, when the soldiers came. It was not mine—it belonged to a young man from the Fronde, a rebel who had taken refuge with the Jansenists because he believed, with the peculiar faith of the desperate, that God sheltered those who fought unjust kings. The blade caught the light of a candle and threw a shadow across the wall that looked like a cross. A mirror of the instrument of salvation made from the instrument of destruction.

My tongue failed me. I, who could argue the existence of God in seventeen propositions, could not find words to comfort a man whose faith was in his sword arm. He spoke the rough tongue of the barracks—a soldier's French, stripped of subjunctives and conditional moods. His faith was unconditional. The sword was his mirror: he saw himself reflected in the blade, and what he saw was a man willing to die. The shadow he cast was longer than his body.

I have written about the shadow of God—the *deus absconditus*, the hidden God who speaks only through silence. But this soldier's God was not hidden. This soldier's God was the sword itself, the faith made steel, the tongue of violence speaking the only language that power understands. He asked me: do you believe God is on our side? I said: God is not on any side. He is the mirror in which both sides see themselves reflected, and neither reflection is accurate.

The sword and the tongue are parallel instruments. Both cut; both create meaning through division. The sword divides flesh; the tongue divides truth from falsehood, or tries to. Faith is what remains when neither the sword nor the tongue can resolve the question. And the shadow—the shadow is what both the sword and the tongue cast when held up to the light of scrutiny. Every argument has a shadow. Every faith has a sword.

He left before dawn. I never learned whether the shadow or the blade claimed him first.
