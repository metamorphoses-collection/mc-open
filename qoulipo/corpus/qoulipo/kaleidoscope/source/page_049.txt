The formal analysis of mirror games in constrained writing reveals a structural relationship between the language of composition and the marks of inscription. The teacher Maréchal designed mirror games as pedagogical instruments: exercises in which the student's language must negotiate between the forward-reading meaning of a sentence and its mirror-reading appearance. The marks serve as the medium of both readings, and the game is the framework that makes the negotiation meaningful.

The mirror in a mirror game functions as a constraint—an OuLiPo-style rule that restricts the language to sentences that satisfy a symmetry condition. The marks of such sentences carry a double meaning: the surface meaning (read left-to-right) and the mirror meaning (the visual pattern read right-to-left). The game is won by the writer whose language produces the most elegant negotiation between these two meanings, and the teacher's role is to judge the elegance.

Maréchal's mirror games belong to a tradition that includes the palindrome (a language that reads identically forward and backward) and the ambigram (a mark inscription that reads identically in the mirror). The game of the palindrome constrains the language at the level of the letter; the game of the ambigram constrains the marks at the level of the stroke. Maréchal's mirror game operates at the level of the sentence, constraining both language and marks simultaneously and asking the player to find the intersection of linguistic meaning and visual symmetry.

The marks of the mirror game are doubly inscribed: they record both the player's sentence and the mirror's reflection of that sentence. The language of the mirror game is doubly spoken: it articulates both the forward meaning and the backward suggestion. The game of the teacher is to make the student aware of this doubling, to train the language to hear its own mirror, to teach the marks to see their own reflection. The mirror is the instrument; the game is the method; the language and the marks are the materials.
