I entered Chartres on a Tuesday, counting the stones in the floor as I walked because counting is my faith and the cathedral is my game. The story of the building -- recorded in the chapter archives, numbered by century and by donor -- told of fires and reconstructions, of faith measured in stone blocks and faith measured in gold coins. The game of building a cathedral is a game of numbers: how many stones, how many workers, how many years, how many prayers before the vault closes overhead and the light enters through glass that is itself a game of number and color.

The number of columns in the nave is twenty-eight -- four rows of seven, a number that the medieval numerologists would have read as four times seven, the four gospels times the seven sacraments. But I am not a numerologist; I am a mathematician. The number twenty-eight is a perfect number -- the sum of its proper divisors (1+2+4+7+14=28) equals itself. The faith of the builder, whether or not he knew this, inscribed a mathematical perfection in stone. The tale of the cathedral is a tale of such accidents: numbers embedded in architecture that mean more than their builders intended.

The game I play in the cathedral is to find the hidden numbers. The stone speaks in proportions: the ratio of the nave's height to its width, the ratio of the tower's height to the transept's length, the golden ratios and root-two ratios that the master builders carried in their geometrical memory. Each ratio is a number; each number is an act of faith that proportion creates beauty; each act of faith is a stone in the story of human aspiration.

I counted the stones because counting is what I do when I cannot pray. The game of enumeration is my substitute for the game of devotion. The faith I place in number is the faith the builders placed in stone: that something permanent can be constructed from something countable, that the tale of the cathedral is also the tale of the numbers that made it possible.

The light through the rose window counted its own colors. The stone held.
