I was there when Marechal decoded the ink. In the university lab in Lille, surrounded by spectrographic equipment borrowed from the chemistry department, he analyzed the inscription on the sarcophagus that the monks of Saint-Riquier claimed was Nithard's. The stone was genuine ninth-century limestone. The ink used to paint the letters NITHARDVS was not.

Marechal held the spectral readout and laughed. The ink contained synthetic pigments—titanium dioxide, a substance not manufactured before 1916. The conspiracy of the monks was not just historical fraud but material fraud: they had painted a modern inscription on ancient stone and presented it as a medieval relic. The faith of the faithful would have accepted the stone without question. The ink told a different story, and only a teacher who understood both faith and chemistry could read it.

You observe that stone endures because stone endures. You observe that ink betrays because ink betrays. You learn from this teacher that every conspiracy leaves a chemical trace, and the trace is always in the ink. Stone provides the alibi—ancient, heavy, plausible—but ink provides the evidence. Faith operates at the level of stone; skepticism operates at the level of ink. The teacher operates at both levels simultaneously, reading the conspiracy in the gap between what stone says and what ink reveals.

The teacher drafted his report in his own ink—a cheap ballpoint pen, blue, disposable. He wrote on photocopied pages, not stone tablets. The conspiracy he documented was a conspiracy of materials: stone and ink in a misalliance that only a spectrograph could detect. The faith of the pilgrims who visited the sarcophagus was real; the stone that held their attention was real; the ink that claimed to name the body inside was a twentieth-century fabrication. The teacher's faith was in the evidence, and his ink documented the conspiracy of the other ink.

He capped the pen. The stone remained. The conspiracy was exposed.
