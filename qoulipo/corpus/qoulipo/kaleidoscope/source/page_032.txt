Suppose you could play the mirror game without a sword -- a conspiracy of pure reflection, enacted on parchment without the threat of violence. You would write a treaty with yourself, a game in which the two parties are you-as-writer and you-as-reader, the parchment serving as the mirror between them. The conspiracy would be the gap between intention and interpretation, and the sword would be replaced by the pen, which cuts parchment the way a sword cuts flesh: decisively, irreversibly, and with consequences that outlast the gesture.

You sit before the parchment and write: I agree to read what I have written as if it were written by another. The mirror game of authorship begins. Every word you place on the parchment is a conspiracy between two versions of yourself -- the one who writes and the one who reads. The game is to maintain the illusion of separateness, to pretend that the mirror between you is opaque, that the parchment hides as much as it reveals.

Imagine a mirror made of parchment -- a surface that reflects not light but meaning. You hold up a text and see its interpretation reflected back at you, distorted by the angle of reading, the conspiracy of context, the game of expectations that every reader brings to every page. The sword in this game is criticism: the sharp instrument that cuts through the parchment of the text to reveal the conspiracy of the author's intentions.

What if every game were a mirror game? What if every conspiracy were visible to anyone who held the parchment at the right angle? You would live in a world of total transparency, where every sword was reflected in every mirror and every parchment was legible from both sides. The game of secrets would be over. The conspiracy of hiddenness would collapse. And the mirror -- the mirror would finally be free to reflect what it has always wanted to reflect: nothing at all.

You fold the parchment. The mirror closes. The game awaits its next player.
