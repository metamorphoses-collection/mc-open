I am falling. I am black, I am wet—the gall's bitter smoke still inside me, iron threading through, and the acacia's sticky weeping that will fix me where I land. Below spreads a pale field: the flayed skin of a creature that once grazed, now stretched drumtight on wood. I do not know I will become *D* or *e* or *u*, that I will spell salvation or damnation. I only fall.

Consider: the skin might reject you. The dead animal's membrane, having surrendered warmth and breath, might refuse this final indignity—your human scripture pressed into its surface. The tallow flame would then illumine only absence. That blank page would hold every scripture at once, unwritten: all devotions superposed in whiteness, every prayer that muteness knows.

And so the droplet met the skin. The writer's hand—steadied by centuries of just such meetings—drew her into *aleph*, into *alpha*, into the first stroke of an old case for divinity. Other droplets joined her, a procession of dark siblings each taking shape beside her on the pale field. Together they cast their own small shadow: an argument made visible, faith rendered in ferrous black. The candle shuddered; the skin quivered. She had become permanent—or what passes for it when liquid hardens on slaughter's remnant. Time had already kindled the slow fire that would take her. Its shadow was already everywhere.

Suppose you gathered every parchment ever darkened—every monastery's hoard, every scholar's proof—and made of them one pyre. The faith that writing holds meaning past the moment of its making would blacken with the skins, and the blaze would mark the sky. Yet the words would persist as vapour, as soot riding wind, as carbon seeking carbon. Fire does not end meaning. Fire only alters the form meaning takes.

You are falling. You are ink. The skin waits, faithful as waiting is.
