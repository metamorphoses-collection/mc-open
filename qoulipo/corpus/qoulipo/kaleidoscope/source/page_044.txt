The heat itself had something to say—not filtered through vellum, not whispered by those who bent over desks with quills, but raw, unmediated. I heard it: a sound like old pages being devoured, destruction that somehow set things free—words released from their vellum prisons, ink-conspiracies dissolving, the speaking-heat returning to breath and warmth.

Standing there while the library burned, you listened. Every consumed text lent its accent to the conflagration: church Latin, Frankish annals, common-tongue verse, marginal whispers—all melting into one voice of combustion, a universal dialect of vellum becoming smoke. The teacher would have grasped this speech. A man who'd spent his life parsing hidden meanings would have recognized this final reading—the interpretation that reduces every language to ash and every secret to the democracy of carbon.

Picture vellum that could withstand such heat—writing so essential, meaning so perfectly wrought, that flames would part around it like the Red Sea before Moses. Such fire-proof pages would fulfill the teacher's dream: meaning so transparent, truth so crystalline, that no fire of criticism, no flame of revision, no heat of reinterpretation could alter it. The teacher would have said: impossible, because every language burns and every secret is kindling.

Reaching into the flames, you extracted one scrap of vellum. The writing remained readable—a few words in Nithard's script, a conspiracy of syllables not yet consumed. The fire had edited, removing what didn't matter, leaving only the essential core. This surviving fragment spoke more clearly than the complete manuscript ever had. The fire was the teacher's final revision.

You read the fragment. The fire approved. The vellum spoke once more.
