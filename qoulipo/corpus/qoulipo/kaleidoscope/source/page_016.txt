I remember the instant when the paper caught—first one page, then all of them, the black words shriveling into white smoke. I stood ten feet away and felt the heat on my cheeks. The teacher stood closer, his face orange in the flicker. I could hear the crackling, like static, like whispers folding into silence. My hands were empty. I'd been holding those pages an hour before, feeling their weight, their grain. Now they were lifting into the dark, not even ash, just brightness swallowing itself. I wanted to reach in, to rescue something. But he'd said this was the point, the whole point, and I stood there breathing smoke and trying to hold the words in my throat before they scattered.

You must understand what the burning accomplished. By removing the physical text, the teacher created a void that only memory could fill. The absence migrated from matter into mind. You became the repository, whether you chose it or not. The heat was merely the transfer mechanism; the declared purpose—that this was instruction, not annihilation—gave the transfer its shape. Without that declaration, the destruction would have signified waste. The stone beneath would have witnessed only vandalism. But framed as pedagogy, the act forced you into a new relationship with what you had learned. You could no longer defer to the page. You had become the page.

Once upon a time, a teacher built a fire to teach his students a lesson about keeping. He burned all the papers in his garden, and the papers turned to smoke, and the smoke turned to sky. The students who watched remembered some of what burned—a few rules, a few patterns, a few tricks for making things fit together. Their memories were not perfect mirrors. Each student carried a different piece, a different sliver of what had been whole. But together, all those pieces made a kind of book that lived in many heads instead of one hand. No fire could burn it all at once, because it was everywhere and nowhere. The stone beneath the fire turned black where the heat touched it, and the black mark stayed for years and years. The teacher smiled. He had wanted the mark to last longer than the stone.

The fire needed something solid underneath—scatter paper in open air and it flies before it flames. The stone needed the trial of heat—a foundation untested remains unknown. What remained after, the dark ring burned into the patio, recorded their meeting. The teacher had wagered on the ephemeral against the enduring.

His wager was this: that the mark would outlive the stone itself.
