I stood in Marechal's garden in Lille on the evening he burned his notebooks. He had carried them out in a cardboard box—forty years of teaching notes, game designs, constrained-writing exercises, the shadow-archive of ZaZiPo—and set them on the stone patio beside the barbecue. He lit a match. The fire took slowly, as if the paper itself were reluctant.

He said it was a game. He said the constraint was: destroy everything that can be destroyed, and what survives in memory is the real text. The shadow of the burned notebooks would be more durable than the notebooks themselves, because a shadow cannot burn. The stone patio was the foundation; the fire was the test; the game was to discover what remained when the physical record was gone.

I watched Marechal's handwriting curl and blacken. I recognized exercises I had completed as a student—lipograms, prisoner's constraints, univocalisms—games that had taught me to hear the shadow-frequencies of language, the meanings that emerge only when you remove the obvious sounds. The fire ate them methodically, and Marechal watched with the expression of a man playing a game whose rules he had invented and whose outcome he already knew.

The stone patio survived, of course. It always does. When the fire had finished and the ash had cooled, Marechal swept the remains into the garden with a broom and said: now the game begins. Because the shadow of a burned text is the text that the students carry in their heads, and that text is not fixed—it evolves, it mutates, it plays its own games with memory. The teacher's fire is not destruction; it is propagation. The stone records nothing; the fire releases everything; the shadow is the message.

I carry his games in my head now. The fire took the paper but not the game. The stone patio is cracked. The shadow endures.
