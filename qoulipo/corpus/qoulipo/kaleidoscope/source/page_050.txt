What if the mirror could play the game by itself—writing sentences in its own wet surface, in a speech it invented for this purpose alone? You would watch the mirror fill with marks, each sentence a ghost of the one before, each word an echo. The game would make itself: the mirror speaking to itself, the wetness recording what flickers there, the teacher watching a game that has forgotten its students.

You sit and read as the marks appear. The speech of the mirror is neither French nor Latin nor anything human—it is speech made of light bouncing, of surfaces that show without understanding, of wetness that writes without intention. Marechal would have said: this is the purest game, because it wants nothing but the game. The mirror writes; the wetness holds; the speech tells no one.

Picture a room of mirrors, each playing its own game of wet and speech. The teacher moves among them, reading what the mirrors have made, judging how beautifully they speak to themselves. The game is nonsense and gorgeous: a school of surfaces, an education in the speech of pure reflection, lessons written in wetness that lives only in the mirror and vanishes when you turn it face-down.

You would learn from this mirror-game that speech does not come before wetness, nor wetness before speech. They happen at once, like a reflection and what it copies, and the game of telling them apart is the game of being awake. Marechal taught this without words: every mirror game was a lesson in how speaking and writing happen together, the speech that makes sound and the wetness that makes line. The mirror is where they touch, and the game is the touching.

You speak toward the mirror. The wetness appears. The speech of reflection says: the game has always been playing. You are what it sees.
