What if you could build a telling from stone alone -- no hide, no ash, no steel, just the naked strata themselves shaped by vanished seas? You would read layers of sand pressed flat as pages, the cracks as chapter-breaks, the coiled shells as dramatis personae. The blade in this telling would be rain -- the slow savagery of sky-water gnawing stone the way metal gnaws meat. The ash would be rust, the natural stain of failing iron. The hide would be the naked cliff, a surface inscribed by ages beyond counting.

You stand before this stone telling and read it with the patience of one who knows that every stratum is a folio and every folio took ten millennia to set. Rain's blade has carved the chronicle more deeply than any reed ever pressed, and the rust-stain of old metal endures longer than soot ever did. The cliff's naked face outlasts any scraped skin, and the fundament -- the rock beneath the rock -- is the oldest telling of all.

Imagine writing a mortal telling on this geological hide -- scratching the words of Nithard's Historiae straight into cliff-face, using a sword-point as graver and the sword's own iron as stain. The chronicle would outlive any hide copy; the stone fundament would shield the scratches from the rot that devours libraries. The blade that scratches would be the same blade that made the events described, and the stone that receives the scratches would be the same stone that witnessed them.

You would need a cliff large enough for four books of histories, which at the scale of geological scratching would require some fourteen meters of naked limestone. The graver-blade would fail long before the chronicle was finished, and the iron-stain would redden into the same rust that colors the sandstone cliffs of the Dordogne. Stone hide does not moulder; geological telling does not deceive; the fundament of the earth is the only truly lasting archive.

You lift the blade to the stone. The chronicle begins in deep time.
