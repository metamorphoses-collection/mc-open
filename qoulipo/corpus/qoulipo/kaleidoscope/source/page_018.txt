I discovered the conspiracy while counting. In the summer of 1654, between the night of the Memorial and the composition of the Provincial Letters, I sat in my room at Port-Royal and counted the number of times the Jesuits had used the word "probable" in their casuistic treatises. The number was staggering: four hundred and seventeen instances in a single volume, each one a small gamble that the reader would accept the conspiracy of flexible morality without noticing its arithmetic. The speech of the Jesuits was a contest of numbers disguised as theology.

The conspiracy was simple: by declaring any opinion "probable" if a single authority endorsed it, the Jesuits had created a contest in which every gamble was winnable. A man could commit any act, speak any words, violate any commandment, as long as he could cite one casuist who had declared the action probable. The number of casuists was large; the speech of casuistry was infinitely flexible; the conspiracy was that no one counted the contradictions.

I counted them. The gamble of the Provincial Letters was a bet that numbers could defeat rhetoric, that counting the instances of logical fraud would expose the contest for what it was. My speech in those letters was deliberately plain—the speech of the courtroom, not the chapel, the language of a man who has done his arithmetic and found the books do not balance. The conspiracy of the probable opinions collapsed under the weight of enumeration.

The contest, however, continued. The Jesuits responded not with numbers but with authority—the speech of power, which does not need to count because it does not need to persuade. The conspiracy of institutional language is that it operates outside the contest of logic. You can count every fallacy, number every contradiction, gamble your reputation on the transparency of your arithmetic, and the speech of power will simply declare your numbers irrelevant.

I learned that the gamble of truth against conspiracy is a contest without a guaranteed winner. But I kept counting.
