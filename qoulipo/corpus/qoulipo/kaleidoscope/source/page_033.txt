I found Marechal's secret notebook in a box beneath his desk after he retired. It was not really a notebook—it was a chronicle of promises he had made to himself, written in ink so faded it was nearly invisible. Each entry recorded a hope: that a particular student would become a writer, that a particular riddle-text would reveal a hidden structure, that a particular erasure in a manuscript would turn out to be something older underneath. The ink was cheap—the same blue pen he used for everything—but the weight of each entry pressed deeper than the ink alone could account for.

Marechal's chronicle of promises was a secret syllabus. The official curriculum—medieval literature, puzzle-writing, textual criticism—was the visible ink. The secret notebook was the real teaching: the hopes the teacher held for his students, the promises he made about the future of reading, the erasures he saw in texts that no one else had noticed. The ink recorded the erasure; the chronicle organized the hopes; and Marechal, the teacher, was the only reader.

I promised myself that the secret notebook was meant to be found. Marechal did not throw things away by accident; he preserved by design and destroyed by design. The ink of the notebook was fading, but the erasures—the intellectual promises, the pedagogical hopes—were as vivid as the day he inscribed them. The chronicle of a teacher's hidden promises is the most honest chronicle of all, because it records not what happened but what the teacher believed would happen, which is the erasure of his faith in the enterprise of education.

The ink of the secret notebook had turned from blue to gray, the color of an erasure on a cloudy afternoon in Lille. I read each entry with the care of a student who understands that Marechal's promises were also promises about me—about my ability to read the erasure, decode the ink, continue the chronicle. The secret notebook was his last lesson.
