I hear them swear beneath that winter sky, two brothers ringed by armed men. They're not making promises—they're laying a wager on how many mother-tongues fill this field, how few it takes to make a kingdom. I smell iron, wet wool, the smoke of a thousand breakfast fires. Four languages at least: the guttural speech of the northern warriors, the melting syllables of the southern, the priests' careful Latin, the soldiers' mongrel dialects. The brothers say: we can make this chaos into two. I watch their mouths move. Every promise is arithmetic. Two men. One word. Nothing left over.

Stand in that clearing and tally the voices. Before the brothers spoke, Fontenoy was three languages thick: commanders barked in the scholar's tongue, fighters cursed in the raider's speech, the dying called for mothers in whatever words their cradles knew. The brothers' gambit: that two voices could carry all the rest, that the people's rough speech could bear empire's weight, that the number two could prefigure what would come—two great rivers of language and the old imperial tongue to bridge them.

Once upon a time there was a game where every player spoke in riddles no other could solve. The possible common tongue was none at all, for no language ever fit perfectly into another's shape. But the kingdom-makers made a wager: that near-enough would do, that misunderstanding need not mean murder, that speaking across the gap required not perfect mirrors but sufficient bridges—just enough to keep the blades sheathed.

You would make a counting-speech: all digit, all sum, every word a number and every utterance an answer. Such a tongue would hide nothing. No shadows, no riddles, no play but the play of pure reckoning. Yet the story-wager runs against this dream. The story-wager says: the riddle makes more meaning than the sum, and the game of speaking matters precisely because no one can count what it will make.

You speak the promise. The numbers melt. The conspiracy of voices wakes.
