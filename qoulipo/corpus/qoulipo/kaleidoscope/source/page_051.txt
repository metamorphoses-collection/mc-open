I discovered the shadow arithmetic on a night when the candle guttered and the numbers I had written in ink threw shadows on the page that looked like different numbers. The number 6, in shadow, became something that resembled a 9 -- or perhaps an unfinished 8, a number that had not yet decided what it wanted to be. The play of the shadow was a play of numerical ambiguity: the ink said one thing; the shadow said another; and the blade of logic, which should have cut between them, was itself shadowed by uncertainty.

The shadow arithmetic operates by different rules than the ink arithmetic. In the ink, 2+3=5, always, without exception. In the shadow, 2+3 might equal anything, because the shadow of 2 could be 7 (if the candle is at the right angle) and the shadow of 3 could be 8 (if the ink is sufficiently thick). The play of the shadow is a play of transformation: every number becomes every other number, and the blade of addition, which in the ink-world is precise, becomes in the shadow-world a blade of infinite possibilities.

I played this alone, in my room at Port-Royal, writing numbers in ink and tilting the candle to see their shadows. The blade of mathematical certainty, which I had wielded with such confidence in my published work, cast its own shadow: the uncertainty of the mathematician who knows that his numbers are notations, not truths, and that the shadow of any notation is a different notation. The ink records one number; the shadow proposes another; and the play of mathematics is the play of deciding which to trust.

The shadow arithmetic is not a lesser mathematics but a different one. It is the mathematics of approximation, of estimation, of the educated guess that precedes the precise calculation. The ink gives us the answer; the shadow gives us the neighborhood of the answer. The blade of proof cuts through the shadow to reach the ink, but the shadow remains, a penumbra of alternative numbers surrounding every calculation like the halo around a candle flame.
