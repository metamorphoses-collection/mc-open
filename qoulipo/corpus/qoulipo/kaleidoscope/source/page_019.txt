The assault on casuistry in constrained writing reveals a duel parallel to Pascal's attack on the Jesuits. In both cases, the speaker's mouth operates under a numbered rule: Pascal tallied instances of casuistic fraud; the OuLiPo writer tallies letters, syllables, or woven strands. The stake in both cases is that the rule will produce meanings that unbounded language cannot reach.

Marechal understood this parallel. He called the OuLiPo a plot of mathematicians against the tyranny of inspiration. The number is the weapon; the speaker is the medium; the duel is the method. The plot is benign—it plots not to deceive but to reveal, not to hide the number but to make the number fertile. The stake is that formal rule produces more interesting texts than formal freedom, and the evidence from sixty years of OuLiPo production supports this stake.

The numerical dimension of the plot is precise. A lipogram in E removes approximately thirteen percent of available letters; a prisoner's rule removes roughly forty percent of available words; a ruled text with five woven strands per page restricts the semantic space to a calculable fraction of the total. Each rule is a number; each number is a speaker's mouth speaking in cipher; each cipher is a duel whose laws the reader must discover. The plot is that the laws are hidden in the text, not stated in a preface.

The speaker's mouth of plotting and the speaker's mouth of rule share a grammar: both operate through restriction, both create meaning through what they exclude, both stake that the reader will decode the number. The duel of reading a ruled text is the inverse of the duel of writing one. The writer plots to embed a numerical structure; the reader plots to extract it. Between these two plots, the text exists as a speaker's mouth spoken by both parties in a language neither fully controls.

The number is the plot. The speaker is the duel. The stake is that someone will count.
