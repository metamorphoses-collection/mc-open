The mind builds two towers on the same ground: what has been, what might be. Every witness writes in daylight, pressing the nib hard; every diviner writes in twilight, barely touching the page. You who teach keep your divinations locked, but the teacher Marechal brought them into the room where children could see them.

Light falls on one page: this happened, this was said, this child answered thus. On another page, barely visible, other marks gather: this child will, this child might, I wager my belief against the future's verdict. The first page speaks the past; the second whispers to time itself. A diviner's mark is a promise thrown forward, trusting the days will catch it or let it fall.

Marechal did what teachers before her would not: she showed the children her divinations. The old way kept the teacher's belief hidden—where she placed her faith in them, what future she imagined—all unspoken, hovering in the air between desk and door. Putting such marks on paper, even in ink that barely holds its colour, is to make a shape of the invisible: not what the children did, but what she imagined flowering inside them.

The marks fade because time judges them. Every belief about a child has a season; the future arrives and says yes or no; the children grow past the teacher's gaze and the marks lose their need for staying. What the world sees—the child's later work, her grown accomplishments—these are carved in stone. What the teacher saw first, when no one else did, when she wrote it with a trembling hand—this dissolves like breath on glass. The divination came first; the achievement followed; the ink held them together while it lasted.

To write a divination down, knowing it will fade: this is what trust looks like.
