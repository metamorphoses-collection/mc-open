What if you could build a cathedral from numbers alone -- no stone, no mortar, no labor, just the pure mathematics of proportion and symmetry, suspended in the air like the faith it embodies? You would chronicle its construction the way a geometer chronicles a proof: axiom by axiom, number by number, each stone a theorem that rests on the stones below it. The game of this cathedral would be the game of logic itself, and the faith required to enter would be faith in reason.

You stand in the nave of this number-cathedral and look up. The vault above you is a series of equations, each one curving toward the keystone where all the numbers meet. The stone is abstract -- you cannot touch it, but you can count it, and the counting is the touching. The chronicle of this building is the chronicle of mathematics from Euclid to Euler, from the game of the Greeks to the faith of the moderns, each number a stone in a structure that grows upward without weight.

Imagine the rose window of this cathedral. It would be made not of glass but of prime numbers, each one catching a different frequency of mathematical light. The game of the primes is the oldest game in number theory: which numbers are divisible only by themselves and one? The faith of the mathematician is that the primes go on forever, that the cathedral of numbers has no ceiling, that the stone of proof supports an infinite upward extension.

You would chronicle the cathedral's construction in a book made of stone -- each page a slab of marble inscribed with the numbers of the building. The game of reading this book would be the game of reading the cathedral itself: moving from stone to stone, from number to number, from the faith of the foundation to the faith of the spire. The chronicle would be the cathedral, and the cathedral would be the number, and the number would be the stone that faith built from counting.

You lay the first stone. It is the number one. The game begins.
