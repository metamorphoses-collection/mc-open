I heard him once murmur numbers while reading Queneau aloud—his lips moving through syllables like prayer beads, one two three four five, and suddenly the poem wasn't words anymore but weights, measures, a skeleton of integers beneath the flesh of language. I sat in the back row watching chalk dust spiral through September light, and every particle seemed counted, assigned its place in some tremendous ledger only he could see. His handout—creased, coffee-stained, scrawled with red and blue and green annotations—lay before me like a relic, each margin filled with calculations that turned sentences into sums. I couldn't look away. The air smelled of old paper and the sweet rot of oak gall ink, and I realized he was teaching us to see what couldn't be seen: the secret arithmetic hiding inside every paragraph, waiting.


You might impose on yourself this discipline: write such that each sentence knows its own length, such that every paragraph mirrors back to you exactly how many words it contains, such that the final page announces in its opening line how many pages follow. You would create thereby a text that requires no table of contents because it is its own table, no index because every word has already indexed itself. Unreadable, perhaps. Magnificent, certainly. Marechal believed—and here belief matters more than proof—that enumeration could become a form of sight. Count carefully enough and the invisible scaffolding of thought becomes visible. Count long enough and you are no longer reading but listening to the structure itself speak.


Once upon a time in the north country there lived a teacher who believed that all the sheep in Picardy had given their skins for a single purpose: to be written upon with numbers. He would make ink from the galls of Crécy oaks, dark as old blood, and with this ink he would write a book that counted every word it contained, a book that was also a mirror, also a map, also a hymn. Children came from Lille and Arras to sit at his feet while he explained how counting could be prayer. The sheep grazed outside his window. The oak trees dropped their galls each autumn like offerings. And the teacher wrote and counted, counted and wrote, until text and tally became one thing, until the world and its measure were the same.


You begin. The numbers rise like birds from the page.
