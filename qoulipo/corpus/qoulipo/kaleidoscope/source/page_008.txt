I saw them—the mouths moving, every jaw working its small machinery. Oath piled on oath, curses like stones flung, prayers threading upward where no ear would catch them. The eastern men spoke in their harsh northern sounds, the western in something softer, almost song. The priests murmured Latin over boys who'd never learned it. I watched the faith of dying men rise like steam from a kettle, every word evaporating into air no living soul could breathe.

What tongue would iron choose, if iron could choose? It would speak the language learned in darkness—the ore's first dreaming, the hammer's percussion, the slow education of fire shaping metal into purpose. Its faith would live entirely in the hand that grips it, and its twin would be the other blade across the field, the two of them conversing in geometries of ruin, each insisting on the other's end.

You stand at Fontenoy as the morning breaks. Before you, a choice: the blade or the word. Choose the blade and you may see evening, but your reflection will hunt you through every polished surface for all your remaining years. Choose the word and you may not finish your sentence—the man with the blade does not pause for rhetoric—yet what you cast upon the ground will be lighter than blood, and words evaporate where blood congeals. Either choice demands faith past measure. Neither finds favor with reflection.

Picture a world where every blade is also a mirror, every mirror also a mouth. Violence would collapse under its own contradictions—each stroke simultaneously a looking-back and a speaking-forth. The shadow of that world is this one: a place where blade, reflection, and speech stand separate, held in their uneasy compact only by faith.

You lower the blade. The mirror fogs. The shadow of an unspoken tongue falls across the field.
