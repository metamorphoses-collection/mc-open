I crouch near the patio where yesterday's flames ate Professor Marechal's papers. Heat still breathes from the flagstones. The air tastes of ash—lavender ash, paper ash. My fingertips brush warm granite. The Professor stands before us, palms open, empty. "Today," he says, "you rebuild what I burned."

Nothing left. No handouts. No prompts. Only what lives in your skull—the games he made you play. That text he assigned with no letter E anywhere, five pages of missing vowels. That prisoner's drill: each sentence starting with A, then B, then C, down the alphabet like a ladder. That morning he made you count: five words, how many orders? You scribbled numbers until your wrist ached. These exercises—hard, strange, obsessive—won't burn. They're carved in you now.

The archive could grow back from those seeds. Every drill you recall becomes a blueprint. What the fire did—maybe this was its purpose—was strip the decorative scroll-work, leave only load-bearing stone. Marechal knew which pieces would outlast flame. A teacher always knows. He built the pyre; he struck the match. The entire spectacle was arranged so the right things would survive.

Picture a school conjured entirely from ash-memory. Walls of limestone and slate—solid, cool, permanent—but the lessons themselves are fire: leaping, eating, changing shape. Every student carries a different shard of what the Professor taught, so no two rebuildings match. Education becomes a problem of combinations: how many schools can one fire's ghost generate? Marechal would say: as many as there are students. The stone we share. The fire we share. The shadow belongs to each alone.

I sit in the garden. The stones hold yesterday's heat. The game starts over.
