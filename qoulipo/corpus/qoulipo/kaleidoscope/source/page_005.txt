Imagine you are the last speaker of a tongue that has no written form. You stand before a wall of stone—perhaps the ruins of a Roman aqueduct, perhaps the foundation of a church not yet built—and you understand that when your mouth closes for the final time, an entire universe of meaning will vanish. No ink has ever captured your language. No fire can destroy what was never recorded, but neither can any future scholar recover it.

You would wager everything for a single page of ink. You would trade the stone walls of a cathedral for one sheet of parchment bearing your words in your tongue, because stone without inscription is merely geology, and fire without something to burn is merely chemistry. The wager of the last speaker is the inverse of the scribe's wager: the scribe bets that ink will survive; you bet that ink will arrive in time.

What if every fire that ever consumed a library was, in some structural sense, necessary? What if the tongue that survives the flames is the tongue that deserved to survive—not by merit but by the sheer statistical wager of having been copied often enough? The ink that survives is the ink that was redundant. The stone that endures is the stone that was over-engineered. Perhaps the lesson is not that fire destroys but that fire selects.

You could build a language from stone itself—carve each word into granite, make the tongue indestructible. But stone is slow, and language is fast, and the fire of conversation moves at the speed of breath while the ink of inscription moves at the speed of a hand. By the time you have carved the first sentence in stone, the tongue has already evolved past it. The wager of permanence against velocity is the oldest wager in the history of writing, and ink—fragile, flammable, mortal ink—has always been the better bet.

You turn from the stone wall and speak into the wind. The fire of the living tongue burns without fuel.
