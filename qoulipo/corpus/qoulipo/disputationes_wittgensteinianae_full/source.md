# Disputationes Wittgensteinianae

*Triginta quaestiones de Tractato Logico-Philosophico — standard versus resolute readings, across five thematic blocks.*

> A benchmark on the standard-versus-resolute reading debate in Tractatus scholarship.
> Thirty contested propositions, six per block, across five thematic blocks: De mundo,
> De imagine, De forma generali, De ethica, De silentio. Each quaestio pairs a
> Sic paragraph defending the standard metaphysical reading (Hacker, Pears, McGuinness,
> Anscombe) with a Non paragraph defending the resolute reading (Diamond, Conant,
> Goldfarb, Kremer, Crary, Read). Aquinas-style \emph{Sic et Non}, Latin titling,
> English body — *clin d'œil* to Wittgenstein's own Latin-titled vernacular treatise.

---

**¶0001 Proem.** *De duobus modis legendi Tractatum.* Two interpretive traditions divide Wittgenstein scholarship. The *standard reading* (Hacker, Pears, Glock, Anscombe) takes the Tractatus to advance substantive theses about logic, language, ontology, ethics, and the mystical — theses that may turn out, by the book's own lights at 6.54, to be strictly nonsense, but which carry real philosophical content along the way. The *resolute reading* (Diamond, Conant, Goldfarb, Kremer, Crary, Read) holds that the Tractarian propositions are plain nonsense; the book's purpose is therapeutic, to bring the reader to recognise their own attempt to make sense of metaphysical pseudo-questions, and to set those attempts down. Thirty quaestiones follow.

---

## Block I — De mundo et facto

**¶0002 Praefatio I.** *De mundo et de facto.* The opening propositions of the Tractatus (1.x and 2.x) lay out a putative ontology: the world is the totality of facts, facts decompose into atomic facts, atomic facts are configurations of objects, objects have form. Whether this is genuine ontology or self-cancelling preliminary is the first interpretive divide.

**¶0003 Quaestio I — Sic.** *The world is the totality of facts, not of things (TLP 1.1).* On the standard reading, this is a substantive ontological thesis: facts, not objects, are the basic constituents of reality. Wittgenstein commits to a fact-ontology opposed to Russell's logical atomism of objects. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*) reads 1.1 as Wittgenstein's distinguishing thesis: the world is structured into obtaining states of affairs, each a configuration of objects standing in relation. McGuinness traces the doctrine to Hertz's mechanics and Boltzmann's physics — facts as the structured content of representations.

**¶0004 Quaestio I — Non.** 1.1 has no determinate metaphysical content. Diamond and Conant argue that the proposition draws the reader into supposing "facts" can be substantively distinguished from "things" — only for the supposition to collapse under examination. Once one tries to specify what makes a fact not a thing, the distinction unravels into nonsense. The proposition is part of the ladder 6.54 says we must climb and throw away. There is no doctrine of facts versus objects in the Tractatus; the appearance of one is the trap.

**¶0005 Quaestio II — Sic.** *Atomic facts are independent of one another (TLP 2.061).* On the standard reading, this is the substantive **logical independence thesis**: each atomic fact obtains or fails to obtain independently of every other, and the totality of atomic facts gives the world its determinate content. Pears (*The False Prison*, vol. 1) treats this as Wittgenstein's commitment to logical atomism in the strong Russellian sense — an atomic-fact mosaic on which all complex truths supervene by truth-functional combination.

**¶0006 Quaestio II — Non.** The independence claim cannot be coherently stated by anyone who accepts the Tractatus's strictures on what can be said. Goldfarb ("Das Überwinden") notes that "atomic fact" never receives an actual elucidation in the Tractatus, and that attempts in the secondary literature to specify even one are notoriously unsuccessful. The independence proposition has the grammatical form of a substantive thesis but functions as an empty placeholder: the reader is invited to suppose there are such things as atomic facts standing in such relations, only to be brought, by 6.54, to recognise the supposition was vacuous.

**¶0007 Quaestio III — Sic.** *Substance is what subsists independently of what is the case (TLP 2.024).* On the standard reading, this is the **substance argument**: the world must contain a substantive layer of objects whose existence does not depend on contingent facts, lest the sense of any proposition depend on the truth of other propositions. Anscombe (*Introduction to Wittgenstein's Tractatus*, ch. 2) treats 2.024 as the conclusion of a transcendental argument from the determinacy of sense to the necessity of simple objects. Hacker reads the argument as committing Wittgenstein to a robust modal ontology: simple objects are the metaphysical foundation of representational determinacy.

**¶0008 Quaestio III — Non.** The substance argument is a paradigm of Tractarian self-cancellation. Kremer ("The Purpose of Tractarian Nonsense") observes that the argument presupposes a meaningful distinction between "what subsists" and "what is the case", and that this distinction cannot be drawn within the limits of the very semantic theory the argument is meant to support. The argument's apparent force evaporates the moment one notices that "substance" and "object" never receive any independent specification. The reader is meant to climb the argument and then recognise it as one of the rungs to be thrown away.

**¶0009 Quaestio IV — Sic.** *The world divides into facts (TLP 1.2).* On the standard reading, this is the **structuring thesis**: the world is not an unstructured totality but is articulated, by its very nature, into discrete facts. Hacker reads 1.2 as Wittgenstein's commitment to a non-conventionalist view of articulation: how the world divides into facts is not up to us. Pears connects this to the picture theory: a proposition succeeds in representing only if there is a fact, antecedently structured, for it to represent.

**¶0010 Quaestio IV — Non.** "The world divides into facts" is not a description but an empty gesture in the direction of a description. Conant ("The Method of the Tractatus") presses that the proposition presupposes one can stand outside the totality of facts and observe their division — exactly the position the Tractatus elsewhere denies is intelligible. The proposition appears to assert something substantive about world-structure while in fact specifying no content the reader could test, contest, or even formulate clearly.

**¶0011 Quaestio V — Sic.** *Objects form the substance of the world (TLP 2.021).* On the standard reading, this is the **simple-objects thesis**: objects (in the Tractarian technical sense) are the irreducible, unanalysable simples out of which all complexity is composed. Anscombe and Hacker treat the simple-objects doctrine as Wittgenstein's most distinctive metaphysical contribution: a commitment to compositional semantics grounded in non-decomposable referents. The Notebooks 1914-1916 confirm Wittgenstein's serious wrestling with what objects are and how their simplicity is to be specified.

**¶0012 Quaestio V — Non.** Wittgenstein famously declines, throughout the Tractatus, to specify a single example of a Tractarian object. The resolute reader (Diamond, *The Realistic Spirit*; Kremer) takes this not as a regrettable omission but as a deliberate refusal: "object" in the Tractatus does not refer, and the would-be reader who tries to fix its reference is meant to discover that they cannot. The simple-objects doctrine is a pseudo-doctrine, designed to be entertained and then recognised as empty. Wittgenstein's later remark to Norman Malcolm — that he no longer believed there were such things — is read as the natural completion of a critique already implicit in the Tractatus itself.

**¶0013 Quaestio VI — Sic.** *Reality is the existence and non-existence of states of affairs (TLP 2.06).* On the standard reading, this is the **reality thesis**: what is real is the totality of obtaining states of affairs together with the totality of non-obtaining ones. Hacker treats this as a substantive commitment to a modally-rich ontology in which possible states of affairs have a definite status as elements of reality. McGuinness sees 2.06 as the bridge from the ontology of facts to the picture theory of proposition: a proposition pictures by sharing the form of the possible state of affairs it represents.

**¶0014 Quaestio VI — Non.** The reality thesis purports to describe reality as the joint totality of the existent and the non-existent — a description that requires the speaker to enumerate non-existent states of affairs as constituents of reality. Goldfarb and Diamond argue this is exactly the kind of nonsense the Tractatus is engineered to expose: a proposition that promises to describe reality, while the form of the description (joint-totalising over existence and non-existence) is incoherent. The "reality" of 2.06 is a Tractarian pseudo-totality, not a notion the reader can pin down.

## Block II — De imagine et propositione

**¶0015 Praefatio II.** *De pictura logica et de propositione.* The middle range of the Tractatus (2.1x and 3.x and 4.0x) develops the picture theory of proposition: a proposition is a logical picture of a state of affairs, sharing its form. Whether the picture theory is a substantive doctrine of representation or a self-cancelling preliminary to silence is the second interpretive divide.

**¶0016 Quaestio VII — Sic.** *A proposition is a picture of reality (TLP 4.01).* On the standard reading, this is the **picture theory**: propositions represent by depicting the formal structure of the states of affairs they describe. Pears (*The False Prison*, vol. 1) reads the picture theory as Wittgenstein's central semantic doctrine — propositions and what they represent share a logical form, and that shared form is what makes representation possible. The theory has influenced semantics from Russell to Kripke to truth-conditional semantics broadly construed.

**¶0017 Quaestio VII — Non.** The picture theory pre-supposes a vantage from which one can compare proposition and reality and find they share form — exactly the vantage the Tractatus declares impossible. Diamond (*The Realistic Spirit*, ch. 6) and Conant ("Elucidation and Nonsense") argue that the picture theory is the most seductive of the Tractatus's pseudo-doctrines: it appears to explain representation while in fact presupposing the unintelligible god's-eye view it elsewhere forbids. The doctrine is meant to be entertained, found compelling, and then — at 6.54 — recognised as empty.

**¶0018 Quaestio VIII — Sic.** *A picture is a fact (TLP 2.141).* On the standard reading, this is the **picture-as-fact thesis**: representation is a relation between facts (the picture, which is itself a fact) and other facts (what it represents). Hacker treats this as a substantive ontological claim about representation: pictures are not abstract objects, propositions, or mental contents, but facts in the same sense as the facts they represent. The thesis distinguishes Wittgenstein's view from Fregean and Russellian theories of judgement.

**¶0019 Quaestio VIII — Non.** "A picture is a fact" presupposes that "picture" and "fact" are categories the reader can fix independently — which the Tractatus's own strictures preclude. Crary and Read (*The New Wittgenstein*) press that this proposition is part of a chain of pseudo-elucidations in which each successive technical term is supposed to be clarified by the one before, but in fact none of them ever gets a non-circular specification. The picture theory's apparent content is an artefact of the reader's willingness to entertain undefined technical vocabulary.

**¶0020 Quaestio IX — Sic.** *A proposition has one and only one complete analysis (TLP 3.25).* On the standard reading, this is the **determinacy of sense thesis**: every proposition has a unique full logical analysis into elementary propositions. McGuinness and Hacker treat this as Wittgenstein's commitment to determinate logical structure beneath everyday language; the apparent vagueness of natural-language propositions is a surface phenomenon, dispelled by analysis. The thesis grounds the substance argument of 2.024.

**¶0021 Quaestio IX — Non.** The complete-analysis thesis cannot be coherently formulated. Diamond ("Throwing Away the Ladder") argues that to assert determinate analysability presupposes a metalinguistic vantage from which analyses can be compared and a unique one identified — a vantage incompatible with the Tractatus's own picture of language. The "one and only one" of 3.25 is a pseudo-quantifier; the proposition is meant to entrap the reader into supposing they understand it before recognising it as one more rung to discard.

**¶0022 Quaestio X — Sic.** *The general form of a proposition is: things are thus and so (TLP 4.5).* On the standard reading, this is the **general-form thesis**: every proposition is the assertion that things stand in some configuration, and the abstract form of this assertion is fully general. Anscombe reads 4.5 as Wittgenstein's most compressed statement of his account of proposition: any proposition whatsoever is structurally the assertion of a configuration. The thesis underwrites the truth-functional account of 5.x.

**¶0023 Quaestio X — Non.** "Things are thus and so" is a deliberately empty formula whose Wittgensteinian function is to mark the impossibility of saying what the general form of proposition is. Conant ("What 'Ethics' in the Tractatus Is Not") observes that the proposition substitutes a place-holder for an account: the reader who takes 4.5 to express the general form of proposition has missed that the would-be expression is grammatically incomplete. The proposition models its own emptiness.

**¶0024 Quaestio XI — Sic.** *In a proposition a thought finds an expression that can be perceived by the senses (TLP 3.1).* On the standard reading, this is the **expression thesis**: propositions are the perceptible vehicles of thoughts. Pears reads 3.1 as Wittgenstein's commitment to a non-mentalist account of thought-expression: what is expressed is a logical structure, the perceptible signs are the medium, and the relation between them is internal. The thesis grounds Wittgenstein's later (in PI) distance from psychologism while preserving the irreducibility of meaning to use.

**¶0025 Quaestio XI — Non.** "A thought finds an expression" treats thoughts as antecedently determinate items that subsequently receive perceptible clothing — exactly the picture of mind-language relation Wittgenstein dismantles. Goldfarb argues this is one of the Tractatus's most fertile traps for the reader: the proposition appears to explain how language relates to thought while reproducing, in compressed form, the philosophical confusion the book is meant to dispel.

**¶0026 Quaestio XII — Sic.** *Logic is transcendental (TLP 6.13).* On the standard reading, this is the **transcendental status of logic thesis**: logical truths are not propositions about the world but conditions on the possibility of any proposition whatsoever. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*) treats this as Wittgenstein's most explicit commitment to a transcendental philosophy in the Kantian tradition. The thesis distinguishes Wittgenstein's view of logic from Russell's (logic as the most general truths about the world).

**¶0027 Quaestio XII — Non.** "Logic is transcendental" gestures at a thesis whose content cannot be specified within the framework it would inhabit. Conant ("The Method of the Tractatus") notes that to say logic is transcendental is to assign logic a position with respect to the world; but the very characterisation of the transcendental requires one to occupy a non-transcendental standpoint from which to point at the transcendental. The proposition collapses on its own grammar and is one of the most paradigmatic Tractarian non-positions.

## Block III — De forma generali

**¶0028 Praefatio III.** *De forma generali propositionum.* The 5.x range of the Tractatus develops the truth-functional account of proposition and arrives, in 5.47–5.5, at the general propositional form. Whether this is Wittgenstein's most substantive technical contribution or his most ironic self-cancellation is the third interpretive divide.

**¶0029 Quaestio XIII — Sic.** *Every proposition is a truth-function of elementary propositions (TLP 5).* On the standard reading, this is the **truth-functional thesis**: the meaning of any complex proposition is determined by the truth-values of its elementary constituents via Boolean operations. McGuinness and Pears treat this as Wittgenstein's most substantive technical contribution: a precise theory of how complex propositions inherit their truth-conditions from elementary ones. The thesis is the algebraic backbone of the Tractatus.

**¶0030 Quaestio XIII — Non.** The truth-functional thesis cannot be elementarily stated. Diamond and Goldfarb press that "every proposition" requires the speaker to range over the totality of propositions — a meta-position the Tractatus elsewhere declares incoherent. The thesis appears precise because it borrows the form of Russellian quantification, but the form is borrowed without the entitlement: the would-be Tractarian who asserts 5 cannot occupy the standpoint the assertion requires.

**¶0031 Quaestio XIV — Sic.** *The general form of truth-function is $[\bar{p}, \bar{\xi}, N(\bar{\xi})]$ (TLP 6).* On the standard reading, this is the **N-operator thesis**: every proposition can be constructed from elementary propositions by successive applications of the joint-negation operator $N$. Hacker treats 6 as Wittgenstein's most technical and most substantive doctrine, defining a complete combinatorial system. Pears connects it to the philosophical foundations of recursion theory and to Wittgenstein's later interest in calculation.

**¶0032 Quaestio XIV — Non.** The N-operator formalism, examined closely, does not specify a procedure that could be followed by anyone who took the Tractatus's strictures on the speakable seriously. Kremer ("Mathematical Meaning in the Tractatus") observes that 6's formula is a hieroglyph: its content depends on prior understanding of "elementary proposition", "operation", "general form of all propositions" — all terms the Tractatus has refused to fix. The formula is a piece of pseudo-mathematics, designed to look like a definite achievement and to dissolve under examination.

**¶0033 Quaestio XV — Sic.** *Logic must take care of itself (TLP 5.473).* On the standard reading, this is the **logical autonomy thesis**: logical syntax is self-sustaining and requires no external justification. Anscombe treats 5.473 as Wittgenstein's commitment to a non-foundationalist view of logic; logical rules don't need grounding in some prior metalogic. The thesis is Wittgenstein's most direct critique of Frege's and Russell's logicism: logic is autonomous, not derived.

**¶0034 Quaestio XV — Non.** "Logic must take care of itself" sounds substantive but expresses no determinate content. Goldfarb argues that the proposition's appeal lies in personifying logic, as if logic were an agent that could take care of itself or fail to. Once the personification is dispelled, the proposition is empty: there is no clear thesis here, only a slogan that strikes the reader as profound before they ask what it would mean for logic to *fail* to take care of itself.

**¶0035 Quaestio XVI — Sic.** *The world and life are one (TLP 5.621).* On the standard reading, this is the **solipsism thesis** in its Tractarian form: the metaphysical subject coincides with its world, and what we ordinarily call "the I" is not a constituent of the world but its limit. Pears (*The False Prison*, vol. 2) treats 5.621 as Wittgenstein's most serious wrestling with Schopenhauer: the metaphysical I is non-empirical, transcendental, and identical with the limit of language.

**¶0036 Quaestio XVI — Non.** "The world and life are one" is the kind of Tractarian gesture-proposition the resolute reader takes as its most direct self-defeat. To say "world and life are one" is either to say something trivially true (if "world" and "life" are co-extensive by stipulation) or to say something incoherent (if they were specifying distinct domains, the assertion of their identity would require a vantage from which to compare them). Either way the proposition has no content.

**¶0037 Quaestio XVII — Sic.** *The limits of my language mean the limits of my world (TLP 5.6).* On the standard reading, this is the **linguistic-limit thesis**: what can be thought is what can be said, and the bounds of expressibility coincide with the bounds of intelligible reality. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*, ch. 3) treats 5.6 as Wittgenstein's most influential thesis on the relation of language to world: the propositional shapes available to me are also the worldly shapes available to me. The thesis underwrites the entire programme of philosophical clarification.

**¶0038 Quaestio XVII — Non.** "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world" is the kind of slogan that has launched a thousand misreadings. Diamond and Conant note that taken at face value, 5.6 ascribes to "my language" a definite boundary, observable from outside — exactly the position 5.6 itself implies is impossible. The proposition is meant to draw the reader into picturing language-as-a-bounded-region and then to dissolve the picture by noting that bounded regions require an outside.

**¶0039 Quaestio XVIII — Sic.** *That logic should be a priori consists in the impossibility of illogical thought (TLP 5.4731).* On the standard reading, this is the **a priori thesis**: logic's a priori status is grounded in the impossibility, not the unreliability, of illogical thinking. McGuinness reads this as Wittgenstein's central argument for the necessity of logical truth: logic isn't supported by anything because nothing could have supported it; its truth is built into the very possibility of thought.

**¶0040 Quaestio XVIII — Non.** "The impossibility of illogical thought" presupposes that "thought" is a coherent category prior to logic — and that we have grasped, in advance, what illogical thought would have been. Conant argues this is the model case of a Tractarian self-undermining proposition: the proposition explains logic's a priori status by reference to a notion (impossible illogical thought) whose specification requires the very logic it would explain.

## Block IV — De ethica et voluntate

**¶0041 Praefatio IV.** *De ethica et de voluntate.* The 6.4x range of the Tractatus addresses ethics, the will, and the mystical. These are the propositions where the standard-vs-resolute divide is most consequential for what one takes Wittgenstein's positive view to be.

**¶0042 Quaestio XIX — Sic.** *Ethics is transcendental (TLP 6.421).* On the standard reading, this is the **transcendental ethics thesis**: ethics, like logic, is not a domain within the world but a condition on the meaningful inhabitation of the world. Anscombe (*Modern Moral Philosophy*; *Introduction to Wittgenstein's Tractatus*, ch. 13) reads 6.421 as Wittgenstein's commitment to a substantive metaethics: there exists a transcendental ethical dimension that cannot be reduced to facts about the world but is real nonetheless.

**¶0043 Quaestio XIX — Non.** "Ethics is transcendental" gestures at a thesis whose content cannot be cashed out without violating the strictures of the very framework that supposedly grounds it. Diamond ("Ethics, Imagination and the Method of Wittgenstein's Tractatus") argues that the proposition is part of a pattern in which the reader is led to entertain "the transcendental" as a discoverable feature of reality, only to recognise that no such feature has been specified. The transcendental of 6.421 is a place-holder, not a thesis.

**¶0044 Quaestio XX — Sic.** *The world of the happy is a different world from that of the unhappy (TLP 6.43).* On the standard reading, this is the **subjective-world thesis**: the will, though it cannot alter facts, alters the world in a deeper sense by altering its limits. McGuinness and Pears connect 6.43 to Wittgenstein's wartime religious experience and to Tolstoy's *Confession*: the will-as-attitude reshapes the world in a way that transcends factual description. The thesis is part of the Tractatus's most overtly Schopenhauerian moment.

**¶0045 Quaestio XX — Non.** "The world of the happy is a different world from that of the unhappy" cannot be paraphrased without disappearing. Conant ("Mysticism and Nonsense") notes that if the two "worlds" share all the same facts (as Wittgenstein insists), then what distinguishes them is unspecifiable; if they differ in some respect, the proposition contradicts the prior insistence that facts exhaust the world. The proposition is a particularly clear case of a Tractarian gesture that the reader is meant to recognise as empty.

**¶0046 Quaestio XXI — Sic.** *God does not reveal himself in the world (TLP 6.432).* On the standard reading, this is the **theological non-immanence thesis**: God, if there is a God, does not appear within the totality of facts. Anscombe and McGuinness treat 6.432 as Wittgenstein's serious engagement with the via negativa: the divine is real but not part of the world, and the propositional resources of the Tractatus cannot reach it. The thesis is the religious counterpart of the transcendental ethics of 6.421.

**¶0047 Quaestio XXI — Non.** To say "God does not reveal himself in the world" presupposes "God" as a referent whose mode of (non-)revelation can be discussed. Kremer ("Wittgenstein's Tractatus, A Christian Reading") argues that the proposition is more honest than it appears: it doesn't assert that there is a God who happens not to reveal himself, but exhibits the emptiness of theological propositions of the kind that purport to be substantive while their subject-term lacks reference. The reading is consonant with Wittgenstein's later remark that he wrote the Tractatus from a religious standpoint, while remaining austere about what propositions could carry.

**¶0048 Quaestio XXII — Sic.** *How things are in the world is a matter of complete indifference for what is higher (TLP 6.432).* On the standard reading, this is the **higher-realm thesis**: there exists a "higher" — value, the divine, the absolute — that is independent of the contingent arrangement of facts. Anscombe reads this as Wittgenstein's most direct commitment to the existence of a non-factual dimension of reality.

**¶0049 Quaestio XXII — Non.** "What is higher" is a referring expression without a referent. Goldfarb and Crary argue that the resolute reader takes 6.432 as the most overt invitation to a Tractarian pseudo-doctrine: the proposition appears to assert the existence and indifference of a higher realm, while in fact specifying nothing the reader could test, locate, or characterise. The "higher" is the placeholder par excellence.

**¶0050 Quaestio XXIII — Sic.** *Not how the world is, is the mystical, but that it is (TLP 6.44).* On the standard reading, this is the **mystical-existence thesis**: the brute factual existence of the world — as opposed to its particular features — is the proper object of mystical experience. McGuinness ("The Mysticism of the Tractatus") treats this as Wittgenstein's most direct commitment to a Heideggerian-avant-la-lettre wonderment at being. The thesis is positive metaphysics in apophatic dress.

**¶0051 Quaestio XXIII — Non.** "That the world is" is grammatically a proposition but propositionally empty: the bare existence of the world cannot be a contingent fact (it is not the kind of thing that could fail to be the case), and so is not the kind of thing about which a meaningful proposition could be formed. Diamond and Conant treat 6.44 as a particularly stark case of the Tractatus exhibiting an expression that strikes the reader as profound, by an attentive reader's lights revealing itself as ill-formed.

**¶0052 Quaestio XXIV — Sic.** *The will is an attitude of the subject towards the world (TLP 6.423).* On the standard reading, this is the **will-as-attitude thesis**: the metaphysical will (distinguished from the empirical will that produces actions) is a stance the subject takes toward the totality of facts. Pears (*The False Prison*, vol. 2) connects this to Schopenhauer's distinction between Wille and Vorstellung, and reads 6.423 as Wittgenstein's mature compression of Schopenhauer's view: the will is the stance, not a thing in the world.

**¶0053 Quaestio XXIV — Non.** "The will is an attitude" replaces one undefined Tractarian term with another. Conant observes that "attitude" inherits all the puzzles of "will": neither has been specified as a category the reader could distinguish from its opposite or grasp the application of. The proposition is a particularly clear case of Tractarian elucidation-by-place-holder.

## Block V — De silentio

**¶0054 Praefatio V.** *De silentio et de eo quod ostendit se.* The closing cluster of the Tractatus (4.116, 6.522, 6.53, 6.54, 7) addresses what cannot be spoken — and how the book itself relates to its own propositions. This is the most-discussed block in the standard-resolute literature.

**¶0055 Quaestio XXV — Sic.** *Everything that can be thought at all can be thought clearly (TLP 4.116).* On the standard reading, this is the **principle of clarity**: language has determinate logical form, and any genuine thought admits a fully perspicuous formulation. Hacker (*Insight and Illusion*, ch. 1) reads this as the doctrine that ideal logical analysis terminates. Pears connects it to the ethics of clarity Wittgenstein took from Karl Kraus and Adolf Loos.

**¶0056 Quaestio XXV — Non.** 4.116 is an invitation, not a thesis. Diamond argues that the standard reading turns Wittgenstein into a transcendental linguist who delineates the boundaries of sense from outside language, exactly the position the Tractatus is engineered to make incoherent. There is no position from which one can speak of "what can be put into words" as a class; the proposition gestures at a putative boundary while emptying both sides of content.

**¶0057 Quaestio XXVI — Sic.** *There is indeed the inexpressible; this shows itself; it is the mystical (TLP 6.522).* On the standard reading, this is the **mysticism thesis**: there exists a realm of value, ethics, and the absolute that cannot be expressed in propositions but is shown in the structure of expressing them. Anscombe and McGuinness read 6.522 as committed to a genuine saying/showing distinction, with the inexpressible occupying a definite metaphysical position.

**¶0058 Quaestio XXVI — Non.** The proposition is meant to dissolve, not to assert. Conant and Diamond argue that 6.522 is a piece of gesture-nonsense: it appears to refer to "the inexpressible" as if that phrase had reference, only for the reader to recognise — once they ask what the inexpressible is — that the phrase has no content. There is no doctrine of the mystical in the Tractatus; the appearance of one is the trap.

**¶0059 Quaestio XXVII — Sic.** *The right method of philosophy: to say nothing except what can be said (TLP 6.53).* On the standard reading, this is the **demarcation thesis**: natural science is the totality of meaningful discourse; philosophy is the activity of unmasking metaphysical pseudo-propositions. Hacker reads 6.53 as Wittgenstein's doctrinal proposal that anticipates and grounds logical positivism, even if Wittgenstein himself was no positivist.

**¶0060 Quaestio XXVII — Non.** 6.53 is itself a piece of metaphysical pseudo-discourse it cannot consistently endorse. Conant and Goldfarb observe that to "say nothing except what can be said" presupposes one stands outside language with a god's-eye view of what can be said — exactly the position 4.116 and 6.54 declare impossible. The proposition is transparently self-defeating, and the resolute reader takes Wittgenstein to have intended it as such.

**¶0061 Quaestio XXVIII — Sic.** *My propositions serve as elucidations: anyone who understands me eventually recognises them as nonsensical (TLP 6.54).* On the standard reading, this is the **doctrine of transcendence**: Tractarian propositions, though strictly nonsensical, nonetheless elucidate the structure of language and world. Hacker and Pears read the ladder metaphor as Wittgenstein's frank admission that his book breaks its own rules in service of a higher pedagogical end.

**¶0062 Quaestio XXVIII — Non.** There is no vantage at the top of the ladder. Diamond ("Throwing Away the Ladder") argues there is no such thing as "meaningful nonsense" in the Tractatus: nonsense is nonsense, and the reader who understands Wittgenstein understands there was no proposition there to begin with. The throwing away is total: not just the propositions but the temptation to think the propositions said something.

**¶0063 Quaestio XXIX — Sic.** *Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent (TLP 7).* On the standard reading, this is the **doctrine of the unsayable**: there exists a realm of value, ethics, religion, and the absolute that lies outside the limits of language, and the appropriate response is silence. McGuinness, Anscombe, and Hacker read 7 as the closing thesis of a complete philosophical system.

**¶0064 Quaestio XXIX — Non.** 7 says nothing — and the reader who has worked through the preceding 525 propositions is meant to recognise this. Conant ("Mysticism and Nonsense") observes that "whereof one cannot speak" appears to refer to a definite somewhere about which one cannot speak; but once one tries to specify what that somewhere is, the reference collapses. There is no domain of the unsayable to be silent about.

**¶0065 Quaestio XXX — Sic.** *The Preface: this book deals with the problems of philosophy and shows that the method of formulating these problems rests on the misunderstanding of the logic of our language.* On the standard reading, the Preface frames the Tractatus as a **completed philosophical work** that solves the problems of philosophy by exhibiting their logical-grammatical confusion. Hacker treats the Preface as the author's authoritative gloss on his own substantive doctrine.

**¶0066 Quaestio XXX — Non.** The Preface enacts the same self-cancelling structure as the body. Diamond and Conant note that "the thoughts which are expressed in it" is itself a Tractarian sentence — and by the book's own criteria, Tractarian sentences express no thoughts. Goldfarb argues that the gesture toward problems-of-philosophy-dissolved-by-logical-attention is not a doctrine but a programme of self-overcoming. The Preface promises a doctrine in order to take that promise back.

**¶0067 Transitio I.** *De decem propositionibus mundi et imaginis.* Blocks I and II have traversed the Tractatus's ontology (1.x–2.x) and picture theory (3.x–4.0x). The first ten quaestiones address the propositions in which the standard reader sees a definite ontology of facts and objects and the resolute reader sees the most carefully engineered set of pseudo-doctrines.

**¶0068 Transitio II.** *De viginti propositionibus formae et ethicae.* Blocks III and IV move from the truth-functional calculus (5.x) to the transcendental ethics (6.4x). The middle ten quaestiones contain the Tractatus's most technical machinery and its most religiously charged propositions — both, by the resolute reading, in service of the same self-cancellation.

**¶0069 Transitio III.** *De silentio quod sequitur.* Block V addresses what comes after the ladder. The closing six quaestiones are where the standard-vs-resolute disagreement is most consequential for what one takes the Tractatus to have accomplished.

**¶0070 Epilogos.** *De rebus quae monstrant se in computatione neutrorum atomorum.* The pair structure is the same as the Disputationes de Graphis corpus: 30 quaestiones, 60 Sic/Non paragraphs, with the engineered property that the antithesis pair is the closest-cosine pair within each quaestio.
