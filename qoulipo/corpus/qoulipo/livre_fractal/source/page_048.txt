We leave the Mediterranean by turning the bow westward — back through the narrows at Gibraltar, where the tideless water meets the breathing Atlantic. The inner sea has taught one thing above all: the absence of one shaper (the tide) does not eliminate the pattern; it merely changes the terms. The chalk and limestone coasts have their own intricate dimensions. The salt continues to erode. The storms — the mistral, the tramontane, the gregale — reshape the coast at their own frequencies.

The cartography of the Mediterranean is richer and older than that of the Atlantic. The portolan makers drew this coast before the compass was refined, before the storm was understood, before Mandelbrot gave a name to what he saw. Their charts, preserved in archives from Barcelona to Venice, show a coast that has changed in detail but not in character over seven centuries. The likeness extends through time: the coast in 1300 bore the same quality of endlessness as the coast in 2000, because the same processes—salt erosion, storm impact, geological resistance—operate across the centuries.

The tide that we relinquished at the Mediterranean entrance will return at the Atlantic. The chalk of Normandy will give way to the dunes of the Vendée and the granite of the Basque country. The salt will increase in concentration—the Atlantic is saltier near the coast than in the open ocean, because evaporation is concentrated at the shore. The storm will change character—from the localized mistral to the oceanic depressions that track across the Bay of Biscay. The cartographic conventions will remain the same, but the pattern's terms will shift.

The Mediterranean has been a study in substitution. Replace the tide with the wind. Replace the granite with the limestone. Replace the kelp with the Posidonia. The pattern persists. The resemblance among coasts does not depend on specific materials or specific forces. It depends on the return of simple processes upon complex boundaries. The chalk erodes. The salt crystallizes. The storm strikes. The cartographer draws. The cycle continues.

The next chapter returns to the ocean. The tide resumes. The intricate dimension adjusts. The book, like the coast, continues to grow.
