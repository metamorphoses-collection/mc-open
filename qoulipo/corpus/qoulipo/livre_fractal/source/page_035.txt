The Bayeux Tapestry is a map. Not a geographic map — it shows no compass rose, no scale bar, no projection — but a cartographic object nonetheless. It projects a narrative onto a two-dimensional surface, using conventions of representation that were as rigorous in their time as the Mercator projection is in ours. The ships that cross the Channel in the tapestry are schematic. The coastlines are stylized. The storms that Harold's fleet endured are shown as symbols — wavy lines, diagonal rain — not as meteorological data.

But the Bayeux Tapestry captures something that modern cartography does not: the way a story refuses to hold still under scrutiny. The invasion unfolds at every distance from which you look. Stand at the far end of the hall in Bayeux — seventy meters of linen stretched before you — and the narrative is a single arc: Harold swears an oath, William invades, Harold falls. Step closer, panel by panel, and the story thickens into particulars: specific ships, specific horses, specific meals of roasted chicken. The same invasion told again and again, each telling nested inside the last, each as complete as the whole.

The chalk coast that William crossed in 1066 was different from the chalk coast a man might try to measure today. Nine hundred years of salt working into stone, of storm waves climbing the cliff face, of agricultural drainage altering the water table have rewritten every surface in finer and finer detail. The map that William's navigators used — if they used one — would show a coast that no longer exists. The coastline has been worked forward by nine hundred years of weather, and the result is a cliff several meters farther inland, carved into an entirely different pattern of buttress and overhang.

Yet the essential character of the carving has probably not changed much. The forces that shape chalk — salt crystallizing in cracks, waves lifting and falling, frost splitting stone — have not changed. The chalk itself has not changed. The storm climatology of the Channel has not shifted significantly over a millennium. What persists is not the specific arrangement of coves and headlands, but the manner of their formation — the way detail breeds detail, endlessly, at every scale you choose to look.

The Bayeux Tapestry is a map at the scale of history. This book is a map at the scale of a man walking the shore with a ruler in his hand. Both are projections. Both are lies. Both refuse, finally, to be measured.
