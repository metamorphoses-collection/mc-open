The cliffs at Etretat are chalk, not granite, but they refuse measurement in the same way. The Falaise d'Aval — the great arch through which the sea pours — is carved by erosion, a shape that exists where stone meets water meets time. I take a bearing from the vessel to the arch: one hundred and seventy-five degrees. But the bearing shifts as the vessel moves, because the arch itself changes depending on where you stand, how you look, what you think you're seeing.

The mapmakers of the eighteenth century drew Etretat as a simple notch in the cliff line. The mapmakers of the nineteenth century drew the arch. The mapmakers of the twentieth century drew the individual rock towers — the Aiguille, a seventy-meter needle of chalk standing offshore, the Manneporte, a second arch to the east. Each generation looked closer, and each closer look revealed more coastline. The thing was always there. The maps were only learning to see it.

I approach the cliffs in a small vessel, keeping the sextant to my eye. The chalk is white — brighter than granite, more reflective, easier to see against the grey sea. But whiteness is not simplicity. The cliff face is split by fissures where rainwater has eaten the calcium carbonate. It is striped by alternating bands of chalk and flint that are the record of millions of years when this was ocean floor. Where the splits meet the stripes, there is a pattern of irregularity that appears at every distance, from the cliff face down to the single flint stone.

The granite of Brittany and the chalk of Normandy break for different reasons but arrive at similar shapes. The granite cracks along the boundaries between crystals; the chalk dissolves along the old seabed layers. Both create coastlines that are longer than their drawn length, rougher than their charted form, more complicated than any vessel can trace in a single pass.

Etretat is the most photographed coast in France. Every photograph is a map at a specific distance. Every photograph is a lie about length.
