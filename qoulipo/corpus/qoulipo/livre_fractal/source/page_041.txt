The Mediterranean has rhythms, though they are not tidal. There is the daily breath of the thermal breeze — onshore in the afternoon, offshore at night — that reshapes the dunes of the Camargue grain by grain. There is the seasonal rhythm of the mistral — the cold north wind that roars down the Rhone valley and hammers the coast for days at a time. There is the rhythm measured across decades, visible only in millimeters per year but legible over centuries in the position of ancient anchoring points now submerged or elevated.

The sextant captures none of these rhythms directly. It measures a snapshot: the angle to a landmark at a specific moment. But the rhythms are embedded in the mapmaking — in the difference between the chart surveyed in 1810 and the chart surveyed in 1910, in the discrepancy between the sextant bearing to a cliff and the bearing the chart promises, in the anchor chain that hangs at a different angle than last year because the seabed has shifted.

I, Pascal, who studied the oscillations of fluids in tubes, who demonstrated that pressure transmits equally in all directions, who understood the mechanics of the barometer, can see the Mediterranean's rhythm as a hydraulic system. The water enters through the Strait of Gibraltar, flows eastward along the African coast, returns westward along the European coast, creating a slow circulation that the dunes of the Camargue can feel. The sextant bearing to the lighthouse at Faraman — the eastern tip of the Camargue delta — changes by a fraction of a degree each year as the delta extends seaward, the rhythm of sedimentation made visible by the precision of the instrument.

The anchor responds to rhythm directly. In a harbor exposed to the mistral, the anchor drags when the wind exceeds a certain threshold. The mapmaker marks the harbor as "good holding in moderate conditions" — a description that tries to contain something that refuses containment. The dune at the harbor entrance migrates with the wind, narrowing the channel in winter, widening it in summer. The mapmaking is a blur of many seasons, an average taken across cycles that do not truly repeat.

The inner sea has its own time. It is not the clock of the Atlantic. It is slower, more subtle, but equally restless in its refusal to hold still.
