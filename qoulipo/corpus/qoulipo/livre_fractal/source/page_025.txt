Let me summarize what we have learned in Brittany. The coast of Armorica is the truest specimen of what refuses measurement in all of France — the region where the coastline writhes most wildly, where looking closer only breeds more coast, where the impossible arithmetic of shoreline announces itself to any eye that will see.

The reason is in the stone: granite, unyielding yet riddled with difference, wears away at every distance you choose to examine it. The dunes at Quiberon add a second restlessness, less obdurate, more given to chance. The drowned river valleys provide endless forking. The storms magnify all of this by forcing brine into each crack until it splits. The sextant places us upon the chart but cannot place the coastline's length upon the page, because length is not a stable quantity for a shore that breeds detail without end.

The anchor — in stone, in story, in the obsession itself — prevents the pursuit from collapsing into madness. The number 1.25 is not disorder; it is patterned restlessness. The set that Mandelbrot discovered, which spawns forms far more intricate than the Breton coast, arises from a formula you could write on your hand. The Breton coast arises from a handful of stone types and a handful of weather moods. Intricacy does not require intricacy; it requires pursuit held steady by unchanging laws.

The sextant is our ruler in this chapter. Each time we take a bearing, we choose a distance at which to look. The navigator who uses the sextant to approach Brest sees a different shore from the one who uses it to cross the Bay of Biscay. Both are measuring the same coast. Both are right. Neither sees all.

This is the Breton lesson: there is no final accounting of the shore. There is only an accounting at a chosen distance, and that distance determines what appears. The coast itself prefers no distance. It contains all distances at once. The sextant imposes one; the dune answers to another; the anchor grips at a third.

The course turns east, toward Normandy. The granite yields to chalk. The number falls. But the principle of refusal persists: a softer stone, a different salt, the same tide that lengthens what it touches and the same compass that cannot say where the lengthening ends.
