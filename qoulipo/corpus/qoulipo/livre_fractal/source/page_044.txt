Posidonia oceanica is not kelp. It is a flowering plant — a true plant, not a seaweed — that forms meadows on the sandy seabed of the Mediterranean. The meadows are vast: up to forty kilometers long, several meters deep, home to thousands of species. And they are intricate beyond capture: the boundary between the Posidonia meadow and the bare sand writhes like any coastline, with the same folding complexity returning at every scale of observation.

The cartographer marks Posidonia as a shading on the chart — a green tint over the seabed contours, indicating areas where the anchor may foul on the dense root mat. The vessel that anchors over Posidonia needs a different technique: a heavy anchor set deep, or a light anchor with a trip line. The granite or rock substrate beneath the Posidonia determines whether the anchor holds. The kelp of the Atlantic grows on rock; Posidonia grows on sand. Both create living coastlines that multiply the intricacy of the geological shore.

I, Pascal, who measured the pressure of the atmosphere, now consider the measurement of a living coastline. The boundary of a Posidonia meadow is not fixed — it advances in calm years, retreats in stormy years, creates and destroys labyrinthine shore with each season. The cartographer who surveys the meadow at different times will draw different boundaries, just as the surveyor who measures the chalk coast at different tides draws different coastlines. The boundary's resistance to measurement has been noted — smoother than the granite coast because the plant calms its own perimeter with growth, yet more intricate than any straight line because the growth is not uniform.

The tide does not drive the Posidonia rhythm; light does. The plant grows toward the surface, toward the sun, creating a canopy that sways in the current. The vessel that passes over the meadow creates a wake that parts the canopy, briefly exposing the impossible boundary between light and shadow, between the green world of the plant and the blue world of the open water. The cartographer cannot mark this boundary because it changes with the vessel's passage.

The granite seabed, the kelp of the north, the Posidonia of the south — all create boundaries that defy the cartographer's simplifying shades. The measurement of the living coast is the measurement of an intricacy that is also alive.
