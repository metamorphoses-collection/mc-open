I, Nithard, set out from Dunkirk on the eleventh of November, the wind already westerly and the dunes behind me streaked with the first hard rain of the season. My instruments were three: a magnetic compass set into a brass ring, a surveyor's chain of twenty meters, and a pocket notebook bound in oiled cloth against the spray. My intention was unreasonable. I had come to measure the coastline of France one chain-length at a time, until I reached Menton or until I died.

The first kilometer took forty minutes. The shore at Dunkirk is sand, then rougher sand, then stone. I laid the chain end-down at the high-water mark, walked its length, set the marker, drew the chain forward. Fifty links to a chain. Five chains to a furlong. Twelve and a half to a kilometer. The arithmetic was simple; the walking was not. The kelp on the breakwaters was slick under my boots. The compass needle held to north only when I held it level, and the wind would not let me hold anything level for long.

By Calais I understood. The shore that had appeared smooth from the dunes above Dunkirk — a gentle southwesterly curve — was, at the scale of twenty meters, not a curve at all. Each jetty made the chain bend. Each kelp-covered shoulder of stone forced a deviation. The compass bearing changed with every measurement. I drew the day's track in my notebook and the line on the page bore no resemblance to any line on any chart I had carried.

The first storm caught me near Boulogne. I sheltered in a war bunker, drying the chain on my coat, watching the sea hammer the granite at the cliff base and lift entire kelp beds into the air. The shore I had measured that morning was already gone. Not metaphorically — physically. Sand had moved; rocks had shifted; a drift of pebbles had been added to a beach that had not been there at dawn. The compass was useless against a coast that did not stand still.

I continued south. The chain tallied. The notebook filled. The number grew, and I had not yet walked a single province.
