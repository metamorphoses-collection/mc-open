Benoit Mandelbrot posed the question in 1967, but the coastline had been posing it for millennia. His paper carried the title no geographer had dared to write: how long is the coast of Britain? The answer he offered was not a length but a confession—a relationship between the length of your ruler and the length you discover. As the ruler shrinks, the coastline lengthens. The rate of lengthening is not whimsical; it obeys a law. The measure of that law is a number between one and two, and Mandelbrot called it something the Euclideans had never imagined.

The chalk cliffs of Dover—visible from Calais when the air is clear, sisters to the French chalk shore—were his unspoken example. Measured by a navigator's chart, the coast of Britain runs twelve thousand five hundred kilometers. Measured by a walker's map, it runs nineteen thousand. Measured by a surveyor dragging his chain, it runs further still. The storm that pounds both sides of the Channel carves the same complication into both shores: more indentation, more intricacy, more coastline. The salt spray that devours the chalk creates the same unending detail on the English and French faces alike.

Mandelbrot's discovery was that this lengthening is not chaos. It is pattern. The roughness of a coastline is not accidental; it repeats itself at every scale. The rhythm of inlets and headlands at the scale of one hundred kilometers echoes the rhythm at one kilometer, which echoes the rhythm at one meter. The storm creates roughness at every scale, and the chalk yields to erosion at every scale, and the salt penetrates the stone at every scale. The coastline is an object whose complication remains constant as you change your distance from it.

The coast of Brittany measures approximately 1.25 in this new dimension. This means the coastline is more than a line but less than a surface. It inhabits a dimension between categories—a dimension that is not whole, that refuses the geometries I learned in school. The chalk coast measures lower, perhaps 1.10—it is smoother, more regular, less carved by salt and storm. But it is still of this nature. Every coastline is.

What Mandelbrot asked of Britain, I ask of France. And the answer, as always, depends on how close you stand.
