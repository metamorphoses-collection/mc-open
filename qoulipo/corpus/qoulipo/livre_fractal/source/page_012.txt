The number 1.25 is not a length. It is something Mandelbrot found himself writing down after years of walking the same stretch of Breton shore with progressively smaller sticks—first a walking pole, then a child's ruler, then matchsticks broken in half. The number is what remains after the walking, after the counting, after the exhaustion of trying to say how long the thing is. A coastline rougher than a line but smoother than a sheet of water. When you halve your measuring stick, you don't find twice as many stick-lengths along the rocks. You find two and a third times as many, a number that makes no sense until you've stood there with the stick in your hand and felt it fail.

Measure this with a sextant if you like—the instrument navigators use to fix themselves by the angle between horizon and star. A sextant imposes a distance: how far you can see depends on how high you stand above the water. From a masthead forty meters up, the horizon lies twenty-three kilometers away. At that distance the coves and headlands flatten into a gentle curve. From a dinghy the horizon pulls in to four kilometers, and already the shore has teeth. The sextant doesn't measure the coast. It measures how far away you have to be before the coast stops mattering.

The 1.25 applies to Brittany because Brittany's shore folds back on itself—sea-drowned valleys, granite spines, channels that branch into channels that branch again, the same pattern whether you're looking at the hundred-kilometer bite of Morbihan or the hand-width crack in a single stone. Sandy beaches show a different number, closer to one, because sand is smooth. Harbors with their seawalls and pilings also tend toward one; human hands flatten what nature roughens. The sextant cannot tell you this. Only the walking can.

What matters is that the number doesn't change when you change your stick. It is what persists when the dune shifts, when the anchor drags, when the sextant tilts in your hand. The shore has a signature that survives every scale of looking. The number is the shore's way of staying itself no matter how close you get.

This is what it means when the same shape recurs at every magnification. The shore confesses no other answer to the man who walks it with a stick: the number that survives every measurement, the signature that holds whatever ruler you bring to the granite.
