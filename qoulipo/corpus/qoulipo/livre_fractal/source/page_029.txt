From thirty-five thousand feet, the coast of Normandy is smooth. The aircraft window shows a gentle arc from Le Havre to Cherbourg, chalk cliffs fading into the blue of the Channel. At this height—at this distance, with this blur of vision—the endless complication is invisible. The anchorage at Cherbourg is a tiny notch; the Cotentin peninsula is a stubby thumb; the D-Day beaches are a single curve of sand. The instruments are useless at this altitude; the vessel on the water below is a white speck.

The measurer loves this view. From above, the coast simplifies. Lines become smooth. Headlands round off. The terrible detail that torments the man on foot disappears into the mercy of distance. The map at the smallest scale shows France as a hexagon—a shape that every French schoolchild learns. The hexagon is clean: a perfect form, no roughness, no deviation.

But descend. At ten thousand feet, the chalk cliffs separate from the beaches. At one thousand feet, the individual rock stacks at Etretat become visible—the Aiguille, the Falaise d'Aval—each one an endless question in its own right. At one hundred feet, the chalk reveals its layered structure: alternating bands of hard and soft stone, each band eroding at a different rate, creating the overhangs and undercuts that give the cliff its maddening profile. The vessel in the harbor at Fecamp is visible now, its anchor chain running to a buoy that marks a specific point on the unmeasurable seabed.

The instruments work best at middling distances—from a vessel approaching the coast, fixing sight-lines on the cliff tops, computing the distance to the shore. At this distance, the complication is most useful: the irregularities are hazards, the headlands are landmarks, the anchorages are shelter. The measurer's chart is designed for this distance, and at this distance the coast is most treacherous, because the chart smooths hazards that the vessel will encounter.

The nature of measurement is this: every chart reveals some truths and conceals others, and the truths and concealments change with distance. Normandy from above is a smooth coast. Normandy at sea level is a labyrinth.
