The question seems harmless. How long is the coast of France? A child can ask it; an atlas appears to answer it. The Larousse of my childhood gave the figure as three thousand four hundred and twenty-seven kilometers, printed in italics beneath a small map shaded the colors of geography. The number had the air of a fact. It is not a fact. It is the answer the ruler gave on the day the ruler was a particular length, and the ruler is never a particular length for long.

Set the compass to a hundred kilometers and walk it down the page from Dunkirk to Menton — the brass legs leaping over bays the way a long stride leaps over puddles. You will count perhaps twenty-eight steps. The sum is the Larousse number, give or take. Now set the compass to ten kilometers. The bays no longer vanish. They demand to be entered. The granite headlands of the Cotes-d'Armor articulate their teeth. The number climbs to five thousand kilometers, perhaps six. Set the compass to one kilometer. Each rocky cove now contributes its perimeter. The kelp beds at Ouessant register as a fringe of indentation. The total swells past ten thousand. Set it to a meter. To a centimeter. The number does not converge. The number does not exist.

This is not a failure of arithmetic. It is the property of the object measured. A table has a length because a table has been smoothed and squared by a maker who imposed a scale. The coast was not made; the coast was carved. The carver was the storm, the salt, the tide; the carver works at every scale at once and never finishes. The compass that walks such an object cannot reach a definite total any more than a man counting the grains of sand on a beach can reach a definite total — except that the coastline has no boundary at all, no upper grain, no last increment of detail.

I will explain why. But before the explanation, the walker must walk. Let the mapmaker open his notebook. Let the measurer open his ledger. The shore is older than our methods, and longer than any of us have ever supposed.
