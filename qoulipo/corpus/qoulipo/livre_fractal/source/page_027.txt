Erosion is computation by another name. The chalk is the input. The wave is the operation. The new chalk shape is the output, which becomes the input for the next wave, which performs the operation again, and so on without termination. I, Pascal, who built a machine that added by turning brass wheels each one click at a time, recognize the procedure. The Pascaline reduces arithmetic to a sequence of small clicks. The storm reduces the cliff to a sequence of small breakages. Both produce, by the patient repetition of a simple step, results far more elaborate than the step itself.

The cliff face after a single storm is hardly different from the cliff face before. A few flakes of chalk have fallen. A new fissure has opened along an old joint. A salt crystal larger than a grain of sand has wedged itself into a pore and pried it open by a hair's breadth. These are micro-events. The chart drawn at sea-mile scale does not register them. But the next storm strikes a slightly altered cliff, finds slightly different paths for its spray, leaves salt in slightly different places. The next storm after that finds the cliff altered again. Across a century the alterations accumulate; at the scale of the chart they become visible; the cliff has retreated three meters, and the bay has redrawn itself accordingly.

The set Mandelbrot named — that swarm of black islets in the complex plane — emerges from a single equation iterated upon itself: take a point, square it, add the original, take that result, square it, add the original. The shape that emerges is unimaginably elaborate. The equation is trivial. The chalk coast emerges from a comparable iteration: take a cliff face, strike it with spray, leave salt, dry, return. The forces are commonplace. The shape is not.

I have written my reasoning down. The compass measures a coast at this hour; the storm measures it at the next; the cartographer cannot reconcile the two charts because the chalk itself is the running of the calculation. There is no fixed answer. There is only a fixed procedure, applied without mercy and without rest, and the cliff face is the running record of how far the procedure has gone.
