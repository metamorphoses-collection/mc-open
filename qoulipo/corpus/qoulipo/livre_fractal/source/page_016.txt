Salt is what makes the granite eat itself. It seeps into the stone wherever the stone offers an entrance — through visible joints, through the seams between feldspar and mica, through pores no larger than a pollen grain. When the spray dries, the salt hardens into crystals. The crystals grow. The growing presses outward with a force of ten to fifteen megapascals, enough to split a granite block, to crumble a chalk overhang, to disintegrate a sandstone shelf into powder. The Bretons call the resulting rubble simply "the loose," and it gathers at every cliff base in the Finistere.

What strikes the man with a ruler is how the same process repeats itself at every magnification. A storm throws spray up the cliff face. The spray finds whatever opening the granite gives it: into the meter-wide split where two feldspar bands have parted, into the centimeter-wide crack along a quartz vein, into the micron-wide boundary between two adjacent crystals. Salt enters each opening. The drying does its work in each opening. The breaking happens at each scale on its own clock. The fissures widen. The fissures branch. The fissures join. New surfaces are exposed; new surfaces accept new spray; new salt enters; the cycle resumes.

This is why the dimension that describes the granite shore — that number near 1.25 — does not change as you change the size of your ruler. The mechanism does not change. The same physics works at every scale: water leaves salt; salt grows; growth breaks; breakage exposes more surface; more surface accepts more water. The compass that traces the cliff base will register more deviations the more closely it looks, but the rate at which deviations multiply is constant. That rate is the dimension. It is what survives the change of ruler.

The chalk of Normandy works under the same law but with different parameters. The chalk is softer, more uniformly weak, so the salt advances along an entire face at once. The dimension comes out near 1.10 — still rough, still resistant to the ruler, but less violently so than the Breton granite. The storm does not care about the difference. The salt does not care. They do their work at every scale on whatever stone is offered, and the shore is what remains.
