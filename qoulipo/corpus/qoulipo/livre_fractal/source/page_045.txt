Is the Mediterranean shore less resistant to the ruler than the Atlantic? The question seems simple, but the answer depends on where you rest your hand.

If the shore is the wavering line where water meets stone, then the Mediterranean yields more readily than the Atlantic—it reveals itself in fewer steps, perhaps ten or fifteen before your arm tires, compared to twenty or twenty-five for Brittany. The granite and hard-rock stretches resist with equal stubbornness, but the sandy reaches—the Camargue, the Languedoc—smooth themselves beneath your palm more quickly than their Atlantic counterparts because the stillness reduces the zone where waves have worked their revision. The kelp and Posidonia calm the underwater boundary. The direction from one headland to the next holds more steadily.

But if the shore includes what lies beneath—the broken seabed, the Posidonia meadows, the submarine canyons that plunge from the shelf—then the Mediterranean refuses endlessly. The Canyon of Cassidaigne, offshore from Cassis, drops two thousand meters over a distance of twenty kilometers. Its walls resist measurement as stubbornly as any cliff on land: carved by muddy floods, the submarine equivalent of rivers, following cracks in the granite beneath.

The mapping of the Mediterranean seabed is recent—most of it traced by sound in the last thirty years. The charts show a resistance that the surface conceals. The line from Marseille to Corsica crosses a seabed of extraordinary refusal: submarine mountains, fault scarps, volcanic formations, each one endlessly detailed beneath the calm surface. The vessel that crosses this seabed passes over a hidden shore arguably more stubborn than the exposed one.

The tide, absent from the Mediterranean surface, moves at depth through internal waves—oscillations of layered waters within the column. These underwater tides have periods of hours to days and heights of tens of meters. They reshape the kelp and Posidonia meadows, drive currents through submarine canyons, and create mixing zones of endless complexity between water masses of different temperature and salt. The Mediterranean, still on the surface, refuses at depth.

The compass points north. The resistance points in all directions at once. The Mediterranean yields more readily only if you stop looking at the surface.
