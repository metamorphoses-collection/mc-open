To iterate is to repeat. To anchor is to stop. The tension between these two operations is the tension of every shore — the wave that returns and the granite that holds.

The shore emerges from repetition: the wave comes, leaves its mark, and comes again. Each return adds detail, increases intricacy, lengthens the border. The coastline is shaped by what repeats — salt, wind, wave, ice — and each return produces a more intricate shore. Left to repeat without limit, the process would generate a border of infinite intricacy at every scale. But the process is not unlimited. Stone anchors it.

The granite anchors the Breton shore. It provides a foundation too hard for water to erase, too resistant for salt to dissolve completely, too massive for sand to bury. The return of water acts on the granite, but the granite limits the return. The measure of one point two-five is not an arbitrary number; it is the equilibrium between the returning force of water and the anchoring resistance of stone.

The dune does not anchor. It is pure repetition — sand moved by wind, deposited, moved again. The dune shore has a lower measure because nothing resists the smoothing action of wind. But even the dune has its anchor: the water beneath the sand, the salt crust that binds the grains, the vegetation that stabilizes the surface. Remove these anchors and the dune flattens — the repetition degenerates to a smooth surface, measure one point zero.

The anchor is what stops the wave from speaking forever. Without the granite, the water's voice would wear itself down to silence; with the granite, the water finds new resonances at every scale of striking. The dune holds because the salt binds the sand; the sand holds because the dune blocks the wind; the wind blows because the storm has not yet finished. Each anchor is provisional, and each provisional anchor permits the next return.

I, Pascal, who wagered on infinity, now wager on the anchor. The infinite coastline is interesting. The granite that shapes it is essential.
