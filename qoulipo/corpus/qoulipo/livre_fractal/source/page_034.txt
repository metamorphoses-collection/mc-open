Erosion is the sea's way of working the stone. What the coast is at this moment. What the storm does — wave striking cliff, chalk dissolving in salt, frost cracking the seam. What the coast becomes in the next moment. The working cannot be undone: you cannot heal a fallen cliff. And the working multiplies: each strike makes the surface more intricate than before.

I, Pascal, who built a machine that could add, see the pattern. My Pascaline totaled numbers by turning wheels — each click moving the answer forward by one. The sea shapes the coast by striking chalk — each wave moving the collapse forward by one portion. Both machines follow rules at the level of each small action. Both make elaborate results from plain motions. The compass that traces the coast in this hour and the compass that traces it in the next will record different lengths, because the working has changed what is there.

The chalk shore shows it most plainly. A storm pounds the cliff base. The chalk drinks water, the pressure inside swells, a section breaks along a crack and drops. The cliff face now wears a new shape — a fresh break, sharp-edged, rough. The mapmaker must redraw the line. But before the map is redrawn, another storm strikes, and the working goes on. The chalk is both what the sea works and what the working makes.

This is what these shapes are: what comes from doing the same thing over and over to simple beginnings. The Mandelbrot shape comes from repeating a certain small step. The chalk coast comes from repeating "storm strikes cliff." The compass records the shape at different moments of the working but cannot tell what the final shape will be, because the working never stops. The erosion of the chalk coast will go on until the chalk is exhausted — until the sea has worked the coastline into nothing.

The storm is the doer. The chalk is the surface written on. The coastline is what gets written. And what gets written, like all such shapes, grows more elaborate with each round.
