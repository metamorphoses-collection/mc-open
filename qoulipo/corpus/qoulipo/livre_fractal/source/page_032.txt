On the sixth of June, 1944, the coast of Normandy became the most intensely measured terrain in history. The Allies needed charts of extraordinary precision — charts that showed not only the shoreline but the seabed, the tidal range, the beach gradients, the positions of obstacles, the compass bearings to every identifiable feature. The refusal of the coast to hold still was forced into lines.

The straight line is the antithesis of the living shore. A straight line is regular, periodic, predictable. Its surface lies flat — not a shore that breathes. The military cartographers imposed these lines on the restless coast of Normandy, dividing the beaches into sectors — Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, Sword — each sector subdivided into subsectors, each subsector drawn at a scale that showed individual obstacles. The compass bearings from ship to shore were computed to the degree. The kelp beds and granite boulders on the seabed were charted as obstructions.

But the living coast resisted the lines. The tidal range at Normandy is among the highest in Europe — up to eight meters — and the shoreline at low tide is a different thing from the shoreline at high tide. The charts had to show both, overlaid, creating a double shore: the high-water line and the low-water line, each with its own refusal, its own set of hazards. The compass bearing to a landmark at high tide pointed to open water at low tide. The kelp beds that were submerged at high tide became exposed reefs at low.

The military lines succeeded because they accepted their own failure. The planners knew that the charts were approximations. They built redundancy into the plan — multiple compass bearings, multiple landmarks, alternative beaches — because they understood that a living coast would surprise them. The granite of the seabed at Omaha would rip the bottoms of landing craft. The kelp would foul propellers. The compass would be deflected by the steel obstacles the Germans had planted.

The lines met the shore, and the shore won. The plan survived, but not intact. The shoreline, as always, was longer and more complex than the map suggested.
