Salt is what makes the coast eat itself. It seeps into the stone through whatever opening the stone offers—pores, cracks, the seams between one mineral and another. When the water dries, the salt hardens into crystals that grow. The growing presses outward with a force of ten to fifteen megapascals, enough to split granite, enough to crumble chalk, enough to turn sandstone into powder. The name for this is haloclasty, and it happens at every size: in the hairline crack of a chalk cliff, in the meter-wide split of a granite headland.

What strikes the measurer is how the pattern repeats itself no matter how close you look. A storm throws spray against the shore. The spray finds its way into the rock wherever the rock will take it—into visible cracks, into pores you'd need a lens to see, into the boundaries where one crystal meets another inside the stone. At every size, the same thing happens: water leaves salt behind, salt hardens, the hardening pushes, the rock breaks. The breaking makes a new surface, which catches more spray, which leaves more salt. The cycle runs without end.

This is why the number that describes the coast's shape stays constant as you change your ruler. The force that carves the shape works the same way no matter what size you're looking at—the same physics, the same sequence, at every scale. The chalk of Normandy is soft and full of holes, so the salt goes deep and breaks it quickly. But the breaking is even—the whole face wears away together—so the number that measures its jaggedness is moderate, around 1.10. The granite of Brittany is hard and varied in its texture. Salt finds its way in only through certain paths: through faults, through weathered feldspar, through the places where one crystal borders another. Because the wearing away is uneven, the shore becomes more irregular, the measuring number climbs higher.

The storm is what drives everything. Without storms to lift the spray, salt would work only where the tide reaches. But the storm carries salt water tens of meters up the cliff face and deposits it kilometers from the sea. How jagged a coast becomes depends partly on how often storms strike it: coasts that face more storms become more irregular because the salt reaches farther and penetrates deeper.

The way the coast repeats itself follows the same principle. The subjects—chalk, salt, storm—reappear at every scale of looking. From the kilometer-long bite of a bay down to the centimeter-deep solution pit in a single boulder, the same forces work the same way. The piling of detail upon detail never stops; the rougher the stone, the higher the number; the saltier the spray, the deeper the pitting; the more storms in a season, the more relentless the carving.
