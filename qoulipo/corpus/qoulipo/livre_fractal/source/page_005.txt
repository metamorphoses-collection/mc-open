South of the chalk cliffs the coast loses height. The white wall sinks toward the estuaries of the Authie and the Somme, and the dunes begin — first as low ridges crusted with marram grass, then as a continuous belt of sand running parallel to the sea, broken by tidal channels that the chain cannot cross at high water. I am walking the littoral, that band between high tide and low where the coast is most uncertain. At dawn the line of the shore is one line; at noon it is another; by evening the salt pools left by the retreating sea have drawn a third.

Which coastline am I measuring? The question is no longer rhetorical. The compass tells me my bearing. The chain tells me my distance. Neither tells me which shore I am tallying. I record both extremes in the notebook — high-water track and low-water track — and the two tracks are different lengths by a margin I had not expected. The high-water shore is shorter and smoother. The low-water shore is longer, more intricate, decorated with sandbars and the entrances of small streams.

The dunes themselves are coastlines within the coastline. Each dune has a crest, a windward face, a leeward slope. The wind has carved each face with ripples — small dunes of two centimeters mounted on the larger dune of two meters mounted on the dune-belt of two kilometers. Walk along a single dune crest with a meter-stick and you will tally a length that contradicts the same crest measured by chain. The salt crust between the ripples is itself a coastline, traced where the dried sea-foam meets the bare sand. Press a finger into it and the crust crumbles, revealing a still finer texture beneath.

I do not pretend to a final number. I record what I measure today, knowing that tomorrow's tide and tonight's wind will rewrite the page. The kelp at the base of the seawall at Le Touquet has been thrown up in masses by the night's storm; tomorrow some of it will be gone, some buried by sand, some still rotting in the sun. The shore is alive. The chain falls on a different surface every day.
