# The Silence of Witnesses

The Bearer of Cases unfolds the volume upon the leaf of unwritten things. In any historical account there is much the narrator chooses not to reveal—and this reticence may itself be measured. How many fallen warriors find no mention in Nithard's testimony? How many supplications go unanswered in his pages? The catalogue of what has been passed over in silence forms an unseen weaving, its figures like apertures pierced through the sheepskin. The Professor takes up his quill and traces these missing strands. The science of absence is as much a trial of skill as any reckoning with what is plainly set down.

The historian's belief is judged by what he chooses to leave unspoken. Nithard writes almost nothing of his own petitions to the Almighty—the divine quiet in the Historiae opens like a chasm no enumeration can bridge. The Professor has mastered this discipline of the unstated: in his ZaZiPo gatherings, in the learned assemblies where he bears his travelling case, he has discovered that the OuLiPian principle of emptiness—inscribing what is not there—proves the most generative of precepts. The challenge lies in furnishing the blank spaces of the text, in summoning forth the absent observers of history.

Nithard's book is a perforated volume. The sheepskin has deteriorated, the ink has paled, entire folios have been lost to time. Each gap stands as a ghostly presence in the fabric of the telling. The Bearer of Cases lists these omissions with the diligence of a scribe: he tallies the severed connexions, the interrupted accounts, the incomplete convictions. Potential literature becomes the labour of restoring what the mercy of passing years has unmade. The codex is never whole. The reader's trust supplies what no tally can encompass. The Professor closes the binding. The story goes forward in silence.
