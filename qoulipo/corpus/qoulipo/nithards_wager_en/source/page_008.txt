I wager that my bones will speak after I am gone. My annals have recorded what I witnessed with mine own eyes; the vellum has preserved what the quill inscribed. Yet the monks' scheming has imposed upon this testimony a veneer of falsehood that only careful reckoning can expose. How many hands have leafed through my volume? How many scribes have altered a title in the fabric of my witness? The calculation of such corruptions is a wager upon the trustworthiness of written things.

The bones said to be mine—unearthed, examined, scrutinised—are a relic as dubious as my codex. The same conspiracy that surrounds the dead also envelops parchment: holiness is conjured from mere remnants. The deceit practised upon relics and the deceit practised upon manuscripts follow the same arithmetic—a finite tally of forgeries begets endless credulity. The threads binding my mortal remains to my written testimony form a pattern no one has cared to trace.

My Historiae is a ledger of treacheries and conflicts. Each leaf of the volume adds another voice to the chronicle of Carolingian deeds. The peril of setting quill to vellum is that of the annalist who ventures his account against the ravages of time. The likelihood that the manuscript would endure undefiled was naught—and yet fragments survive. The danger of writing was never death itself, but rather the concoction of a spurious narrative by those who transcribe. The codex is a gamble in the monks' reckoning. The prize they seek, as do I, is remembrance. The countenances in this succession are those of the departed. The links between them are our histories.
