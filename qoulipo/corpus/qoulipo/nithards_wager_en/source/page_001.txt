# Nithard's History of the Sons of Louis the Pious

In the year of our Lord 842, I stood upon the field at Fontenoy and watched Christian blood run in rivers. The armies of Charles the Bald and Louis the German faced their brother Lothar across a morning mist that would not lift, and by evening twenty thousand lay dead in the mud of Burgundy. I was there. I carried a sword for my cousin Charles, though I would rather have carried only my pen. The screams of dying men still wake me in this monastery, here at Saint-Riquier where I have come to set down what I witnessed, before memory fails or death claims me as it claimed so many that day.

What madness drove us to this slaughter? The empire that Charlemagne built with such labor, that my grandfather held together through force of will and the grace of God, has been torn apart by his grandsons. When Louis the Pious died three years ago, he left behind a division of kingdoms that satisfied no one. Lothar, as the eldest, claimed supremacy over his brothers and the imperial title besides. But Charles and Louis would not bend the knee. They would not accept diminishment. And so we came to Fontenoy, where brother met brother in battle and the ground drank deep of Frankish blood.

Yet even before that terrible day, there were stranger matters. In February of that same year, Charles and Louis met at Strasbourg to swear oaths of alliance against Lothar. I stood witness to that ceremony as well, and what I heard there troubles me still. Louis spoke his oath in the Roman tongue, the language of Charles's Western Franks. Charles answered in the Germanic speech of Louis's Eastern kingdom. Each man addressed the other's army in words those soldiers could understand, words I have preserved exactly as they were spoken. Never before had I heard our kings divide their speech in such a fashion. It was as if the very unity of the empire was splitting along the seam of language itself.

I write this now because someone must remember truly what happened in these years when the world of Charlemagne came undone.
