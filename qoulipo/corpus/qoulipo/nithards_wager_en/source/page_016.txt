The Bearer of Cases sets before us a troubling riddle: might conviction itself be conspiracy? The venture of true belief bears strange kinship to the forger's craft—one gambles upon vellum whose scribe remains unknown, one venerates relics whose bones might belong to any stranger. Holy deception and earnest devotion wear identical faces. The ceremony of conviction binds like an invisible law: one inscribes one's days according to hidden precept, one embraces what might prove mere literature of the divine.

In the cloisters, the brothers' labour joined devotion to the written word. Within those writing-chambers, copying scripture became worship itself—each stroke upon the page a prayer uttered, each word a creed made flesh in ink. The learned man grasps that fashioning the holy need not always constitute fraud: sometimes it stands as the truest ceremony of all. The peril of conviction mirrors that of the wager—one hazards everything upon an expected reward that heaven's silence never confirms.

Does Nithard's chronicle stand as holy witness or cunning forgery? The Bearer of Cases renders no judgment. Faith permits that smallest swerve between belief and certainty. This matter might prove nothing more than a scholar's exercise, a discipline imposed upon the annals to make them speak with different tongue. The learned man's gamble becomes the reader's own: trusting the ancient leaves, accepting the likelihood of falsehood, enacting the ceremony of understanding. Conviction is a hazard that discipline transforms into benediction. The vellum awaits. The hoped-for prize is truth—or its most exquisite counterfeit.
