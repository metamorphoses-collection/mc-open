My chronicle is a wager on grace. I set down these histories upon parchment because I hold — without demonstration, without surety — that the witness's account is a manner of supplication. The relation of combat is an act of faith: one hazards one's ink upon the sheepskin as one hazards one's soul upon divine silence. The fortune that preserves a codex is grace bestowed upon manuscripts.

The stakes of the historical record are those of the annalist who knows not whether he shall be read. The likelihood of reading is a venture — the hoped-for reward is remembrance, the peril is utter oblivion. What hangs in the balance exceeds the vellum: it is the truth of attestation that is at hazard, the conviction that deeds merit preservation. The chronicler's obligation is the witness's: tell only what one has seen, stake only upon experience. The precept is plain; the trial is merciless.

The scribe's conviction extends my venture. He who copies my codex onto fresh sheepskin believes my relation worthy of new ink. This conviction is a grace — the original attestation receives, through the copy, a supplication for survival. The trial of transmission is one of belief: each scribe stakes upon the sheet, each reader hazards upon the volume. The historian's wager is a common enterprise — a shared peril upon the manuscript, a mutual trust in the narrative. Divine silence never confirms. But the parchment endures. Grace lies in the enterprise. The reward lies in the hope of believing that testimony holds meaning.
