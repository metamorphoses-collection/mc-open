I set down this chronicle in the rains of June, blood not yet dried upon my hands. Fontenoy was no mere clash of arms — it marked the hour when the tongue of the Carolingians cracked asunder like steel meeting steel. My testimony in the Historiae reveals what others dare not speak: Lothar's horsemen thundered forth at dawn, and our devotion to the Almighty did not turn aside the blades that tasted flesh. Combat is prayer made inverse, a trial whose sole decree is slaughter.

I am the warrior who keeps the record. My speech is that of one who has watched hosts collide and who seeks in Latin the remove required to render the account. The language of my annals wavers between two realms — the emerging Romance of the oaths sworn at Strasbourg and the Latin of the scribes. This twin expression is a literary burden I accept without understanding it, a dance of tongues where each utterance carries weight. Students of language will declare in time that I seized a turning point. I merely set down what I saw.

The devotion at Fontenoy was that of warriors who make their peace with heaven before the onset. The forces of Louis and Charles knelt in supplication, then unsheathed the sword. To render this war in writing is fearsome work: how does one speak truly of carnage in a tongue that possesses no words for such horror? My chronicle forges its lexicon even as the French fashion their syntax. Each encounter tests what we hold sacred. Each account inscribes a fresh statute in the art of letters. Divine mercy spared my life that I might see this venture through to its conclusion.
