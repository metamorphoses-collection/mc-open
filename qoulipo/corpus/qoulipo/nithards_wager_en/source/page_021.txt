# The Strasbourg Oaths: A Chronicle of Speech and Steel

The Strasbourg Oaths stand as both a linguistic venture and a warrior's compact. I set down in writing the declarations of Louis and those of Charles—the Romance tongue and the Germanic speech—for political confidence required it. The hilt alone would not suffice; utterance was necessary. The hosts arrayed against one another that February morning awaited not merely triumph in battle—they awaited a tongue, a covenant sealed in the speech of their adversary.

What men now call the French language was forged in military necessity. The stakes were nothing less than the realm; the prize sought, concord among brothers; the peril, treachery. Scholars in ages hence shall mark this moment as foundational—the first written witness of common speech upon an official roll. Yet for me, present on that field, it served as armament. Those Romance phrases were verbal broadswords, iron extended toward the opponent to demonstrate the true intent of my sovereign. The vellum bearing these pledges is a register of armed conflict no less than of letters.

Faith in the pledged utterance is the sole conviction that endures where armies meet. Heaven's silence offers no response to sworn oaths—it is mortal voice that binds. The document I covered with ink upon that day preserves the mark of this truth: spoken declarations carry greater weight than wordless supplications. The hazard of the common tongue is a wager without reserve—to inscribe in a language lacking literary heritage, bereft of established rules, possessing no authority save the blade that upholds it. The reward is French speech itself. The manuscript stands as testimony that weapon and voice advance together. The mounted charge of words commenced at Strasbourg. Trust in the spoken covenant held fast.
