The Bearer of Cases traces upon the board a pattern of Carolingian recorders. Each voice stands as testament, each thread a common speech. The roll of those who set down records from that age is sparse — one hand's fingers would suffice to number them. Nithard, Thegan, the Astronomer: three scribes bound in a design the blade has inscribed. The reckoning of martial chronicles becomes a trial against time's erosion.

Language itself forms the cord joining these witnesses. My own speech differs from Thegan's — the emerging French of the Strasbourg pledges stands apart from the polished Latin of the palace. This philological study reveals ties that students of constrained writing might acknowledge: limits of register, canons of expression that mark each recorder's particular voice. The OuLiPo masters might claim every scribe follows an unwitting prohibition — certain terms are denied him by his mother tongue, his station, his sword's burden.

The Professor counts the engagements named in each chronicle. The tally from Fontenoy emerges as a haunting cipher — the tie binding all these vellum leaves. That cavalry clash on a June dawn in 841 stands as the crimson struggle around which all our annals of the Carolingians revolve. The vocabulary of slaughter knows no boundaries — the grammar of gore requires no interpreter. Steel articulates a tongue that the sum of fallen warriors renders into ciphers. The Professor's pattern grows thick with designations and linkages. This catalogue of historical records becomes an archivist's labor — the principle being to omit no testimony, no idiom, no stroke of the hilt.
