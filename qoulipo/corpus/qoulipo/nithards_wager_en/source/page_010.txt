The Bearer of Cases reckons the likelihood of what is holy. How many tongues must speak before God is named? The sum of languages wherein devotion has found voice forms a vast tapestry -- the Latin of the Church Fathers, the common speech of believers, the French of those who sought union with the Divine, the wordless contemplation of Port-Royal. Each tongue stands as testimony, each rendering a covenant. The tally of possible supplications surpasses what any vellum might contain.

The choice of language is equally a wager upon divine truth. When the Strasbourg Oaths shift from Latin into the emerging Romance tongue, political necessity alters speech -- and with it, the conviction that gives weight to the vow. The Professor grasps that the peril of rendering one tongue into another is the very peril of grace itself: one stakes all upon words whose desired reward is understanding, yet the hazard of utterance may betray what was intended. The scroll preserves the marks of this gamble -- words in the common tongue beside words in learned Latin, a bilingual record like a psalter in two voices.

The sum of manuscripts that have borne witness to the faith is knowable. The accounting proves straightforward: some hundreds of codices, some thousands of leaves, yet a conviction that has touched countless souls. The likelihood of transmission is a wager the scribes never named, though the Professor counts it in his travelling cases. The testimonies of this great web are the languages themselves; the threads, the translations into fresh speech. Belief passes from one vernacular to another as ink passes from one leaf to the next. Divine silence alone stands apart -- the cipher that defies reckoning, the word that refuses all utterance, the leaf that remains unmarked.
