I wager that the scribe who transcribed my Chronicles played a game. The constraint of transcription is that of the inverted lipogram—nothing is forbidden, everything is obligatory. Reproduce every term, every character, every shade of the original tongue on fresh sheepskin. The plot of transcription is a plot of fealty—the making of the same, the illusion of the identical. But the language trembles under the scribe's quill, and the Carolingian vernacular deforms imperceptibly.

The competitive sport of textual intrigue is a hazard of fortune. The likelihood that the scribe reproduces exactly my manuscript is a wager the vellum does not guarantee. Each error is a clinamen—a deviation of speech that creates a new text from the old volume. The making of these variants is the hoped-for gain of time. The sheepskin evolves from copy to copy, from plot to plot.

The Bearer of Cases would recognize in this contest the OuLiPo's rule: restraint begets variation, transcription begets invention. The vernacular of my annals has traversed the centuries transforming itself—Carolingian French became medieval, then classical, then modern. At each copy, the hide records a linguistic scheme—the language that cheats on itself, the term that slides toward another meaning. The chronicler's stake is to wager on this fertile betrayal. The peril is the loss of original sense. The gain is the living speech, the perpetual contest of the making of French on the vellum of the ages.
