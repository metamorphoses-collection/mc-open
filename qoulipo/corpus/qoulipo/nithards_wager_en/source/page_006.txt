I maintain that speech harbours a plot. The Strasbourg Vows, as I set them down in my annals, stand as the first witness to the French tongue—yet who shall vouch for their truth? My account is itself a kind of contest: I have chosen to record the common speech rather than casting all into the mould of Latin. This choice of tongue is a stake, a perilous venture. The hazard is that none may comprehend it; the prize I seek, the fidelity of witness.

The constraint of which the OuLiPians speak was already present in this Carolingian record. To write in the vulgar tongue during the ninth century was to impose a law as severe as any lipogram: one forsakes Latin as Perec renounced the letter e. This sport with linguistic forms conspires against custom. Literature of possibilities is born in such refusal—this fashioning of a French yet without grammar, without lexicon, holding no authority save that of the spoken word. Fortune decreed that the French language should make its first appearance in a tale of warfare. The venture was not merely one of statecraft—it was philological.

The intrigue of tongues unfolds on many planes. The scribes who copied the Vows in their cloisters may have corrupted the original vernacular. Deception remains ever possible—a counterfeit Oath would serve as a false baptismal record for French. My venture was to stake my testimony upon a document in two tongues, an account that courts the deviation inherent in rendering one speech into another. The likelihood that these words are precisely those uttered at Strasbourg is vanishingly small. Yet the law of this literary trial accepts such uncertainty. The prize sought is not precision—it is verisimilitude.
