The conspiracy I lay bare is that of tongue turned to ice. They fashioned a holy Latin, a cant of devotion that thrust aside common speech—and this making is as grave a deception as the forging of sacred relics. The sheepskin of Latin Gospel manuscripts stands as a linguistic remnant whose holiness does not declare itself by nature. The purpose of inventive writing is precisely to resist such rigidity—to inscribe devotion in every tongue, beneath every limitation.

Devotion belongs not to Latin alone. The masters of Port-Royal grasped this truth—we offer our prayers in French, we compose our Provincial Letters in the people's idiom, we reject the conspiracy of priestly discourse. The law of our endeavor is lucidity: the banishment of obscure terminology, the discipline of openness. Divine mercy requires no sanctified parchment—it marks itself upon the lowliest lambskin, in the plainest vernacular.

The creation of the holy through speech is the most cunning of stratagems. The scribes in their cloisters did not merely counterfeit their volumes—they counterfeited the very bond between devotion and discourse. By reserving Latin for the sacred manuscript, they created a linguistic treasure that shut out most of the faithful. The path of constrained writing offers a contrary conspiracy: rendering discourse available through discipline, throwing open the codex to all tongues. The wager of conviction expressed in common parlance is a swerving aside—that deflection which brings liberty. Supplication in French is a contest of words against heavenly silence. The speech of ordinary folk is the most hallowed sheepskin.
