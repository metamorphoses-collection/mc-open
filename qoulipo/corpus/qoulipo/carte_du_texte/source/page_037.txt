The fog comes first from the east, where the river bends around the lower town, creeping through the iron gate in tendrils that soften the geometry of headstones. By the time the light fails, the cemetery exists in gradations of grey—granite markers darker than mist but paler than the yew trees planted in the old section, their roots so deep now they have displaced the bones beneath. The damp rises from the ground itself, from clay and loam freshly turned, carrying with it the particular cold of disturbed earth, that mineral scent of stones long buried and now exposed to air again.

Bonaventura works in the far corner, his spade striking rhythmically against the Wealden clay. He has been gravedigger here for thirty years, since his father held the post before him, and he knows the soil's character in every season—hard as brick in summer drought, running like soup in the spring thaw, and now in autumn holding just enough moisture to part cleanly from the blade. He does not look up when the widow passes. There are always widows at dusk, women who time their visits to avoid the scrutiny of morning, who prefer to grieve in the failing light when their faces need not compose themselves for neighbours.

She kneels at a stone three rows from the northern wall, her black shawl already beaded with moisture from the mist. The grave is new enough that no moss has crept across the inscription, old enough that the mound has settled and someone has planted rosemary at its head. She does not weep, only rests one bare hand on the cold granite as if testing its temperature, as if surprised each time to find it no warmer than the air.

When full dark comes, Bonaventura will ring the small bell mounted by the gate—the signal that the cemetery closes, that the living must return to the living. But not yet. The fog thickens. The spade strikes stone. The widow does not move.
