Marechal kept a broken sword in his office — not a real medieval sword, but a stage prop from a university production of Le Cid, which a student had given him as a joke and which he had kept as a reminder: a shadow of the real swords he studied in chronicles, reduced to a harmless toy. The silk wrapping on the handle was fraying, and the blade — tinfoil over wood — caught the light like water whenever the door opened.

The task, he said, is to distinguish the real sword from the stage sword, the true wager from the bluff, the real silk from the synthetic. The shadow of scholarship is the shadow of the theatre: both present a version of reality that depends on the audience's willingness to believe. The sword on stage kills only if the audience agrees to the fiction; the sword in the chronicle kills only if the reader trusts the chronicler. The wager is the same: suspension of disbelief, the gamble that the reflection shows truth.

He played a puzzle with the broken sword: he would hold it up during lectures and ask his students to date it. The answers ranged from the eighth century to yesterday, and each answer revealed the student's assumptions — about metal, about manufacture, about the relationship between appearance and reality. The silk wrapping looked old; the blade looked new. The surface of the object reflected the student's ignorance, and the puzzle was to transform ignorance into knowledge through the Socratic method of the broken prop.

The wager of the scholar is the wager of the sword-maker: that the work will hold under pressure. The silk of the argument must not fray; the surface of the evidence must not crack; the puzzle of interpretation must not bore the audience. Marechal's sword was broken, but his argument was not. The image he held up to the monks of Saint-Riquier reflected their conspiracy with the clarity of polished steel, and the wager he placed — that the bones were fake, that the chronicle was fabricated — has held for decades, unbroken, like a blade that was forged, not glued.
