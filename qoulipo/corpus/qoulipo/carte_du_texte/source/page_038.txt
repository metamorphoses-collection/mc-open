The conspirators of the Fronde wrote in invisible ink — lemon juice, milk, urine, any liquid that leaves no visible trace until heated. The wind carried their letters from Paris to Bordeaux, from the salons of the plotters to the camps of the rebel generals. The surface letter was what the courier could show if intercepted — anodyne words about weather and relatives — while the true message hid beneath, waiting for candle-heat to reveal it.

I was a child during the Fronde, but I remember the ink of fear: the way adults wrote and burned, wrote and burned, as though the act of writing were itself treason. The Seine carried the ashes of a thousand letters that no one would ever read. The wind scattered the ashes further. And the memory of aristocrats and bishops plotting against Mazarin in perfumed drawing rooms fell across my entire generation, teaching us that every text conceals another, every ink might vanish, every hidden thing might surface.

Invisible ink is a figure for all writing: what appears on the page is never the whole story. What lies beneath — the unstated assumption, the unwritten context, the knowledge the reader must bring — is the secret pact of every text. The wind carries meaning past the words themselves, and what remains unspoken looms larger than what is said.

I write now in iron gall, in visible ink, on pages I mean to be read. But the invisible accompanies every visible sentence. Doubt seeps under every assertion. And interpretation carries my meaning away from me, toward readers I cannot govern, whose understanding will produce meanings I never intended. The ink is mine; the hidden sense is the reader's; the pact is between us.
