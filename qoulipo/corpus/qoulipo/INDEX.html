QOuLiPo Corpus

Metamorphoses of Civilization · 2026

This page is the QOuLiPo Corpus, a strand of the Metamorphoses Collection: the constrained-writing companion to the natural-text corpus the collection has been building around its anchor edition (Pier Francesco Giambullari's 1544 reconstruction of Dante's Inferno). The natural-text companions — Augustine, Boethius, Dante, Galileo, Giambullari, Marguerite de Navarre, Lactantius, Ausonius, Mishnah Avot — are computed with the same graph/MIS pipeline; their full data, alongside the constrained corpus shown here, is in the Zenodo deposit.

The corpus comprises 28 literary works engineered as quantum-benchmark instances on neutral-atom hardware, each both a readable book and a graph whose structural properties (density, rigidity, planarity, bipartiteness) are controlled by design. Each card below shows the canonical compute-instance graph and links to the text's source files, metadata, and graph data.

Six cards carry a ★ flagship badge. The badge marks editorial attention — texts that received more development effort and revision passes — not a finished-literary-quality verdict. The prose in this corpus is generated under graph-topological constraints with substantial human revision; the flagship pieces are first attempts pursued further than the benchmark-only entries, and the author looks forward to contributions from writers, poets, and OuLiPian collaborators that turn these graph-bound exercises into more developed works.

★ flagship
🇮🇹 N=25
A Calvino-style Italian novella in which 25 rooms of a real-and-unreal palazzo sit on the 25 vertices of the 5×5 king graph — an exact 2D unit-disk graph by construction. Every room touches its 8 neighbours; the reader navigating the palace performs a graph walk that the quantum processor can solve in parallel.

Constraint & theme notes
You enter an Italian palazzo that exists and does not exist simultaneously, confronting a 5x5 grid of twenty-five rooms, each a story-cell housing a woman, a manuscript, a number. A woman named Lidia (or her ghost) inhabits the eastern room with a crib and a sealed letter; the northern room contains only numbers and mirrors. You navigate the palace reading fragments of interconnected stories that share characters, objects, and obsessions; the grid is both a literal map and a metaphorical labyrinth where every room touches four others and narrative branches splinter with each crossing. The constraint is architectural: the stories must fit the palace, and the palace must fit the page.
